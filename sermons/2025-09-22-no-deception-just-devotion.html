Overview

  1. 00:20 From Satan’s deception to the danger of deceiving ourselves

    Scripture: James 1:18 John 19:30

    • Josh identifies this as week three in James and locates the message in the final section of chapter 1. Weeks one and two dealt with trials and temptations and with Satan’s attempts to twist God’s Word. James now pivots from external deception by Satan to internal deception by oneself and asks how believers respond to God’s sanctifying work through his Word. Josh warns that it is more serious when Satan does not need to deceive someone because that person will deceive himself.
    • He restores the immediate context by rereading James 1:18. God gives new birth through “the word of truth.” Those who hear and believe the message of Jesus trust the sufficiency of his sacrifice and saving power; Josh invokes Jesus’ “It is finished” to describe the completed victory and release from the law’s condemning weight under Christ’s blood.
    • He develops James’s seed image. The Word has been planted or engrafted in the believer, and the Holy Spirit lives and works within. Scripture is therefore not merely a self-help book but food, air, water, life, nourishment, and an “umbilical connection” to God. The Word grows within believers, changes them, and produces increasing freedom, peace, and confidence.
    • New birth does not end every problem; it begins God’s redemptive work in a person and ultimately in creation. The confusion often lies not in how salvation begins but in what its continuing work—sanctification, God’s pursuit of holiness in the believer—looks like.

  2. 03:45 Self-deception about salvation and maturity is exposed by God’s truth

    Scripture: Matthew 7:22–23 Revelation 3:17 Romans 7:24–25 John 17:17

    • Josh names the most frightening form of self-deception: people may think they are saved when they are not. He recounts reading Matthew 7:22–23 to two Mormon missionaries at his dinner table and challenging their confidence in a message he regards as adding a false prophet to Scripture. He immediately broadens the warning beyond Mormonism: people currently sitting in church may also wrongly assume they are saved. His pastoral prayer is that the Holy Spirit would expose that deception so they can run to Jesus.
    • Self-deception also afflicts genuine believers. Some saved people live an unserious, performative Christian life, mistaking self-created spirituality or gifts for the work of God. A mature Christian can admit, “I’m a sinner saved by grace,” and walk with Jesus while being honestly exposed as a sinner; there is no promotion beyond grace. The self-deceived Christian cannot afford honesty because maintaining a spiritual appearance has become the priority.
    • Biblical portraits contrast false sufficiency with honest dependence. Jesus’ warning to Laodicea exposes people who claim wealth and needlessness while remaining wretched, poor, blind, and naked without him. Paul’s struggle in Romans 7 models mature honesty: he names his wretchedness and then praises God for rescue through Jesus Christ.
    • Josh states the controlling solution: the only protection from self-deception is a right relationship with God through his Word. Jesus’ prayer in John 17 identifies God’s Word as the truth by which his people are sanctified. Properly received, the Word drives out dishonesty, performance, and deception.
    • He previews the sermon’s three responsibilities: receive the Word, obey the Word, and share the Word. These enable honest fellowship with God, unity with other believers, and faithful witness in a dark world.

  3. 08:37 Receive the planted Word: good soil produces faith-fruit

    Scripture: James 1:19–21 Matthew 13:3–23 Galatians 5:22–23 Colossians 1:10 Hebrews 13:15 John 15:4–5 Matthew 13:13–15; Isaiah 6:9–10 John 5:39–40 Hebrews 5:11 James 1:19

    • Josh reads James 1:19–21, which calls believers to be quick to listen, slow to speak, slow to anger, rid themselves of moral filth and prevalent evil, and humbly receive the planted Word that can save them.
    • He connects James’s planted Word to Jesus’ parable of the soils. The hard heart cannot understand or receive the seed and bears no fruit. The shallow heart responds emotionally but has no depth and bears no fruit. The crowded heart never truly repents, so sin chokes the Word and no fruit remains. The fruitful heart receives the Word, lets it take root, puts off old ways through repentance, and yields a harvest.
    • He defines fruit as evidence of a divinely altered life: Christian character, consistent conduct, and selfless gospel ministry for God’s glory. Fruit includes attempting to lead someone to Christ, repeatedly fighting addiction, and refusing evil temptation. It includes sharing resources with believers and the Spirit’s fruit of love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, and self-control. Colossians shows fruit in every good work and in growing in the knowledge of God. Hebrews identifies praise and public profession of Christ’s name as fruit offered to God.
    • Genuine salvation produces fruit in many forms, but churches also contain fake, performative, man-made fruit. Fake fruit becomes recognizable once someone has tasted the real work of God. The decisive difference is the source: Jesus says branches bear fruit only by remaining in the vine; apart from him they can do nothing.
    • Jesus’ rebuke of religious leaders shows that exposure to Scripture is not enough. Matthew 13 describes people who see without seeing and hear without understanding because their hearts have become calloused. Josh also paraphrases Jesus’ challenge that people search Scripture for life while refusing to come to him, the one in whom life is found.
    • If growth and fresh fruit are absent, the seed and vine are not defective; the problem is the soil or branch. Citing Hebrews 5:11 in the ESV, Josh says teaching may be hard to explain because hearers have become “dull of hearing,” not because the truth requires elite academic credentials. His transition is that James wants the Word received in a way that actually produces a harvest in the soul.

  4. 15:20 Receive rightly: listen first, restrain speech, surrender anger, and cultivate humility

    Scripture: James 1:19 Proverbs 10:19 James 1:19 James 1:19–20 Proverbs 12:1 Romans 1:26–27 Luke 9:23 2 Samuel 12:1–7 John 18:10–11; Luke 22:50–51 Psalm 97:10 James 1:20 Luke 22:42 1 John 1:9 Ephesians 2:1, 4–5

    • Be quick to listen (15:20–17:22). Josh repeats the series progression—trials, temptation, then God’s Word—and says flourishing believers become eager to hear Scripture or at least prioritize it when eagerness is absent. He compares this urgency to a first-time mother immediately responding to her newborn’s faintest cry, joking that the response may change with a second child. Application: when an issue arises, rush to Scripture first, develop a godly mindset, and only then move forward. Christians too often begin with “I think.” The decisive question is whether they have listened to God; Scripture should be the first line of defense, not a last-quarter “Hail Mary.”
    • Be slow to speak (17:25–20:36). Josh jokes that two ears and one mouth should convey the point, then quotes Proverbs 10:19 about multiplied words and the prudent person restraining the tongue. He explains that first-century church gatherings could include real-time debate with the speaker, even contentious exchanges. In the present congregation, order matters: he prepares and prays over the biblical “meal” all week, asks hearers to receive it, and invites later conversation in the lobby rather than live interruption. He defines the “Christian dummy” not by intelligence or education but as someone who speaks without seeking God’s counsel and lets feelings lead. Feelings unchecked by Scripture endanger both the person and the surrounding community. He corrects a common misuse of “slow to speak”: James is not forbidding clear, loving intervention when God’s Word plainly addresses a brother walking toward disaster. The call is for speech rooted in Scripture and directed toward another’s good.
    • Be slow to anger (20:39–25:14). In context, Josh says the command means not becoming angry with God or his Word when Scripture rebukes and corrects. Proverbs 12:1 labels hatred of correction as stupidity. God’s purpose is not merely to affirm desires but to call people to repentance; Josh connects this to a saying he appreciated from the late Charlie Kirk. He applies this to cultural claims about sexual identity and same-sex desire, asserting that Scripture calls the lifestyle unnatural and calls every person to deny sinful desire. He broadens the point by admitting his own unwanted desires and emphasizing the Spirit’s strength for repetitive discipline and self-denial. Nathan’s stolen-lamb story illustrates misdirected anger: David demanded justice without realizing that justice was aimed at him. Peter’s sword in Gethsemane illustrates acting before listening: he tried to strike a man, cut off his ear, and forced Jesus to heal the injury before addressing the soldiers. The application is to slow down, hear the Word, stop reacting, seek wisdom, and then apply God’s counsel. Josh distinguishes sinful human anger from godly hatred of evil, quoting Psalm 97:10. Pursuit of justice need not be lethargic, but it must be governed by godly wisdom.
    • Human tantrums cannot produce God’s righteousness (25:04–27:49). A child’s checkout-line tantrum cannot force a wise parent’s hand, and anger cannot force God to retract what his Word reveals. He briefly announces a planned space for new parents and infants, expected to open in October, then returns to the parenting analogy: once a parent says no, surrendering to the tantrum teaches the child that escalation works. When God exposes sin, the response should be gratitude and removal, not anger. Anger at correction is pride claiming to know better than God; humility can honestly dislike the process while still submitting. Jesus in Gethsemane models the posture: “not my will” but the Father’s. Josh identifies surrender to God’s will as the location of peace and maturity.
    • Practical preparation of the heart (27:53–29:24). First, confess sins specifically rather than generally. Josh quotes 1 John 1:9 and tells hearers to work through the actual list while meditating on God’s love, grace, forgiveness, and faithfulness. Ask God to plow, tenderize, and soften the heart so the seed of truth can flourish; he calls this a dangerous but necessary prayer. Stay humble: stop fitting God into one’s personal story and instead discover how one fits into God’s story. Do not drag the skeleton of the old, dead life into the new life Christ has given; leave it behind.

  5. 29:26 Obey the Word: let God’s mirror lead to concrete change

    Scripture: James 1:22–25 Romans 10:17 James 1:23–24 Isaiah 6:5 Luke 5:8 Job 42:6 James 1:25

    • Josh reads James 1:22–25 and moves from receiving to obeying. Hearing without doing is self-deception. The forgetful hearer resembles someone who sees his face in a mirror, leaves, and immediately forgets what he saw. The blessed person looks intently into the perfect law of freedom, continues in it, remembers it, and acts.
    • He contrasts salvation and subsequent blessing. Salvation comes through hearing the message of Christ. Once saved, blessing flows from doing rather than hearing alone. His memorable contrast is that many people write in their Bibles but never let the Bible “write on” them. Church attendance and sermon listening do not by themselves make anyone spiritual or Christian: spectators listen and watch; believers listen and do.
    • The mirror tells the truth (31:55–34:16). A mirror permits an honest look at oneself when other people may withhold uncomfortable truth. Josh tells of spending an hour with family before discovering in a mirror that he had a large whitehead; unconditional love was welcome, but a warning would also have helped. People do not blame a mirror for reporting an ugly truth. Likewise, divine revelation shows who a person really is rather than the flattering self-image that person prefers.
    • Mistake one: glancing instead of examining (34:19–36:01). A rushed devotional can become mere religious habit: Scripture is consumed quickly but not allowed to change the reader. Rather than always racing ahead to the next sermon text or “spiritual fix,” Josh urges the church to sit all week with what has already been preached and act on it. The diagnostic question is when the hearer last entered Scripture asking God to reveal how God sees him. Reject the “magical mirror” that shows only what one wants; ask God to expose evil spots, dangerous tendencies, blemishes, and laziness, then work with him to clean them.
    • Mistake two: seeing and then forgetting (36:04–37:45). Conviction demands response. Josh names social media as a present blemish in his own life: celebrity and megachurch conflict, hostile strangers, and unsolicited advice about shepherding have become spiritually corrosive. He says he deleted the apps that morning and is shutting social media and news off for thirty days, perhaps permanently, inviting others to join the experiment. Doing nothing after the Lord redirects him would reproduce James’s forgetful-hearer error. Isaiah, Peter, and Job responded to revelation with confessions of ruin, sinfulness, and self-disgust; Josh contrasts this with Christians who admire themselves in the mirror.
    • He rereads James 1:25 and makes the governing point explicit: the blessing comes in the doing, not merely the reading.

  6. 38:09 Continue in the perfect law with an unveiled face

    Scripture: 2 Corinthians 13:5 James 1:25 John 8:31–32 Romans 8:37 2 Corinthians 3:9 Exodus 34:29–35 Matthew 27:50–51; Mark 15:37–38; Luke 23:45–46 2 Corinthians 3:18 James 1:25; 2 Corinthians 3:18 Psalm 139:23–24

    • God’s mirror reaches beneath behavior to motives, desires, heart, and soul; it is a deep and potentially frightening examination. This is not a one-time inspection. Believers must look again and again and be ready to fight recurring blemishes daily. Addressing men especially, Josh asks what might change in homes, churches, and the community if men inspected their souls through Scripture as carefully as their wives inspect a physical mirror. Instead of expertly identifying everyone else’s problems, turn the examination inward and invite the Lord into that space.
    • He urges hearers to dust off Scripture and begin by testing whether their faith is genuine. He mentions 1 Corinthians, Romans, and Ephesians as direct places to begin, while his phrase “see if you’re in the faith” echoes 2 Corinthians 13:5. Anything built on counterfeit faith will collapse.
    • James calls Scripture the “perfect law that gives freedom” because obedience is God’s means of liberation. Josh quotes John 8:31–32: true disciples hold to Jesus’ teaching, know the truth, and are set free—especially from sin. He returns to the Mormon missionaries and asks why a perfect Word would need Joseph Smith’s addition, insisting that one cannot carry the words of a liar alongside the words of the Savior. He says Scripture is without error and wholly true, and expresses grief over the young missionaries and the men who led them astray.
    • Confession without transformation stops short of experienced freedom. Josh invokes Paul’s “more than conquerors,” then reads 2 Corinthians 3:9 to contrast the glorious old-covenant ministry that brought condemnation with the more glorious new-covenant ministry that brings righteousness. The old covenant condemned and killed; the new covenant in Christ brings forgiveness and life.
    • Moses’ veil becomes the bridge between James’s mirror and Paul’s transformation imagery. Josh retells Exodus 34: Moses descended with a shining face, veiled the fading glory from Israel, and removed the veil when returning to God. At Jesus’ death the temple veil was torn, removing the barrier between humanity and God. Unlike fading reflected glory, the new-covenant believer beholds the Lord with an unveiled face and is transformed into his image with ever-increasing glory, as 2 Corinthians 3:18 states.
    • Scripture helps believers contemplate Christ and be transformed over time. The pace differs from person to person, but the church is coming along together. Combining Paul and James, Josh calls believers to look intently into the law of liberty with unveiled faces: stop the Christian cover-up, remove the mask, and admit that everyone is broken. He leads into David’s Psalm 139 prayer asking God to search, test, expose offensive ways, and lead in the everlasting way.
    • Self-examination is like resuming exercise after a long absence: it may hurt at first, but repetition brings strength. His final exhortation is to get in the Word, stay there, keep returning, and let it nourish, restore, and lead.

  7. 46:05 Share the Word first through disciplined speech

    Scripture: James 1:26–27

    • Josh introduces the final responsibility—share the Word—and reads James 1:26–27. Religion without a restrained tongue is self-deception and worthless. Religion God accepts as pure and faultless cares for distressed orphans and widows and remains unpolluted by the world.
    • He defines James’s “religion” as outward practice rather than ceremonies, temples, special days, or traditions. Pure religion means practicing God’s Word by sharing it, serving people, and pursuing personal holiness.
    • The first outward test is the tongue. A person may perform every expected spiritual activity, but uncontrolled speech makes the claimed religion worthless. Transformation becomes audible when former patterns of cursing give way to the Spirit’s self-control and when believers stop tearing others down to elevate themselves. Josh postpones fuller treatment until James 3, joking that the future sermon will be fun in a terribly convicting way. The mouth is the first place where good soil becomes visible because speech reveals the heart. Not every thought needs to be spoken.

  8. 48:03 Share the Word through costly compassion and holy separation

    Scripture: 1 John 3:16–18 James 1:27 John 17:14–18

    • Proper self-examination redirects attention from personal need to the needs of others. Josh reads 1 John 3:16–18: Christ’s self-giving defines love, believers should lay down their lives for one another, and material resources must become concrete help rather than words alone.
    • He presents Crossroads Cares as the congregation’s practical expression of that command. He asks those who have given or received through the ministry to acknowledge God’s blessing. As the church grows, people may decide that Crossroads is their church, but Josh says identification without joining the work of meeting needs is incomplete. Service is not a side program but the congregation’s core identity: they would rather wash feet with a basin and towel than pursue brand visibility, television, or online prominence. He calls for servants, not consumers seeking flash.
    • A church without compassion—or one that merely outsources compassion—is not pure and undefiled before God. Local churches must do mission work in their own cities, even while also supporting global work financially. If a church vanished and its city would not notice, it should step aside and let active saints serve.
    • Josh returns to the final clause of James 1:27: ministry to the world must be joined to freedom from the world’s pollution. Christians are physically in the world without belonging to it spiritually, seeking to win people to Christ while maintaining healthy separation. A church cannot model radical generosity after becoming selfish like the world, heal others while sharing their sickness, guide the blind while blind itself, or build Christ’s name while consumed with its own. Jesus is the model: perfectly holy while present among sinners. Josh asks God to teach the church to do the same by the Spirit’s power.

  9. 51:31 Final warning: leave devoted rather than deceived

    Scripture: James 1:25

    • Josh returns to the central danger: the most frightening deception is not Satan’s lie but the lie a person tells himself.
    • Religious consumption can coexist with unchanged living. A person can hear sermons, read Scripture, consume podcasts and Bible teachers, join studies, nod at truth, and say “Amen” at the proper moments without ever letting the truth shape life. That gap between hearing and doing is self-deception.
    • He asks whether hearers have allowed God’s Word to set their agenda or are still calling the shots.
    • Repeating James 1:25, he says the person who looks into the perfect law of liberty and does it is the one who is blessed.
    • The final question is not merely whether God’s Word has been heard but whether it will be obeyed.
    • His invitation supplies the sermon title’s contrast: do not leave deceived; leave devoted—devoted to God’s sanctifying process and letting his Word govern one’s words, works, and worship.

  10. 52:58 Closing prayer: the mirror drives sinners back to the Savior

    Scripture: Psalm 139:23–24

    • Josh thanks God for working through the Word and for giving the church an honest mirror.
    • The mess exposed by the mirror reminds him why Jesus came: no amount of moral work removes the need for a Savior.
    • He returns to the gospel: Jesus came down, bore sin on the cross, and was forsaken so sinners could be forgiven. He asks that anyone not resting confidently in Christ’s power to save would run to him inwardly, admit need, recognize sin, and come to the Savior.
    • For those already redeemed, he asks that they leave a subpar spectator life and become doers.
    • The prayer ends by asking God to reveal what is spiritually “nasty” and lead the church in the everlasting way, echoing Psalm 139:23–24.
