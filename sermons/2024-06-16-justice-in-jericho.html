Overview

  1. 00:03 Why Joshua matters

    Scripture: Proverbs 3:3–6

    • On Father’s Day, Elijah says he jumped at the chance to teach when Pastor Josh said the teaching team would go through Joshua. The book is a historical testament to the earliest days of our faith and has practical application for every believer today.
    • Joshua shows what a life devoted to the Lord looks like and the blessing of submitting to his will instead of selfish ambition. Service to the Lord isn’t without challenges, but when we realize the battle belongs to the Lord, we can be confident it is already won.
    • It illustrates Proverbs 3 in practice: let love and faithfulness never leave you; trust in the Lord with all your heart and lean not on your own understanding; in all your ways submit to him, and he will make your paths straight. Through faith and obedience Israel witnessed that with God all things are possible.

  2. 01:39 The story so far

    Scripture: Joshua 6 Joshua 1 Joshua 2 Joshua 3 Exodus 14

    • In chapter 1 Joshua takes up Moses’ heavy mantle with a commission from God and the people’s acceptance. In chapter 2 God uses Rahab, a prostitute, not merely to protect the spies or show he can use anyone, but because her name in Jesus’ bloodline shows it was always the plan to preserve the Messiah’s line through her. “Hey, legalists.” Praise God he uses wicked, rebellious people like us.
    • In chapter 3 God “pulls an Exodus 14” and stops the flooded Jordan so Israel crosses on dry ground, far from the last miracle in the book. All of it shows a sovereign God in full control who is also good and faithful, and as he made a way for Israel out of Egypt and into the land, he has made a way for us out of sin through Jesus’ atoning death and resurrection. Salvation and deliverance are written all over this book.

  3. 03:24 More than Sunday school storytime

    • Chapter 6 tells of the destruction of Jericho. Everyone knows the story, but Elijah asks the church not to tune out. He wants to bring clarity to the historical context and to answer objections to the Christian faith that come out of this chapter and Joshua in general, so they walk away with newfound confidence in the truth stories like this present.

  4. 04:14 Chapter 5: consecration and the commander

    Scripture: Joshua 5

    • First, chapter 5. The night before the campaign, Israel camps at Gilgal and consecrates itself to the Lord through circumcision, the sign of the covenant, and through Passover, remembering God’s deliverance from Egypt. That is homework for the week. The chapter shows our ambitions are only good if they align with God’s will; Israel’s consecration should fuel our own devotion and self-reflection.
    • Near Jericho, Joshua meets a man with a drawn sword and asks whose side he is on. The man answers neither; he is commander of the army of the Lord. Joshua falls down in worship and submits to him. This is the second person of the Trinity before he becomes incarnate in Jesus, an Old Testament appearance of God in the flesh, which theologians call a Christophany. Joshua is visited and given battle strategy by God himself.

    1. 06:15 Opening prayer

      • Elijah prays for ears to hear and eyes to see God, and a heart to accept all of who he is: love, grace, and mercy, but also holy, righteous, and just. As they wrestle with the difficult parts, he asks that they lay their doubts, fears, and anxieties at God’s feet, because the battle belongs to him.

  5. 06:51 Joshua 6:1–5: the gates are barred

    Scripture: Joshua 6:1–5

    • Elijah reads verses 1–5: Jericho is securely barred because of the Israelites; the Lord says he has delivered the city, its king, and its fighting men into Joshua’s hands, and gives the plan to march around it for six days, then seven times on the seventh with trumpets, a long blast, a loud shout, and the wall will collapse. An interesting battle strategy, to say the least.

    1. 07:42 God’s reputation went before them

      Scripture: Joshua 2:9

      • The gates were barred because God’s reputation and his people’s preceded them. Rahab showed in chapter 2 that Canaan already knew; with about 600,000 fighting men, Israel couldn’t move secretly. In chapter 2, verse 9, Rahab says everyone is melting in fear. Elijah loves that term: the enemies of God can’t even fight back; they simply melt away. That should encourage us when we see wickedness today. Jericho is about to learn its war is not with Israel but with the God of creation they have mocked and rebelled against.

    2. 09:03 Jericho’s double walls

      Scripture: Joshua 6:1

      • Because of that fear, Jericho added defenses to an already well-fortified city. Though small, it was the best-defended city in Canaan. Digs in the 1930s and ’40s and as recently as 1997 found an embankment and a double wall: a lower retaining wall 10 to 15 feet high topped by a mud-brick wall another 20 to 26 feet high and about 6 feet thick, then an embankment and a second wall of similar size sitting about 46 feet above ground, protecting the main living area of about six acres.
      • Homes in the embankment between the walls likely housed the poorer citizens: if you’re poor, you only get one wall. The whole city sat on about ten acres, nothing for a town when Anthem is about 5,900 acres. At about 200 people per acre in small stacked one-room homes, owning three acres would mean 600 people in your backyard. Roughly 2,000 people were now tightly fortified, and verse 1 says no one went out and no one came in.

  6. 12:00 “I have delivered Jericho into your hands”

    Scripture: Joshua 6:2 John 16:33

    • Elijah gives this picture so the church understands the impossibility of the task, which makes what God says next so important: “See, I have delivered Jericho into your hands.” Joshua, no rookie at warfare, knew how hard this would be, so the Lord encourages him that the city is already conquered.
    • That should give us immense confidence, because the God who went before Israel goes before us in every season. The song Good Plans says, “I will take heart in deserts and gardens,” which reminds Elijah of John 16: in this world you will have trouble, but take heart, I have overcome the world. Believers aren’t called to run from trouble but to face it head-on, knowing the one who overcomes the world lives in us. Too many Christians fear the future instead of embracing the battles ahead, confident God has a purpose and will receive all the glory.

  7. 13:47 A strange plan that requires faith and humility

    Scripture: Joshua 6:3–5

    • Elijah reads the marching orders again from verse 3. It is an unorthodox strategy, and he imagines some confusion when Joshua took it back to the people. Charles Spurgeon thought so too: “Why?” the fool might have asked, “You are doing nothing. You are not loosening a single stone,” and by the fifth or sixth day many asked, “What is the good of it all?” Elijah agrees with his old dead British friend.
    • The plan requires Israel to step out in faith that God will do what he said, and to humble themselves by submitting to a method beyond their understanding. Israel’s more passive role also shows the Canaanites that Israel’s God is the one true God: they can plot, build walls, and call on their false gods, but when God tells the walls to fall, they fall.

    1. 16:00 Man’s plans cannot thwart God’s will

      Scripture: Psalm 2:4–5

      • Many Christians live as though people can thwart God’s will. They will try, but every attempt is in vain, and those in power who seek to dethrone God and deify themselves heap up judgment on themselves. In Psalm 2 David says he who sits in the heavens shall laugh and hold them in derision.
      • God is a God of justice. The wicked may seem to prosper, but justice awaits them on this side of eternity or the other. God will bring justice to every act of evil, so take heart: darkness will never win in the end.

  8. 16:49 The ark leads the march

    Scripture: Joshua 6:6–7

    • Picking up at verse 6, Joshua tells the priests to take up the ark with seven trumpets before it and orders the army to march with an armed guard ahead. Bringing the ark into battle wasn’t customary, but this isn’t much of a battle; it is a showcase of God’s authority over Canaan’s best-defended city. If God can take Jericho, the rest of Canaan is light work. The ark is mentioned eleven times in this short section.
    • The ark forces Israel to set their minds and hearts on the Lord instead of their military ability, and it tells Jericho that judgment from the one true God is coming and his people aren’t afraid.
    • When you face difficulty, do you keep your heart set on the Lord, or backpedal and lean on your own understanding? We will all face suffering, some more than others, but what the world, the flesh, and the enemy mean for evil, God means for your good and his glory.

  9. 18:33 Six days of marching

    Scripture: Joshua 6:8–14

    • Elijah reads the long section without skipping: the priests and trumpets go forward with the ark, the armed guard ahead and rear guard behind; Joshua commands the army not to give a war cry or say a word until the day he tells them to shout. They circle once, return to camp, and do this for six days.

    1. 19:37 Immediate obedience showcases faith

      Scripture: Romans 3:23 Ephesians 4:22–24

      • Commentators notice, and Elijah loves, Joshua’s initiative to immediately do what the Lord commanded, without hesitating. Have you struggled with delayed obedience? Elijah has, many times: God wanted him to give something up, get stronger in a weak area, or do something far outside his comfort zone, and in stubbornness and selfishness he kicked the can down the road until the burden became unbearable or blew up in his face. Looking back, acting right away would have spared so much stress and heartache.
      • Immediate obedience is a showcase of your faith. It shows you believe God’s will is best for you and that he will do what he says. That isn’t an endorsement to rush into big decisions without prayer, godly counsel, and discernment; “Eli did not tell you to do that.” But Scripture makes clear followers of Christ are set apart by love for one another, humble service, boldness to share the truth, and putting others first. None of these save you, but they speak to your consistency with what you profess and your witness to the world.
      • We all fall short of the glory of God, but if you are delaying obedience because you are comfortable, do some self-evaluation. Remember what Christ did for you while you were still a sinner and ask whether it is really his will for you to stay complacent. Paul calls us out of lukewarmness in Ephesians 4: put off your old self, be made new in the attitude of your minds, and put on the new self, created to be like God in true righteousness and holiness.
      • Elijah prays the church would be excited to obey God’s commands, love his word, and stand boldly on its truth; not like so much of the American church with empty words of meaningless praise, but with genuine worship that bears witness to the amazing grace of our Lord.

  10. 23:02 The seventh day: the wall collapses

    Scripture: Joshua 6:15–21

    • Back to the march at verse 15. On the seventh day they circle seven times, and at the trumpet blast Joshua commands, “Shout! For the Lord has given you the city.” The city is devoted to the Lord; only Rahab and those in her house are spared; keep away from the devoted things or bring trouble on the camp; the silver, gold, bronze, and iron go into the Lord’s treasury. The army shouts, the wall collapses, they charge straight in, and they destroy every living thing in the city.

  11. 24:15 Why would a good God command this?

    Scripture: Matthew 5:44

    • This is where everyone starts to squirm: “Eli, are we really going to talk about this today?” Yes. Skeptics and atheists use verses like this as an indictment of God’s goodness: why would a good God send his people to take another nation’s land and let them use violence? If Jesus says love your enemies, why does God declare war in the Old Testament?
    • Elijah uses the rest of the time on this because many Christians struggle with it, and anyone who does evangelism will hear it. It is multifaceted, but he wants to help people give an answer and see the gospel even in a story like this. As a bit of a theological nerd, he is going to nerd out.

    1. 25:39 The atheist’s foundational problem

      • The most foundational problem with the objection, especially from an atheist: if there is no God and all of this is a cosmic accident, sound and fury signifying nothing, and we are products of random evolution, then like Darwin said, might makes right, and you have no right to complain. Monkey see, monkey do.

    2. 26:12 Forts, limits, and rules of war

      Scripture: Deuteronomy 2 Deuteronomy 9 Deuteronomy 2:9 Deuteronomy 7:22 Deuteronomy 20 Joshua 11:9

      • First, the cities Israel destroys, Jericho, Ai, and Hazor, are military strongholds, not civilian population centers. Joshua Ryan Butler, a local pastor, writes that we should picture a military fort being taken, not a civilian massacre: God is pulling down the Great Wall of China, not demolishing Beijing; taking out the Pentagon, not New York City.
      • Second, God put explicit limits on the conquest, which is why those boring first five books matter. In Deuteronomy 2:9 God tells Moses not to harass the Moabites, because he saved that land for Lot’s descendants, keeping an earlier promise. Deuteronomy 7:22 puts a time boundary on it: God would drive the people out slowly, not lay waste to everything at once.
      • Third, Deuteronomy 20 lays out explicit rules for war, where Augustine got just war theory and the West derives its Geneva Conventions. “All is fair in love and war” isn’t biblical. We are not pacifists, but there is a moral way to decide when to go to war and how to conduct it. These rules included offers of peace, which were consistently rejected according to Joshua 11.

    3. 28:20 Driving out a wicked culture, not genocide

      • Finally, Leviticus, Deuteronomy, and what we know of Canaanite culture show the conquest isn’t about eliminating a people group; it isn’t genocide but driving out a depraved and wicked culture. John Walton compares it to the Allies driving out the Nazi regime without killing every German. The consistent language of driving out lends itself to exile and dispossession, language God uses with Israel too.
      • There is also a language issue with battle idioms in ancient Near Eastern narratives. If the Chiefs beat the Cardinals 45 to 3, a likely outcome from this Cardinals fan, we would say they got annihilated or destroyed, but we don’t mean every Cardinals player is dead. In 1 and 2 Samuel there are still Canaanites living in cities supposedly totally destroyed, so each battle must be taken case by case. Anyone who wants more can email Elijah for coffee.

  12. 30:23 God judging human evil

    Scripture: Joshua 5:13–15

    • All the nerdy stuff aside, Elijah’s biggest problem with the objection is that framing this as Israel versus Canaan misses the bigger story. It is about God judging human evil.
    • He rewinds to chapter 5: Joshua asks the man with the drawn sword, “Are you for us or for our enemies?” “Neither,” he replies, “but as commander of the army of the Lord I have now come.” Joshua falls facedown, asks what message his Lord has, and is told to take off his sandals, for the place is holy.

    1. 31:31 Is Israel on God’s side?

      • This has nothing to do with Israel and Canaan and everything to do with God judging human evil. Earlier Scripture shows the Canaanites sacrificed their children to false gods, burning them alive; you know it’s evil when there is state-sanctioned child murder, and if that makes you squirm, it should. They celebrated rape culture, were incestuous, and were needlessly violent. Imagine the wickedness of Hamas or ISIS on a national scale.
      • The question isn’t whether God is on Israel’s side but whether Israel is on God’s side. God would have judged the Canaanites with or without Israel, but he invites us into his plan to reveal the fullness of who he is, and here he wields Israel as a tool of judgment against a wicked, unrepentant, idolatrous people.
      • This is hard, but we don’t need to be ashamed of who God is. We can take joy that we serve a God who cares about real justice, not justice based on man-made ideology or the color of your skin, but divine justice from the Creator who knows what is best for human flourishing. If we tell people about God’s grace and love, we must acknowledge his justice and wrath toward evil, or there is nothing to forgive. Stories like this show God is faithful to his promises and really cares about making things right.

  13. 33:49 While we were still sinners

    Scripture: Romans 5:6–8 Ephesians 2:4–5

    • How much does God care about making things right? Romans 5: at just the right time, when we were still powerless, Christ died for the ungodly; God demonstrates his own love for us in this, while we were still sinners, Christ died for us.
    • Apart from Christ’s work, Elijah is under the same wrath the Canaanites felt; apart from his grace he is no better than them; apart from the Spirit he is a wicked man. Every day he wakes up in need of mercy, and every day God is faithful to give it. It reminds him of an old song: till on that cross as Jesus died, the wrath of God was satisfied. Because of his great love, God, rich in mercy, made us alive with Christ even when we were dead in transgressions; it is by grace you have been saved.
    • If you take nothing else away, see the goodness of the gospel even in a story of war and conquest like Jericho, because only with a vivid picture of God’s wrath and justice can we fully appreciate the undeserved grace and mercy he has lavished on us.

  14. 35:54 Remembering grace at the Lord’s Supper

    • As the church remembers this grace through the Lord’s Supper, Elijah prays they would remember where they were before Christ and humble themselves before the cross: because of his perfect life, death, and resurrection, we are co-heirs with Christ and adopted sons and daughters, and when the Father looks at us he sees his one and only perfect Son. He has brought down every dividing wall and made a way for us to be saved from the wrath to come. What is too difficult for us is never too difficult for the Lord.
    • He thanks God that he is loving, gracious, and kind, and also holy, righteous, and just, and will judge evil. Though we struggle with our own wickedness, by faith we are called children of God, and he prays anyone carrying a burden would remember all of who God is.
