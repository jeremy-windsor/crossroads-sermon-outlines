Overview

  1. 00:15 Unsolicited advice, and where Peter finds himself in Luke 5

    Scripture: Luke 5

    • Josh opens with the irritation of unsolicited advice from someone who has never done your job: the person who has never raised children asking, “Have you tried telling them no?”, the YouTube viewer explaining to the mechanic what went wrong, the guest who opens the lid on your smoker and says the temperature seems low, and the WebMD search that ends with telling the doctor what’s wrong. It takes extreme patience when someone who has never done what you do starts telling you how to do it. That is where Simon Peter finds himself in Luke 5.
    • This is week three of King For All. In week one, Luke showed that this king entered real history: not a fairy tale, but an account investigated with a historian’s skill and a doctor’s attention to detail, so that we might be certain. Last week, Mary taught faith, trusting that the child in her womb fulfilled God’s ancient promises.
    • Now the king has grown up and makes his way to the shore of the Sea of Galilee. Jesus steps onto a fisherman’s boat, and that fisherman faces the single most important decision of his life.

  2. 02:14 Peter the career fisherman

    • To read as a first-century reader: Peter is not fishing recreationally, with a folding chair and a cooler and his toes in the sand. This is his livelihood. He knows the lake, the boats, the nets, the weather, the routine, and the habits of the fish. He has done this before.
    • Jesus knew how to work with his hands too, but he was a carpenter turned preacher. Peter has already listened to Jesus preach and has already witnessed his authority.
    • Luke 5 is not Peter’s first encounter with Jesus. Jesus already entered Simon’s home, rebuked his mother-in-law’s fever, and healed her. Peter’s family has benefited from Christ’s kindness and compassion.

    1. 03:38 Jesus meets confident people in their confidence

      • Josh pauses to note that we tend to picture Jesus stepping into desperate situations, like the woman caught in adultery, the woman at the well, and the ten lepers. Today he steps into the life of a man who feels in control, experienced, and capable. Peter does not know the Old Testament scrolls the way Jesus does, but he knows fishing. Peter doesn’t need instructions; he gives them.
      • Jesus meets desperate people in their desperation and confident people in their confidence. No matter where we are in life, we can encounter Jesus, admire Jesus, benefit from Jesus, and even make room for Jesus without ever surrendering our lives to him.
      • Peter had let Jesus into his house and had gone on a brief missions trip with him to Capernaum. Now Jesus calls him to full-time discipleship. Peter keeps offering to help Jesus, and today he will learn that Jesus doesn’t need or want his help. He wants his life.

  3. 05:12 Verse 1: a crowd hungry for the word of God

    Scripture: Luke 5:1

    • Josh reads verse 1. The lake of Gennesaret is another name for the Sea of Galilee, where Jesus had already been teaching, healing the sick, casting out demons, and proclaiming the kingdom.

    1. 05:33 The crowd in chapter 4 and the crowd in chapter 5

      Scripture: Luke 4:43

      • Rewinding to chapter 4: the crowds tried to keep Jesus from leaving because they wanted him to keep healing in their village. Jesus told them he must proclaim the good news of the kingdom of God to the other towns because that is why he was sent. Josh’s point: he wasn’t sent to heal; he was sent to proclaim the kingdom of God.
      • At the end of chapter 4 the crowd wants to keep his healing power in their village. In chapter 5 Luke emphasizes something else: their hunger for the word of God.

    2. 06:24 Do you gather to hear the word?

      • Josh prays that this hunger would take hold of the North Valley, and at least of this body of believers. God has changed him through his word: anything good in him, as a husband and as a father, is there because of it. He is not convinced most Christians gather to hear the word; he thinks other, more sinister reasons have risen up in the church.
      • That is part of why this church does church the way it does. It loves the word of God and will never tire of hearing it preached, hearing it sung, obeying its commands, and expecting fellow believers to obey it too.
      • Josh jokes that if Jesus preached today, there would always be room in the church parking lot, because self-denial doesn’t land well with people who think only about themselves. The question: do you gather to hear the word and let it shape you, or are there other motives lingering?
      • The crowd presses in closer and closer until Jesus is standing at the water’s edge without enough room to teach.

  4. 08:05 Making room for neighbors: move up and park farther out

    • Josh asks, in a spirit of hospitality, that anyone who calls this church home move up to the front starting next week, and, if physically able, park on 41st Street or at the Creative Castles lot, so the close spots and the open seats are clear for first-time visitors. The elders had discussed it, and this verse landed at the right moment. They are seeing movement in that space and have plenty of room for people to come in.

  5. 09:12 Verse 2: two boats, washed nets, and nothing to show for it

    Scripture: Luke 5:2

    • Josh reads verse 2. The fishermen are wrapping up their shift after working all night: they wash the nets, dry them, look for weak spots, and repair them for the next outing. Washing nets is a lame job unless you are picking fish out of them; tonight there is no cha-ching.
    • These are not lazy men and not bad fishermen. They are skilled, generational fishermen, and they caught nothing. Josh tells of fishing with Curtis, who is “part man, part fish,” and catching nothing: if they aren’t biting, you aren’t fishing.
    • As with last week’s disappointment, some are there right now: you worked hard, prayed, planned carefully, gave it time and energy, even invited wisdom in from outside, and the nets came up empty. Empty nets are exhausting because the work feels like a waste. That is where Peter is: tired, disappointed, wanting to finish cleaning the equipment, go home, eat, and be with his wife.

  6. 11:00 Verse 3: the boat becomes a pulpit

    Scripture: Luke 5:3

    • Josh reads verse 3. Jesus chose Peter’s boat, so the boat is now a pulpit and the shoreline an amphitheater; Jesus sits, as Jewish teachers commonly did, and teaches the crowd from the water.
    • The crowd is listening voluntarily, but Peter is a captive audience, an exhausted fisherman counting the minutes until the sermon is over. Josh says many are guilty of over-glorifying those who walked with Jesus: respect, sure, but not reverence. They had good days and bad days, just like us.
    • Peter probably believes he is doing Jesus a favor. But making something available to Jesus is not the same as surrendering everything to Jesus. Many people give Jesus access without giving him authority: he can enter the house when someone is sick, the conversation when the marriage struggles, the finances when money is tight; he can have an hour on Sunday as long as it doesn’t conflict with four hours of NFL; he can comfort at a funeral and give advice when needed. But giving Jesus access when you want to is not the same as giving him authority when he demands it.

    1. 13:20 Jesus in a box, as long as it’s still our boat

      • Josh compares this to Ricky Bobby’s dinner table: we laugh because it is partly true. “I like my Jesus in a box,” available, but not running around without a leash. Nobody says that in the lobby, but walk around Christians long enough and you’ll see it.
      • People love the exalted Christ and his glorious return, hooting and hollering at the king of heaven on a white horse, but when that king starts talking about forgiveness, they become the tired fisherman counting the moments until the sermon ends. They love the Prince of Peace, but when he starts talking about money, they want him out of the boat so they can go home and count their money.
      • We are cool with Jesus being in the boat as long as it’s clear it is still our boat. Peter thought Jesus needed his boat; he is about to discover that Jesus came for the boat’s owner.

  7. 15:05 Verse 4: a sermon to an audience of one

    Scripture: Luke 5:4

    • Josh reads verse 4. Have you ever felt the preacher was preaching directly to you? God, through his Spirit, communicates that way to each of his people. But imagine if it were literal: Josh asking the stewardship team to pull up everyone’s giving history and starting an impromptu sermon aimed at everyone who takes but never gives. The fifty other churches in Anthem would see a big bump next week.
    • In verse 4 the public sermon ends and Jesus begins teaching an audience of one. He doesn’t say, “Let’s go see what happens.” He says, “Let down the nets for a catch,” like a father about to play catch with his child: open your glove, I’m going to put it right there. Jesus knows exactly what he is doing and is not guessing.

  8. 16:48 Verse 5: “Because you say so”

    Scripture: Luke 5:5

    • Josh reads Peter’s response in verse 5. The word translated “master” is used only by Luke and has a range of meanings that all speak of earthly authority: chief, commander, magistrate, governor, president. Peter’s response is respectful but not enthusiastic.
    • Josh imagines what Peter is thinking: the nets are wet because we just washed them; we fished during the best time of day, with the best equipment, the best guys, and the best spots; Jesus, the fish are sleeping. Peter is saying, in the most polite and professional way, “You’re the master when it comes to the kingdom of God, but I’m the fisherman.”
    • Even so, Peter submits to Jesus’ authority: “Because you say so, I will let down the net.” He doesn’t obey because it makes sense; none of this makes sense. He obeys because Jesus said it.

    1. 18:18 Josh’s phone, and Jesus’ command to love enemies

      Scripture: Luke 6:27–29

      • Josh says this is an area he is trying to grow in. Peter had nets in his hands; sometimes Josh has a phone in his. When someone says something cruel, unfair, or untrue about him or someone he loves, he wants to fire back immediately, with the facts, a timeline, screenshots, and a calm, beautifully organized presentation of the evidence with neat bold headings. His instinct is to defend himself or someone he loves and to set the record straight.
      • Then he hears Jesus’ words to those who are listening: love your enemies, do good to those who hate you, bless those who curse you, pray for those who mistreat you, turn the other cheek. Those commands make zero sense to him; everything in him wants vindication and the last word. Jesus tells him to pray and to bless.
      • That doesn’t mean becoming everyone’s punching bag or never correcting harmful behavior. But having a good argument doesn’t mean it is an argument worth having. Josh is learning to put his phone down and pray, and has deleted entire messages, possibly entire sermons, not because he was wrong but because Jesus spoke: “I guess I’ll just pray for you.” The master’s commands must always override our plans: because you say so.

    2. 21:00 We must know what he has said, and who is speaking

      • To say “because you say so,” we must know what is said by him. We may not always know why forgiveness is better than revenge, generosity better than hoarding, sexual purity better than following every desire, truth better than a lie that would make life easier, or loving an enemy better than making them suffer. Everything in us might think we know better than Jesus, and there is nothing wrong with telling him what you think, but where the king speaks, we must obey: “I don’t always like it, I don’t always know why, but because you say so.”
      • That is not blind faith when you know who is speaking. And that is what Peter has yet to figure out: who is speaking.

  9. 22:06 Verses 6–7: the great reveal

    Scripture: Luke 5:6 Luke 5:7

    • Josh reads verse 6. Water that showed no signs of life all night is suddenly bubbling; the ropes strain, the floats disappear, and the same net that came up empty is now too full to lift aboard. They signal their partners in the other boat, who come and fill both boats until both begin to sink. James and John come alongside, fish are flopping everywhere, the nets are tearing, and the boats are over capacity: an absolute joyous nightmare.
    • This wasn’t a better fishing day or a hot spot they’d never seen. These professionals knew the waters so well that this was not a matter of technique or timing. What they hadn’t considered was the person who knew the waters better than they did.
    • Peter is hesitating before Jesus’ authority while the fish do exactly what Jesus says. Jesus made the fish, the lake, and the water; the fish belonged to him before Peter ever pushed out. When the king of creation speaks, creation listens and obeys. Peter is still trying to determine who Jesus is, and the fish seem to know more than Peter at this point: a sermon from a goldfish.

    1. 23:56 The catch is not a prosperity reward

      • This is where the prosperity crowd shows up and hacks the message up: obey Jesus and it will fill your boat; with enough faith your empty nets will fill, your business will grow, your bank account will increase, your dreams will be realized. That is not the lesson Luke wants here, and Josh knows because Peter is about to leave it all.
      • The catch is not a reward and not the result of a great fishing lesson. The catch is a revelation of Christ. Jesus uses the fish to teach men who he is.

  10. 24:48 Verse 8: “Go away from me, Lord”

    Scripture: Luke 5:8

    • You would expect a celebration: Peter running home excited to tell his mother-in-law and his wife, or opening QuickBooks to check net income, or offering Jesus a fifty-fifty business partnership. Peter doesn’t see a business opportunity. He sees the Holy One of heaven, and it shakes him to his core. Josh reads verse 8.
    • Notice the change in Peter’s language. Before the fish delivered their sermon on the deity of Christ, Peter respected Jesus as master and teacher with authority. Now he calls him Lord. The fish helped Peter see Jesus rightly, and now that Peter sees who Jesus is, he can see who Peter is.
    • The holiness of Jesus reveals a bigger problem than empty nets: “I’m a sinful man.” That is what happens when sinners become aware of the presence of a holy God.

    1. 26:30 Isaiah, Job, and Peter

      Scripture: Isaiah 6:1–5 Job 42:5–6

      • Isaiah sees the Lord high and exalted, the temple filled by his glory, and cries, “Woe to me. I’m ruined,” a man of unclean lips. Job spends chapters defending his own righteousness; then God reveals himself, and Job says, “My ears had heard of you, but now my eyes have seen you. Therefore I despise myself and repent in dust and ashes.”
      • Job, Isaiah, and Peter didn’t just encounter something amazing or unbelievable. They encountered holiness, and it brought them to their knees.

    2. 27:08 The Jesus of Scripture and the Jesus modern Christianity manufactures

      Scripture: John 1:14

      • Josh wants the church to see the difference between the Jesus of Scripture and the Jesus that modern Christianity is attempting to manufacture. Campaigns say “he gets us”; Josh thinks we need more campaigns making sure people get him. What if it is a different Jesus from Scripture?
      • The church is creating a Jesus who never makes anyone uncomfortable, soft and cuddly, who affirms every instinct, approves every ambition, and shares every opinion we already hold. That is dumb, because if Jesus supports your opinion and mine, how can he truly support any opinion? We like our Jesus unconditionally supportive and frictionless, less like the Lord of heaven and earth and more like a life coach instructed by the CEO never to challenge a client.
      • Meanwhile here is Peter: “I’m a sinful man.” The Jesus of our invention might be more Instagram friendly and cause less controversy at the Thanksgiving table, but he can’t save, because that Jesus doesn’t exist.
      • The real Jesus comforts, and he also confronts; he welcomes sinners, and he demands they repent. Jesus didn’t just come full of grace; he came full of grace and truth. The light of his holiness exposes the darkness in all of us, and then the mercy in his eyes accepts our repentance and welcomes us home.

    3. 29:40 Comparison ends when the Holy One steps into your boat

      • All four Wyatts are playing fantasy football this year, though they don’t watch football, and Josh is 0 and 2. Explaining matchups to his son, he used Brad, the tall elder, who is better at basketball than Josh, because God gave him an extra ten inches. Which matchup is more likely to be a blowout: Brad versus Josh, or Josh versus his wife?
      • The best player can play a bad game against a good team, and the worst player can play an outstanding game against the worst team. With the right comparison, anyone can feel good about themselves. But comparison ends when the Holy One steps into your boat.
      • You cannot stand beside perfect holiness and pretend your problem is just bad choices, or encounter King Jesus and honestly call yourself a good person. The standard is not the person sitting next to you; the standard is perfection. There is no favorable matchup next to Jesus.

    4. 31:39 Why Peter says “go away”: he is afraid for his life

      Scripture: Exodus 33:20 Judges 13:22

      • Peter knows he is cooked, but this is not so much about what Peter had done as about who he sees. God told Moses in Exodus 33 that no one may see his face and live. When Manoah encountered the angel of the Lord, he said, “We are doomed to die. We have seen God.” Peter may not be a theologian, but he knows sinful people don’t hang around the unveiled presence of a holy God and live to tell about it. “Go away from me” is more than humility; Peter is afraid for his life.
      • This is why the gospel has to be preached to the ends of the earth: fear of God without the gospel can lead to wrong conclusions. Peter asks the one who came to save sinners to leave because he is a sinner. It reveals how precious the gospel is: rubies of infinite value. Peter rightly diagnoses his problem and asks the only one who can cure it to leave.
      • Josh imagines shepherding Peter: “You’re saying go away when you need to draw near, buddy. Peter, you don’t need space. You need a Savior.”

    5. 33:19 Guilt that hides: distance is a sermon from Satan

      Scripture: Romans 6:23

      • Many walk this out like Peter. People sin and stop praying because they feel unworthy, withdraw from worship or fellowship because they feel like hypocrites, or break unity because they feel ashamed: “I need to sort some things out; once I quit this bad habit and repair this mess, I’ll approach God when I’m ready.” If you could cleanse yourself without Jesus, you would not need Jesus.
      • Distance is a sermon from Satan; withdrawal is wisdom from hell. Advance beyond Adam’s approach: there is no healing in hiding. Your sin is not a reason to hide; your sin is why you need Jesus.
      • Josh is not saying sin isn’t a big deal: the wages of sin is death, and this is a big deal. But the gift of God is eternal life in Christ Jesus. Sin presented a significant issue, but God provided the answer. Grace is not God looking the other way; God deals with the full weight of our sin through the work of Christ on the cross.

    6. 34:29 The narrative moves toward the cross

      Scripture: 1 Peter 3:18

      • We may be in a remote fishing village in Luke 5, but the narrative is moving toward Jerusalem and Golgotha, where the Holy One was nailed to a tree. Those nails were more than physical punishment; they were a spiritual reality, as he bore the judgment of the unholy, the righteous for the unrighteous, to bring you to God.
      • Peter doesn’t see this yet, but one day he will help thousands see it for the first time.

  11. 35:20 Verse 10: “Don’t be afraid”

    Scripture: Luke 5:10

    • Josh reads Jesus’ response in verse 10. Peter says, “Go away,” but Jesus stays. Peter says he is sinful, and Jesus doesn’t say “It’s okay, I get you,” or “I know you’re just a pretty good person.” Jesus doesn’t correct Peter, because Peter isn’t wrong. He addresses Peter’s fear: “Don’t be afraid.”
    • Peter is expecting holiness to produce distance. Instead, holiness produces hope.

    1. 36:11 Jesus came to call sinners to repentance

      Scripture: Luke 5:32

      • Later in chapter 5, Luke records Jesus explaining his mission: he has not come to call the righteous, but sinners to repentance. Something amazing is happening on this boat: the holy God has come near, not to consume the sinner who falls short, but to call him to repentance.
      • In this, God has not become less holy, sin has not become less serious, and obedience has not become less important. But in Jesus, the holy one has come near as Savior. The presence and power Peter fears will make his wife a widow is the presence and power that has come to save him.

    2. 36:58 The man who begged Jesus to go away begs sinners to come near

      • Once you discover the gospel, that sinners like you and me can come near to God through Jesus Christ, nothing else matters. What is there to do but tell others, every sinner you know? The man who begged Jesus to go away will spend the rest of his life begging sinners to come near. Jesus has made a way for sinners; the door is open; you can have peace with God forever.
      • Some need that three-word sermon from Jesus right now: “Don’t be afraid.” Don’t hide from the one who heals. Because of the gospel, sinners don’t scatter; they draw near and fall to their knees, and when Jesus picks up their face and says, “Don’t be afraid,” he gives them purpose.

  12. 38:04 “You will fish for people”: grace redirects a life

    Scripture: Luke 5:10

    • Josh reads the rest of verse 10. The king has plans for Peter. Grace doesn’t just relieve our fears; it redirects our entire lives. Jesus calls Peter knowing who Peter is: impulsive, stubborn, arrogant, and about to deny him three times. Jesus is surprised by nothing Peter does or doesn’t do, and he calls him knowing what grace will make him become.
    • The miraculous catch sets the pattern for all of Christ’s ministry, the apostles’ ministry, and the ministry of his local church through the ages. Christ possesses the authority and produces the catch; his servants obey his words and drop the nets; and Christ works through imperfect people. Peter will do amazing things, not because he is impressive but because the risen king will send the Spirit and, through the Spirit, ordinary men will carry heaven’s message and God will gather his people.
    • Christ is the power; Peter is one instrument in a divine ensemble. Jesus does not need Peter, or you, or Josh. The sovereign king could accomplish his purposes without us. But Jesus chose Peter because he loved Peter.

    1. 40:01 A note on apostles

      • Josh takes a moment to clear up confusion many are wrestling with. Peter is an apostle of Jesus Christ. Paul is the last apostle, with a capital A, to be personally chosen and commissioned by Christ, and a key qualification for office apostles is being an eyewitness of the resurrected Christ. No one living today has seen the risen Christ with their own eyes.
      • The word apostolos means “sent one.” Josh says where he stands: he doesn’t think we should call people today apostles because it is unnecessarily confusing. But we are all sent ones.

    2. 40:56 The church doesn’t manufacture the catch

      • Every Christian belongs to the mission Christ gave his church: to make disciples, proclaim the gospel, teach his word, and bear witness to the returning king. The church does not manufacture the catch through clever campaigns and Disney-themed teaching series, and it doesn’t manipulate people into the kingdom or soften the message because the nets are empty. We let down the nets at his word: we proclaim Christ crucified, love our neighbors, pray for the lost, and trust the sovereign king to bring his people home.
      • You may not preach to thousands, but you can explain the gospel to the child at your table. You may not call fire down from heaven, but you might lay a hand on a grieving widow and pray. You may not walk on water, but you can lead a coworker to Christ. You may not feed five thousand, but you can pass out food at the care center.

  13. 41:50 Verse 11: leaving the greatest catch

    Scripture: Luke 5:11

    • The lingering question: will you leave what’s familiar and travel with Jesus into the unknown? Peter will. Josh reads verse 11.
    • What’s in those boats? The greatest catch of their careers, nets and boats full, a life-altering haul, and they leave it to rot. Jesus gives Peter the most successful day of his career and Peter doesn’t even bother to deposit it. The greatest catch of Peter’s life becomes easy to leave after he encounters precious Jesus.
    • There is nothing wrong with success; if you’re successful, praise God and use it for his kingdom. Being the greatest fisherman, businessman, preacher, husband, or dad are all good things. But Peter left a lot of good things because he found someone better.

    1. 43:04 Men’s retreat

      • Josh encourages the men, young and old, to come to the men’s retreat. They are doing good things: working hard, providing for their families, loving their wives like Christ, raising kids and grandkids in the Lord. But sometimes you need to walk away from good things to remember the greatest thing. Your family will understand; Jesus is worthy of your undivided attention. Tap the chair in front of you and register after service.

  14. 43:44 The Lord’s Table: Peter’s movement from pride to fear to grace

    • As the church prepares to drink the juice and eat the bread, Josh asks them to notice Peter’s movement through these eleven verses. At the beginning, Jesus is sitting in Peter’s boat close to shore. In the middle, Peter is kneeling at Jesus’ feet. At the end, Peter is walking behind Jesus, away from his catch. That is discipleship: from pride, through fear, into grace.
    • People will always want Jesus in the boat because they want him to fill their nets, and others want him out of the boat because his holiness exposes their sin. But the Christian will always understand: I need Jesus in my boat because I’m a sinner.
    • Jesus didn’t enter Peter’s boat accidentally; he came for Peter, and if you’re hearing him today it is no accident: he has come for you too. Peter had given Jesus access to his house, his boat, and his time, but Jesus didn’t come for housing, transportation, and support. He came for souls.

    1. 44:56 Would you walk away from the full boat?

      • Maybe you have been around Jesus for years: you have heard his word, seen his work and his kingdom, received his kindness, and even made room for him in certain parts of your life. Would you walk away from that full boat for him, or do you still want to be the captain?
      • Or maybe shame has kept you from drawing near, and you think Christ could receive anyone else but not you. Yet here you are listening to his call while you keep saying, “Go away.” Don’t send the Savior away today. Don’t be afraid; he came for sinners like you and me. Don’t hide. Fall at his feet, fall to your knees, trust his grace, and follow the king.
      • Peter gave Jesus his boat, and Jesus revealed Peter’s heart. Peter confessed his sin, and Jesus silenced his fear. Then the fisherman left everything because he had been caught by the king.

  15. 46:00 Closing prayer

    • Josh prays, thanking God that he caught him and is in the process of catching more; nothing in his life has brought more joy than being caught by the king. He asks God to use the church, to give opportunities to share the word, pray over the widow, love the orphan, speak to the unbeliever, and share Christ, and promises faithfulness.
    • Because the Lord builds his church and is in charge of the catch, he asks to be guarded from manipulations and campaigns to attract people, and to be helped to get out of the way and trust him: “because you say so, I will drop nets. We will all drop our nets.”
