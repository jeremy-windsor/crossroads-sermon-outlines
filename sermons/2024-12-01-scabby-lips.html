Overview

  1. 00:03 Meet Isaiah, our guide for Christmas

    • Chris introduced the church to Christmas “not so gently,” so Steve introduces the man who will guide them through it this year: the prophet Isaiah, more widely quoted at Christmas than all the other prophets combined. To unpack his message accurately, we begin with Isaiah the man. We don’t know much except his early years.

  2. 01:28 Wanting to speak for God versus being called

    Scripture: Isaiah 6

    • Isaiah came from noble lineage, perhaps the royal family itself, which would explain his easy access to the king. He was well educated, as his writing style and mastery of Judah’s checkered history show. Some scholars think the first five chapters are excerpts from his early sermons calling out Judah’s corruption and idolatry.
    • But Isaiah wasn’t yet called. It is one thing to want to speak for God and another entirely to be called to speak for God. That brings us to Isaiah 6: until now Isaiah had been trying the prophet gig with little to show for it; his heart was in the right place, but he hadn’t been set apart.

  3. 03:06 Isaiah and King Uzziah

    • The king, on the other hand, was called. To keep score: the prophet is Isaiah, the king is Uzziah. Uzziah was a good king who expanded Judah’s borders and economy, and best of all, for 51 years he reigned with godliness and worshiped the Lord.
    • In year 52, maybe prideful about his resume, he decided he could burn incense on the altar as well as the priests. When the high priest tried to stop him, he pushed the old man aside, was instantly struck with leprosy, and died a horrible death.
    • Isaiah was heartsick, because he and Uzziah were friends, maybe even family, and because Judah plunged into an even greater moral freefall. So in chapter 6 we find a discouraged man, but at least he is headed to the temple; God’s house is a pretty good place to go when you feel defeated.

  4. 05:34 “In the year that King Uzziah died, I saw the Lord”

    Scripture: Isaiah 6:1 Exodus 33:20 John 1:18

    • Steve can’t say with certainty what Isaiah saw, but he knows what he didn’t see. The Bible says no one can see God’s face and live, and the apostle John made it clear that no one has ever seen God. Isaiah came close, though not as close as Moses, who saw God’s backside. Some scholars think he saw the trailing edge of God’s massive robe; what he clearly saw was where God sat, what he wore, who was with him, and the atmosphere of the room. Both are true: no one has ever seen God, yet to a degree unlike any other person, Isaiah saw the Lord.

    1. 07:02 The throne is never empty

      • He saw him seated on a throne. That is huge, because Isaiah thought the throne was empty now that his friend was dead. But the throne is never empty: no matter what you face or what heartache you endure, the one true King eternally reigns. Judah lost her human king, but Isaiah saw the real King, high and exalted, the train of his robe filling the temple.

    2. 07:47 Holy, holy, holy

      Scripture: Isaiah 6:2–4

      • Above him were seraphim, each with six wings: two covering their faces, two covering their feet, two for flying. They covered themselves because no creature, not even an angel, can withstand the blinding light of God’s glory.
      • They called to one another, “Holy, holy, holy is the Lord Almighty; the whole earth is full of his glory.” That threefold phrase is a Hebrew idiom meaning infinity: no one compares to God in holiness. They sang it antiphonally, back and forth, so thunderously that the doorposts and threshold shook and the temple filled with smoke.

  5. 09:12 “Woe to me, I am ruined”

    Scripture: Isaiah 6:5 Job 42:6 Habakkuk 3:16 Daniel 10:8 Luke 5:8 Revelation 1:17

    • How did Isaiah respond? He didn’t check his phone and say, “Keep it moving, pastor, kickoff is in 20 minutes.” He said, “Woe to me, I am ruined, for I am a man of unclean lips, and I live among a people of unclean lips, and my eyes have seen the King, the Lord Almighty.” In other words, “I’m not good enough to be here; somebody get me out of here.” Ushered into the most magnificent place imaginable, all he wanted was out.
    • That is what Bible heroes always did when they glimpsed God’s holiness. Job said, “I am unworthy… I despise myself and repent in dust and ashes.” Habakkuk said, “My heart pounded, my lips quivered.” Daniel said, “My face turned deathly pale and I was helpless.” Peter said, “Go away from me, I am a sinful man.” John fell at Jesus’ feet as though dead. Coming face to face with an utterly holy God is profoundly threatening, because God is holy and we are so very not holy.

    1. 11:35 The wrong yardstick

      • That’s why, when Steve wants to know whether he is good enough, he doesn’t go to the Lord; he looks at the church. By comparison he does okay, and even better next to politicians, celebrities, and axe murderers (“but I repeat myself”). But whether your yardstick is Mother Teresa or Jeffrey Epstein, you’re using the wrong standard. Only in the blazing presence of God do you see how impeccably holy he is.
      • R.C. Sproul said men are never duly impressed with their own insignificance until they contrast themselves with the majesty of God. The more we look at God, the more evil we see in us. Steve guesses Isaiah’s guilt had to do with something he said, a curse, a lie, hurtful words; all we know is that God’s presence alone convinced him, “I am not good. I can’t get good. Lord, don’t look at me, I am a mess.”

  6. 13:54 The live coal: your guilt is taken away

    Scripture: Isaiah 6:6–7

    • One of the seraphim flew to Isaiah with a live coal taken with tongs from the altar. Picture Isaiah prostrate under his guilt and shame, singing “woe is me,” when he looks up and sees an angel swooping like a dive bomber straight for his face with a lump of live coal. Does he duck? Is this God’s way of taking him out?
    • Instead the angel touches the coal to his mouth: “See, this has touched your lips; your guilt is taken away and your sin atoned for.” Like a scalpel in a surgeon’s hand, the coal was applied to the point of Isaiah’s deepest need, cauterizing and disinfecting his lips.
    • You’re forgiven. It’s over. You don’t have to hang your head in shame for the rest of your life, keep paying off your debt to the legalists in your church who are so disappointed in you, or sing one more stanza of the woe-is-me whine. Your guilt is gone.

  7. 15:57 Why Isaiah’s call comes in chapter 6

    Scripture: Isaiah 1:4 Isaiah 1:15–16 Isaiah 2:10 Isaiah 3:11

    • Something hit Steve hard as he studied: in every other Old Testament prophetic book where the prophet tells of his calling, he does it in chapter 1, but Isaiah waits until chapter 6. Why? Isaiah had correctly assumed God was selecting him as his prophet, but incorrectly assumed it was because of his amazing goodness: nobody else in Judah was as good as he was, so he had better get out there and get those heathens straightened out.
    • Flip through the first five chapters and you see him red in the face, teeth gritted, hostile at all those ornery, good-for-nothing sinners, storming in with his own specially prepared prophetic medication and no calling. Listen to some early condemnations: a sinful nation, a people loaded with guilt, brood of evildoers; your hands are full of blood; take your evil deeds out of my sight; go to the rocks, hide in the ground; woe to the wicked.
    • Judah was a hot mess that needed fixing, but until chapter 6 Isaiah was convinced he was the man for the job. In his mind Judah was a mess because they weren’t good enough; he had forgotten he wasn’t good enough either.

    1. 19:24 No one is good enough; we need a Savior

      • That’s why God invited him into the throne room: Isaiah needed reminding that no one is good enough. “That’s why, Isaiah, we need a Savior. The one I am calling you to prophesy about is the only fix our world needs. You can’t fix you, you can’t fix me, and God doesn’t even expect that we should try.”
      • The path to righteousness isn’t angrily confronting people until they knuckle under, or shouting at holiday dinner that all you nasty sinners must repent. You don’t preach hell with a smile on your face, and you don’t extend grace with a clenched fist. Isaiah hadn’t skipped that class in prophet school; he skipped the school altogether, so God called him in for a refresher.

  8. 20:48 A marked man

    Scripture: Romans 3:23

    • Isaiah left a different man, a marked man, with a scab on his lip: a powerful, painful reminder that all have sinned and fall short of God’s glory, including you, Isaiah.
    • Whenever someone in Scripture had a personal encounter with God, they came away less because of it. Time alone with God will take a toll on you. Isaiah left with scabby lips; Jacob wrestled with God and walked away with a hitch in his hip; Moses, after 40 days on Sinai, wore a veil over his face; Saul was blinded on the road to Damascus.
    • In regular communion with God you could get banged up, whether scabby lips, a gimpy hip, glow-in-the-dark flesh, or eyes blind as a bat. You will receive regular reminders that you’re not all that, that he alone is God and is holy and you are anything but, so that you watch your attitude when he calls you to deal with others’ weaknesses, inconsistencies, and failures.

  9. 22:54 Same message, different demeanor

    Scripture: Isaiah 9:2 Isaiah 10:24–25 Isaiah 26:3–4 Isaiah 40:31

    • After chapter 6 there is a discernible change in Isaiah’s tone: the same message with a different demeanor. Judah still faced harsh judgment, but now Isaiah confronted them with a broken heart, not a prideful, judgmental attitude.
    • Listen to the new approach. Chapter 9: the people walking in darkness have seen a great light. “All my people, do not be afraid; very soon my anger against you will end.” Chapter 26: you will keep in perfect peace him whose mind is steadfast, because he trusts in you; the Lord is the Rock eternal. Those who hope in the Lord will renew their strength and soar on wings like eagles.
    • There was still plenty of rebuke and woe that needed delivering, but with a kinder, gentler spirit, because Isaiah was different. Steve is convinced that every time he opened his mouth he licked his lips and realized, “I’m no different from these people, certainly no better. Man, I hope this lip never heals.” From then on his message had hope attached: the blessed hope of the world was on his way.

  10. 25:31 “Here am I. Send me.”

    Scripture: Isaiah 6:8

    • Back in the throne room, Isaiah is still dabbing blood when the Lord asks, “Whom shall I send? Who will go for us?” Steve wondered for years why Isaiah made a big deal of it when he’d already been doing the prophet gig. Now he gets it: Isaiah had messed up and wanted another shot. “Here am I. Send me.”
    • John Newton said that when people are right with God they are apt to be hard on themselves and easy on others, but when they’re not, they are easy on themselves and hard on others. Could it be that your judgment is so swift, cutting, and divisive because your relationship with God isn’t what it ought to be? Maybe you need time in the throne room to lick your own lips or pull a log out of your eye, or an encounter that leaves you gimpy in the hip or briefly blind, so that if you still speak, you speak in a hushed and gentle tone.
    • Until then you can scream “Here am I, send me” till you’re blue in the face, and God won’t set you loose on the people he loves. He’ll say, “Go fish. If you think you’re good enough for this holy assignment, you’re way too good enough. What I need on my team are people with scabby lips and gimpy hips.” Anybody qualified on those terms? Steve is.

  11. 28:09 The unqualified can’t be disqualified

    Scripture: Psalm 103:10

    • The real point of Isaiah’s story is that no one is qualified. If that’s true, how can the unqualified ever become disqualified? Neither qualification nor disqualification rests on our merits; qualification is entirely a God thing.
    • That is the whole gospel story: our God does not treat us as our sins deserve, not because we’re good enough but because Jesus is. Merry Christmas. Jesus came because God is into restoration, not accusation; he specializes in healing, not finger-wagging; he finds solutions instead of just identifying problems. The best summary is an old bumper sticker Steve wishes someone would bring back: “God loves you anyway.”

    1. 29:51 God calls us back to himself

      Scripture: Acts 17:28

      • Stop trying to hide your sin as Isaiah did, diverting attention from his stuff by pointing his finger and clucking his tongue at yours. God knew, and still chose to use Isaiah anyway.
      • It isn’t just “hate the sin, love the sinner.” God condemns our sin but loves us too much to call us sinners; he calls us back to himself. So he called a liar named Abraham his friend, a murderer named David a man after his own heart, and a lily-livered bowl of jelly named Peter the Rock. God sees us not as we are but as what we can become through him.
      • We will never live a nanosecond free from sin, and claiming otherwise claims equal status with God. We are fallen, broken people, but God loves people and chooses to work through them. In him Steve lives and moves and has his being, and yet he is overwhelmed that God has a need for him.

    2. 31:56 God never used a miracle if he could use a man

      • Why else were we created? Since the outset of time there has been a gnawing in the heavenly Father’s heart, which Jesus described as a father waiting, praying, hoping for his prodigal to come home. God longs for fellowship with a being like himself, with a mind, free will, and an open heart. Steve believes in God, but far more amazing, God believes in him.
      • Comb the Scriptures: God never used a miracle if he could use a man. He divided the Red Sea, but Moses held the staff. Israel crossing the Jordan was entirely God, but the priests stood in the dry riverbed holding the ark. Jesus fed 5,000, but the disciples moved through the crowd distributing the food. God has always used people, though he knows we are nothing but dust.

    3. 33:58 Why not the angels?

      • In Isaiah’s story God had a choice. The angels still had two free wings, and when God asked whom he would send, Steve pictures them ecstatic: “Send us! Angel means messenger!” But they weren’t qualified because they were overqualified. An angel can’t understand the crippling heartache of shame, the ecstasy or the agony of sin, a life gone sour, or a broken heart. We do.
      • That unqualified state is what qualifies us. God cleanses us and leaves a mark that forever brands us as one who knows failure, brokenness, and defeat, a scabby lip or a gimpy hip, and then says, “Now go tell my people.”

  12. 35:50 Lick your lips before you open your mouth

    • When you deny the darkness of your own soul, you end up pointing fingers instead of lending a hand, posting memes on social media, or spending half of Thanksgiving, just two days ago, using your cracked and scabby lips to rail against whoever. Just pick someone worse than you; that’s how the game is played, and you know exactly where to poke. What you ought to do is lick your lips and remember your own nasty sin list.
    • Isaiah wasn’t unfit because he had dirty lips; God deals with that dirt, and he’s good at it. He was unfit because he forgot he had dirty lips. He wasn’t unfit because he had sin, which we all have, but because he got way too much pleasure reminding others of theirs.

    1. 37:33 A halo on too tight

      Scripture: Romans 6:1–2

      • A man with an awful headache told his doctor he never touches liquor, thinks smoking is disgusting, and is in bed at 10:00 with his wife. Asked if it was a sharp, shooting pain, he said yes. The doctor’s diagnosis: “You have your halo on too tight. Let me loosen it some.”
      • Steve isn’t preaching a pro-sin message; Paul clearly said, “Shall we go on sinning so that grace may abound? By no means.” It’s a friendly reminder that you can be rabidly anti-sin and still full of sin, and not struggling where someone else struggles doesn’t mean you should apply for sainthood or try on halos at Target. If you think you deserve one, wear it loose and let it tilt, because we all have scabby lips and gimpy hips.
      • Get over your bad self. Your battles may differ from Steve’s, but take inventory: corpses strewn over your life, smoke billowing from moral, spiritual, and relational mishaps, loved ones discarded because they weren’t worthy or wouldn’t take the steps you thought they should. Forgetting the ravages of your own story is a spiritual peril. If you want God to use you with struggling, broken people, learn to lick your lips before you ever open your mouth.

    2. 40:40 Put down the hammer

      Scripture: Acts 20:31 Jeremiah 9:1

      • When you’re holding a hammer, everything looks like a nail. Put down the hammer and grab a tube of ChapStick instead, so that if God leads you to address someone broken by their sin, you address it, but not like the woman in the lobby who, brow furrowed, said, “Pastor, what we need in this church is a really good sermon on hell.” Steve thought, “And I bet you’re the only one qualified to preach it.”
      • Steve agrees with Spurgeon, who told his students that when you preach on heaven your face should be radiant, when you preach on hell your normal face will do, and then smiled: “Never preach on hell unless you have tears in your eyes.” That’s how Jesus did it, weeping over Jerusalem’s rebellion. Paul never stopped warning the Ephesians night and day with tears, and Jeremiah said, “Oh, that my head were a spring of water and my eyes a fountain of tears.”

  13. 42:20 The Christmas promises and the suffering Messiah

    Scripture: Isaiah 7:14 Isaiah 9:6 Isaiah 53:3–5

    • Very soon Isaiah will prophesy beautiful words about a virgin with child, named Immanuel, God with us, and a child to be born and a son given, called Wonderful Counselor, Mighty God, Everlasting Father, and Prince of Peace.
    • But before the gospel can get that good, that same child would be despised and rejected, a man of sorrows familiar with suffering, pierced for our transgressions, crushed for our iniquities, the punishment that brought us peace placed on him. All of it to this end: that mercy could happen. Jesus would be punished so that people ravaged by sin, their own or others’, and people who are hurting, lonely, desperate, and can’t ever seem to get it right, could be welcomed in anyway, and grace extended anyway. Every one of us, especially Steve, desperately needs that mercy.

  14. 44:52 His kindness leads us to repentance

    Scripture: Romans 2:4

    • If you don’t think you need mercy, you need a severe case of chapped lips. Only then will you realize people aren’t motivated to walk the path of righteousness by your rules, control, intimidation, and threats, but as you lift up Jesus, extend God’s love in massive doses, and trust the Holy Spirit alone to redirect their hearts. That’s what Isaiah the man needed to change about himself.
    • Jesus would not come to hold a two-ton stone over our heads or pounce whenever we step out of line. It is his kindness that leads us to repentance, and his grace purchased at Calvary that draws us into obedience. That’s why the one thing we needed was baby Jesus.

  15. 46:29 Come into the throne room

    • As the church prepares for Christmas, Steve invites them to join Isaiah in the throne room: come as you are, but don’t stay that way. Bring ChapStick and be prepared to come clean. The only painful part has already been fully done; the only damage left is what admitting you are a sinner does to your pride, and Steve’s pride needs damaged too. Come clean, and you will find all the mercy your broken, desperate soul could ever want or need, and more.

  16. 47:44 Prayer at the Lord’s Table

    • Steve prays as the church comes to the meal: Jesus never wants us to forget the sin that caused him to do what he did, not so we wallow in it, but so that as we deal with the sins of others we do it with kindness. He asks the Lord to meet them, be firm, and ultimately, because of Jesus, show them grace, because they can’t do it without it.
