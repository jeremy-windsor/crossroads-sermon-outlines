Overview

  1. 00:16 The series closes by returning the tongue to its highest uses

    Scripture: Ephesians 4:15

    • Josh announces the conclusion of the James series and names the day’s subjects: prayer and healing.
    • He recalls the earlier teaching on the tongue: it can destroy or build, weaken or strengthen, hurt or heal, complain or compliment, swear or sing, bear false witness or speak truth in love.
    • The church shines brightest when the tongue is engaged in three divine activities: proclaiming God’s Word; praying for and with one another; praising the Lord personally and publicly.
    • A brief notes/technology interruption occurs at 01:39–02:00. Josh resumes by restating the contrast and the three holy uses of speech, preserving the introduction’s emphasis rather than changing direction.

  2. 02:26 Prayer begins with the greatness of the triune God and the gospel of Jesus

    Scripture: Exodus 3:14; Revelation 22:13 John 1:1–3 John 1:14 Colossians 1:15 2 Corinthians 5:21 John 19:30 1 Corinthians 2:8 Romans 10:9–11 Matthew 10:32–33

    • Josh pauses before James because prayer cannot be understood without considering who receives it. God is the one living God, Maker of heaven and earth, eternal, immutable, independent, wise, holy, free, absolute, loving, gracious, merciful, truthful, righteous, providential, all-seeing, and Judge. God is not distant: like a father, he works in his people according to his unchanging will. God reveals himself as Father, Son, and Spirit—coequal and coeternal. Josh insists that one cannot claim to know God while rejecting the Son.
    • Josh develops Christ’s eternal identity and incarnation. Jesus was not created; the Son already existed before his virgin conception. John 1 presents the Word as with God, as God, the agent of creation, and then as flesh dwelling among humanity. Josh cascades through biblical names and titles for Jesus to stress that no single title exhausts his glory.
    • The doctrinal detour is deliberate: at 07:03 Josh anticipates the question, “What does this have to do with James?” and answers, “Everything. You can’t apply James unless you know who God is.”
    • He moves from incarnation to atonement. The sinless Christ was made sin for sinners, bore sin on the cross, paid sin’s wage, declared the work finished, and secured redemption and forgiveness through his blood. The weekly Lord’s Supper physically reminds the church how far God went to save sinners.
    • He presents the cross as God’s wisdom outmaneuvering evil. Satan’s apparent victory became the means of his defeat; the serpent struck Christ’s heel and had his own head crushed. The rulers crucified the Lord of glory because they did not understand God’s wisdom; the cross was a stumbling block to Jews and foolishness to Gentiles.
    • Josh turns gospel proclamation into immediate response. He quotes Romans 10 and asks whether the church believes Jesus is Messiah and Son of God and will cry out for salvation. He quotes Jesus’ warning about public acknowledgment, asks professing believers to stand, and then asks first-time confessors to remain standing. A congregant responds; Josh celebrates, offers baptism that day, and urges immediate public testimony in the water.
    • He says receiving God’s wisdom requires a renewed mind because the gospel works through paradox: life through death, victory through defeat, and the Lion triumphing as a Lamb. Rahab becomes his illustration: a condemned Canaanite prostitute is rescued from Jericho, joined to God’s people, and becomes a picture of redemption. This gospel celebration transitions back to prayer: if the church truly believes God is this great, its prayer life should show it.

  3. 12:35 Prayer must be the believer’s first defense, not testimony against God’s power

    • Josh identifies a practical hypocrisy: Christians make extraordinary claims about God while treating him as something other than their first line of defense in trouble.
    • A weak prayer life can function as testimony that one privately thinks God is weak.
    • His 1998 Chicago Bulls illustration makes the point concrete: insisting on taking the last shot while Michael Jordan is on the team is absurd; the right play is “pass to 23 and get out the way.”
    • He wants Crossroads to be a praying church, but one with a healthy understanding of both prayer and healing rather than hype or confusion.
    • Transition: he announces a slow, verse-by-verse reading to learn what James teaches about biblical Christian care.

  4. 13:47 James 5:13: answer trouble with prayer and happiness with praise

    Scripture: James 5:13a Hebrews 4:16 James 5:13b Acts 16:25 Colossians 4:2 James 5:13

    • Trouble: “Is anyone among you in trouble? Let them pray.” Josh notes that some translations use “afflicted,” but prefers “in trouble” because it captures the broad range of suffering believers are guaranteed to face. Prayer is the first responder to a believer’s fire. The believer may ask for removal, but should also ask for wisdom to understand the trouble and use it for God’s glory. God may remove it or provide grace to endure it. Jesus in Gethsemane illustrates a request not granted in the requested form: the cup remained, but the Father supplied strength for the cross. Simon of Cyrene and Hebrews 4 reinforce grace arriving in the time of need. Josh challenges resentment toward God when believers neglect the grace already supplied for endurance.
    • Happiness: “Is anyone happy? Let them sing songs of praise.” Happiness and trouble can coexist. Anyone can sing after trouble ends; maturity sings while grief is still heavy. Paul and Silas praying and singing in prison show that other people hear a Christian’s “night song” and “prison prayers.” Josh asks who has heard the congregation’s. Believers must not credit themselves for happiness and blame God for trouble. Relationship with God remains intimate in both trouble and peace.
    • Prayer and praise as church health: when either is neglected, complaining and grumbling fill the space. Praise itself is prayer; the church had already sung three prayers that morning.
    • Public posture and witness: Josh contrasts careful prayer posture with reluctance to lift hands in surrendered praise. In an airport after his stepfather’s celebration of life, Josh prayed over a meal with his family. A stranger later told his children to thank God for a father willing to lead them in faith. The story illustrates the “quiet witness of public faithfulness.” Fear of others’ opinions must not turn worship into convenience rather than devotion.
    • Transition: Paul’s command to devoted, watchful, thankful prayer introduces James’s instructions for the sick.

  5. 18:45 James 5:14–15: the sick call their elders, using spiritual and natural means

    Scripture: James 5:14–15 Mark 6:13 Luke 10:30–37, especially v. 34 James 5:14

    • Josh reads the instruction for the sick to call the church’s elders, receive prayer and anointing with oil in the Lord’s name, and trust the Lord to raise them up.
    • He slows down to define “sick.” The Greek term can denote weakness in several senses, but Josh judges physical illness to be primary here, without excluding spiritual weakness. His jokes about being “sick” of Chiefs championships and lazy men illustrate the range of ordinary word usage.
    • He examines anointing with oil. Mark 6 supplies the only other New Testament example of the same practice; Luke 10 depicts oil poured medicinally on the wounded man. His synthesis is prayer and medicine: Christian care brings both spiritual and natural resources and seeks to meet both spiritual and physical needs. This is not a foolproof technique. Josh has witnessed what he considers miraculous healing through prayer without oil, heard of healing through non-elders, prayed for relatives who nevertheless died of cancer, and prayed with elders for another cancer patient whose cancer disappeared inexplicably.
    • He stresses whom the sick person should call. James says the local church’s elders, not a celebrity faith healer. Elders carry responsibility for souls; a sick believer may be physically unable to pray, spiritually frustrated, isolated, or in need of help and confession. Josh rejects commercial or theatrical substitutes—blessing blankets, white coats, blowing, spitting, or special water—and names Crossroads’ elders as the people members should contact. Members may call Josh, who will arrange the group.
    • Transition: verse 16 exposes the confession component that popular discussions of healing often avoid.

  6. 23:32 Confession, discipline, and healing cannot be separated

    Scripture: James 5:16 1 Corinthians 11:30–32

    • Josh reads James 5:15b–16: forgiveness, mutual confession, mutual prayer, healing, and the powerful prayer of a righteous person belong together.
    • He emphasizes the condition in 1 John 1:9: if believers confess, God forgives and purifies.
    • Josh believes James is describing at least one situation in which sickness is connected to sin. He recounts one hospitalized man who reported an immediate physical release and healing during Josh’s phone prayer. The man apparently confessed to God but not to Josh or the elders, returned to the same lifestyle without accountability, and later became much worse. Josh connects the outcome to Jesus’ warning about an expelled unclean spirit returning with others and assigns Matthew 12 as homework.
    • Paul’s warning in 1 Corinthians 11 confirms that weakness, sickness, and even death can be divine discipline, intended to prevent final condemnation with the world.
    • Elders may therefore need to visit someone too ill, shut in, or isolated to come to church and confess.
    • The conclusion is explicit: God’s healing ministry cannot be disconnected from humble confession. Confession and prayer open the door to healing grace; sin’s grip loosens, heaviness lifts, and the Spirit has room to restore hearts and, at times, bodies.

  7. 26:13 A prayer of faith trusts God’s will; it does not manufacture certainty

    Scripture: James 5:15a Mark 9:24 1 John 5:14–15

    • Josh returns to James 5:15: “the prayer offered in faith” and “the Lord will raise them up.”
    • The agent of healing is clarified. The anointing does not heal; God does. Oil represents an ordinary medicinal resource, while prayer calls for God’s decisive touch. God can heal with or without oil or any particular minister, in whatever manner and timing he wills. Josh rejects healers who center their own résumé or claim the title that belongs to God. In his judgment, such ministries often produce more confusion than healing.
    • He confronts the claim that faith means having no doubt. In the imagined hospital room, the sick person is told that healing depends on eliminating doubt; if healing does not occur, suspicion, blame, and gossip follow. The father in Mark 9 openly combines belief with unbelief, exposing the cruelty of making perfect psychological certainty the condition.
    • Josh defines a prayer of faith as a desire for God’s will, not the absence of doubt. First John 5 grounds confidence in asking according to God’s will. Faith confidently expects God’s will to be accomplished while still making concrete requests.
    • His staff’s ninety-minute prayer meeting illustrates this posture: they asked for servant-hearted workers, protection for growing families and children, and unity in the body, while admitting uncertainty about the coming year.
    • At sickbeds Josh often does not know whether God means to heal or call his child home. His simple prayer is, “Lord, if it is your will, heal your child.”
    • He rejects the assertion that healing is always God’s will. Faith instead asks for joy and a way to glorify God in any outcome, ending with love and trust: “Whatever happens, Lord, I love you and I trust you.”

  8. 31:07 Elijah shows that ordinary, imperfect people can pray earnestly

    Scripture: James 5:16b–18 1 Kings 17:1; 18:1, 16–46; 19:1–4 Matthew 6:7 1 Timothy 2:1–4

    • James 5:16b–18 declares righteous prayer powerful and effective, then presents Elijah as a human being like everyone else whose prayers stopped and restarted the rain.
    • Josh retells Elijah’s story. Ahab and Jezebel led Israel into Baal worship; Elijah prayed for drought. On Mount Carmel he mocked Baal’s prophets, prayed, and God answered with fire, proving the Lord to be the living God. Elijah then prayed seven times for rain, and God sent it. Yet this prophet was not superhuman or perfect: after Carmel he became afraid, discouraged, and fled. His confidence rested in God’s faithfulness, not his own steadiness.
    • “Prayed earnestly” concerns the attitude rather than the sheer quantity or length of words. Jesus rejects pagan babbling. Josh describes Elijah’s “prayers having prayers”: a continual state of prayerful fellowship, request, and awareness of God.
    • Refrain: “If you’re not asking, you’re not really praying.”
    • Josh calls prayer the world’s greatest power—greater than political, economic, military, social, cultural, or religious power—and laments that the church holds this weapon while acting like an underdog.
    • Applications widen from personal healing to public intercession: pray for the nation and its leaders, including opponents; pray for and encourage younger generations rather than merely criticizing them; let prayers attest to the same greatness of God that Christian theology proclaims.
    • Paul’s instruction to Timothy confirms petitions, prayers, intercession, and thanksgiving for everyone and for rulers, toward peaceful and godly life and God’s saving purpose.

  9. 35:14 James 5:19–20 closes with restoration of wanderers and urgency for the lost

    Scripture: James 5:19–20 Ecclesiastes 9:18 Luke 22:31–32 1 Peter 4:8 1 Peter 5:8 Romans 6:23

    • Josh reads the final two verses: bringing back one who wanders from truth saves from death and covers many sins.
    • For a series about doing the Word, James ends with perhaps the greatest action one believer can take for another: restoration, or “the opportunity to be Jesus to the already redeemed.”
    • Wandering can be a slow backslide or a sudden face-plant, but the mission is the same. Seek another’s restoration with the same urgency one would want shown toward oneself. Josh applies this to addiction, disruption, unforgiveness, and division. Refusing to speak truth in love leaves others exposed to further harm.
    • Ecclesiastes warns that one sinner destroys much good. Concerns should reach the elders before someone leaves or disaster occurs. Josh welcomes concerns but says the church’s job is to prevent a train wreck, not watch it happen and later announce that it was predictable. Restoration requires hard conversations; sometimes believers need to be “converted” or witnessed to by their own church.
    • Jesus’ care for Peter supplies the model: he prayed for Peter, spoke hard truth, and commissioned the restored disciple to strengthen his brothers.
    • The church sometimes must “seek and save the found,” always in love. Peter later teaches that deep love covers many sins. Love does not sweep sin under a family rug; love cannot exist without truth. Honest confession permits God’s purification and complete forgiveness.
    • Josh intensifies the appeal from wandering believers to unbelievers. If rescuing a believer matters, rescuing one whose future apart from Christ is hell is even more urgent. Because the devil aggressively prowls for prey and sin pays death, believers must pursue a deliberate gospel battle plan; God’s gift remains eternal life in Christ.
    • Final diagnostic questions: When did the hearer last take a social or cultural risk to present the gift? When did the hearer last confess, receive forgiveness, ask God to reveal someone to serve, pray over that ministry, and then show up to work?
    • Transition: the church must “grow up and start looking alive,” which leads into an embodied response rather than a merely verbal conclusion.

  10. 44:32 Get out of the stands: three arenas of response, prayer, and communion

    Scripture: Matthew 9:37–38; Luke 10:2 James 5:13a Luke 22:19–20; 1 Corinthians 11:23–25

    • Josh uses a football stadium to reject a spectator model of church: 50,000 people watching eleven performers is not the church.
    • James invites believers onto the field to walk by the Spirit. Josh gives a threefold response: At home: pray for the lost and the church’s mission, enter the Word, and actively kill sin. Out there: invite people to church and share one’s testimony, practicing first in a life group if needed. In the church: serve children and youth, encourage believers, meet needs through Crossroads Cares, become a need coordinator, give financially, or serve in café, tech, worship, and gospel ministry.
    • The Great Commission is carried out not by God’s “elite” but by God’s elect. Jesus said the workers—not spectators—are few; kingdom work requires belonging to him, not being exceptional.
    • Josh invites the church to offer God life, hands, time, gifts, home, heart, and wealth because the harvest is ready.
    • During the response song, members are to be the church: sign up through the app; seek the elders for prayer if ill or struggling; participate through the Lord’s Supper, singing, possible baptism, giving, and service.
    • Closing maxim: people will not see the church differently until believers see it differently. “The church does not exist to serve you. It exists so you can serve others.”
    • Closing prayer (44:32–46:38): Josh repents first for his own shallow prayer life before the great, living God. He asks God to change hearts, free people from shame, grant courage to confess, and grant gentleness to answer confession with grace. He invites the Spirit’s work through song, prayer, giving, service, baptism, and oil, asking for spiritual and physical healing according to God’s will.
    • Communion meditation (46:40–47:50): Communion is presented not merely as ritual but as a pattern for life: in trouble, pray, because God meets people amid struggle, doubt, and pain. Bread and cup proclaim Christ’s body and blood given for forgiveness and healing. Unbelievers should refrain and consider Christ’s call; believers should name what they must bring to God, then continue in daily prayer, trust, and community rather than leaving the response at the table.
