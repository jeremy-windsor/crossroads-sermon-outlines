Overview

  1. 00:36 The Christian relay asks whether weary believers can keep going

    • Josh moves from an opening family joke and the psychology of exhausted athletes to the original readers’ spiritual fatigue, the relay imagery joining Hebrews 11 and 12, and the opening command to run with endurance.

    1. 00:36 A bumper-video joke gives way to the endurance question

      • Josh jokes that his son Nolan will miss the Hebrews-series bumper more than the preaching, then turns immediately to the moment every endurance athlete faces: ‘Can I keep going?’
      • When adrenaline and excitement fade into pain, the battle becomes mental. The athlete must reject the path of least resistance repeatedly even though continuing does not become easier.

    2. 02:11 Hebrews arrived for believers already running out of gas

      • The first Jewish-Christian readers were not at the starting line. They had already suffered, sacrificed, and followed Christ for years, but spiritual and emotional weariness made some question whether Jesus was worth it.
      • The letter answers their question—and every believer’s—with confidence: Jesus is worthy of everything, so the church can meet the next blow and keep going.

    3. 03:09 Hebrews 11 passes the baton to Hebrews 12

      • Hebrews 11 displays people who finished by faith; Hebrews 12 passes the baton to those who follow. Noah, Abraham, Moses, Rahab, prophets, apostles, pastors, and teachers form a continuing Christian relay.
      • The church is not called merely to cheer historic saints from the stands. ‘It’s our turn to run,’ and every believer belongs down on the track.

    4. 04:01 Agōn names a race of agony, not ease

      Scripture: Hebrews 12:1

      • Josh explains that the Greek word translated ‘race’ is agōn, source of ‘agony,’ and illustrates it with an ancient pentathlon that ended in a brutal boxing match.
      • The point is deliberately plain: the Christian life is hard. Conversion and baptism do not make all present struggles disappear, though Jesus will finally set all things right.
      • Josh frames the gospel victory as Satan’s defeat: the Son was killed, raised, and proved the enemy’s cause lost.

    5. 05:29 The Spirit coaches the race in five ways

      • Josh previews five lessons in Hebrews 12: how to run while weary, what slows the runner, how to understand suffering, how to become assistant coaches who strengthen others, and why every painful step will be worth it.
      • Josh identifies Paul as the human writer through whom the Holy Spirit speaks as a track coach, while the chapter later moves from athletic coaching into the family room of a loving Father.

    6. 06:29 Run the marked race with eyes fixed on Jesus

      Scripture: Hebrews 12:1–3

      • Josh reads Hebrews 12:1–3: surrounded by a great cloud of witnesses, believers must throw off hindrance and sin, run with perseverance, fix their eyes on Jesus, and consider his endurance so they do not lose heart.
      • Jesus is the pioneer and perfecter who endured the cross for the joy ahead, scorned its shame, and sat at God’s right hand.

    7. 07:01 The witnesses testify to God’s faithfulness; they are not spectators

      Scripture: Hebrews 12:1a

      • Some people of faith saw extraordinary earthly deliverance, while others died without earthly validation. The same faith and God can issue different marching orders, so earthly outcomes cannot be the runner’s fuel.
      • Josh rejects the picture of Moses and Noah watching or betting on individual Christians from heaven. The word ‘witnesses’ points to testimony: their lives bear witness that God can see his people through.
      • Moses’ rescue work and Noah’s ark become testimonies that God can use present believers and, far more, what God himself has made to save.

    8. 08:45 Approval-seeking cannot sustain the race

      • The original readers were tempted to regain acceptance from the world they had left. Josh warns that a race fueled by applause ends when family, friends, or coworkers question Christian convictions; the race is sustained instead by confidence in God’s promises.
      • He imagines his younger basketball self making a big shot: even if the crowd chanted his name, he would look for his father’s smile. The illustration points to pleasing Jesus rather than reading the room.
      • Public approval comes and goes—people may praise churches during disaster and dismiss them when life feels stable—but Christ’s settled approval does not fluctuate.

    9. 10:18 Run for the smile of Jesus

      Scripture: Hebrews 12:2

      • The one who called the believer is also waiting at the finish line. Josh therefore tells the church to fix its eyes on Jesus and run for his smile.
      • This prepares the shift from studying experienced racers to examining the runner’s own unnecessary weight and entangling sin.

  2. 11:00 Throw off weights and execute the sin that entangles

    • Josh distinguishes morally neutral burdens from sin, applies the first category to busyness and prideful self-reliance, then uses marathon clothing and a pastoral story about pornography to demand decisive warfare rather than negotiated coexistence with sin.

    1. 11:00 Experienced runners and honest self-examination both matter

      Scripture: Hebrews 12:1b

      • Josh explicitly cites the ESV wording, ‘lay aside the weights,’ from the second half of Hebrews 12:1. The command requires more than studying veteran runners; it requires looking honestly at what the present runner carries.
      • Josh distinguishes ‘weight that hinders’ from ‘sin that entangles.’ A weight need not be sinful; even a good activity can become harmful when imbalance makes it too heavy for faithful running.
      • Josh makes the category personal: ‘What weight are you carrying that Jesus never asked you to carry?’

    2. 12:01 Busyness can become an idol: stop it or share it

      • Constant activity becomes idolatrous when people cannot rest and begin using minds, bodies, and resources for self rather than God’s glory.
      • Josh’s simple offloading tools are ‘Stop it’ or ‘Share it.’ Pride that will neither ask for help nor permit help keeps the runner unnecessarily weighted.

    3. 13:09 Sin wraps around the legs and turns into chains

      • No sensible marathoner chooses jeans or other clothing that grows heavy and tangles around the legs. Josh uses that absurdity to picture tolerated sin.
      • Sins accepted today become tomorrow’s chains. Scripture’s language is urgent because sin does not merely inconvenience the race; it can bring the runner down.

    4. 14:08 Jesus commands radical amputation of sin’s supply lines

      Scripture: Matthew 5:29–30

      • Josh quotes Jesus’ commands to tear out an offending right eye and cut off an offending right hand. The body part is not the true problem; the violent image displays sin’s deadly seriousness.
      • Anything continually feeding sin must die. The command is not to manage a safer dose but to remove the source.

    5. 14:25 A pornography struggler defended devices that were destroying his marriage

      • Drawing on his own struggles and pastoral calling, Josh recounts advising a man with a pornography addiction to take the computer, router, and smartphone outside and shoot them all, then offered to buy him a flip phone.
      • Each objection protected technology, work, or social media, but none protected the man’s marriage. Josh asks why anyone would defend what seeks to destroy.
      • The extreme counsel matches extreme stakes: hell, divorce, a devastated spouse, children living with a lust-enslaved father, a hardened heart, and standing before Christ after preferring secret sin.

    6. 16:02 Christian athletes practice mortification, not negotiation

      • ‘We don’t negotiate with the sins that nailed Christ to the cross. We execute them.’ Josh calls Christians to kill, delete, cancel, remove, leave, or burn a bridge when it feeds lust, pride, greed, bitterness, or unbelief.
      • Fewer comforts are better than refusing to lay aside sin and never finishing. Unchecked sin kills joy, marriage, home, witness, and usefulness; confession must come before the heart hardens.
      • Josh adds Jesus’ warning that sin can cause someone else to stumble, widening the consequences beyond the runner.

    7. 17:41 Christ died to free his people from sin’s grip

      • Christ’s death does not merely forgive guilt; it frees people from sin’s mastery. Josh’s final instruction is to let go of what Christ came to remove.
      • Great faith runners travel light rather than carrying the very burden their Savior removed.

  3. 17:57 Fix the whole gaze on Jesus, who endured the cross for joy

    • Josh develops the command to look away from competitors, conditions, and hurdles toward Jesus alone. The bronze serpent illustrates trusting sight; Jesus’ own faithful life and cross show both the cost and the joy ahead; and weary hearers are directly urged not to quit.

    1. 17:57 The command is a fixed gaze, not one item in a multitask

      Scripture: Hebrews 12:1b–2a Hebrews 12:2a

      • Josh repeats the call to run the agōn with perseverance while fixing the eyes on Jesus, the pioneer and perfecter of faith.
      • The biblical verb means looking away from everything else, not giving Jesus one glance among many. Josh says life, joy, and outpour flow from that gaze; the runner must stop inspecting another person’s strength, the track, the hurdles, or the surrounding crowd.
      • Josh turns the text into a weekly diagnostic: ‘Where are your eyes?’ Whatever currently owns attention must yield to a renewed look at Jesus.

    2. 19:52 Looking to Jesus both saves and sustains

      Scripture: John 3:14–15

      • The Israelites bitten by snakes looked at the lifted bronze serpent and were healed; Jesus used that wilderness event while speaking to Nicodemus.
      • Josh says the sign also reminded the unbitten of God’s continuing healing presence. In the same way, trusting Jesus is both how salvation begins and how faith is sustained.
      • The pioneer started this work in believers, and the perfecter will finish it.

    3. 20:50 Jesus lived the faith he teaches

      • Jesus did not merely teach prayer; Josh points to his repeated withdrawal for prayer as evidence of a vibrant life of dependence.
      • He endured more than every hero in Hebrews 11 and is therefore both the flawless example and the one who brings faith to completion.

    4. 21:13 The perfecter endured opposition, shame, and the cross

      Scripture: Hebrews 12:2b–3

      • Josh returns to Hebrews 12:2–3: for the joy set before him, Jesus endured the cross, scorned shame, took his seat at God’s right hand, and endured sinners’ opposition so his people would not grow weary.
      • He tells listeners to remember this if they remember nothing else: Jesus endured betrayal, false charges, mockery, scourging, nails, suffocation, and the wrath sinners deserved.

    5. 21:53 The forsaken cry shows judgment deeper than physical pain

      Scripture: Psalm 22:1; Matthew 27:46; Mark 15:34

      • Josh quotes Jesus’ cry, ‘My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?’ to stress that the cross involved divine judgment, not only bodily agony.
      • Jesus drank the cup reserved for sinners. The nails did not overpower the Son of God; he willingly remained.

    6. 22:20 Love and the joy ahead held Jesus there

      • Josh names the joy of obeying and pleasing the Father, accomplishing redemption, walking out of the tomb, being exalted 40 days later, returning in glory, and bringing many sons and daughters to glory.
      • Jesus looked beyond Friday to Sunday, beyond resurrection to ascension and return, beyond shame to the throne, and beyond the cross to the crown. That future victory enabled present endurance.

    7. 23:11 The cross, resurrection, and return make endurance rational

      • Josh compresses the hope: Christ’s cross proves he is committed, his resurrection proves he is capable, and revelation of his return proves he is coming.
      • A person who sees only present suffering will quit; a person fixed on the suffering servant—the crucified, risen, reigning, returning King—can bear today’s cross while awaiting tomorrow’s crown.

    8. 23:58 A direct word to the person considering quitting

      Scripture: Psalm 23:4 Philippians 1:21

      • Josh pauses the exposition for someone who may be contemplating surrender: ‘Don’t quit. Jesus already won the race. Keep running.’ The next morning’s prayer can simply give the Lord one’s steps and ask for courage.
      • He preaches Psalm 23’s confidence through the valley because God is present, then joins Paul’s claim that living is Christ and dying is gain with his own line: “If I live, I belong to Christ. If I die, I still belong to Christ.”
      • Every breath and day can glorify and serve Christ. Even if faithfulness finally costs life, grace makes that life available to him.

  4. 25:10 Receive hardship as a loving Father’s discipline

    • Hebrews moves from the stadium into the family room. Josh reads the discipline passage, distinguishes a Father’s formative love from punishment and natural consequences, applies it through parenting and common failures, and grounds freedom from condemnation in Christ’s completed work.

    1. 25:10 Believers praise God’s presence but often resent his parenting

      • Josh names a common hypocrisy: Christians want the Shepherd’s comfort while resisting the rod that confronts and corrects.
      • The passage first humbles comfortable believers by noting that their struggle has not reached bloodshed and by comparing it with historic and present persecution.

    2. 25:48 Most hearers have not resisted sin to the point of bloodshed

      Scripture: Hebrews 12:4

      • Josh quotes Hebrews 12:4 and contrasts ordinary trials with the 380 million Christians living under persecution and thousands killed each year, including in Nigeria, Congo, and Egypt.
      • The question ‘Have you bled for Christ?’ is meant to reset perspective when believers sweat small troubles.

    3. 26:24 The Lord disciplines children he loves

      Scripture: Hebrews 12:5–11 Proverbs 3:11–12

      • Josh reads Hebrews 12:5–11: do not treat discipline lightly or lose heart; hardship can be received as discipline because God treats believers as children.
      • Earthly parents discipline imperfectly for a short time, but God acts for his children’s good so they may share his holiness. Painful training later yields righteousness and peace.
      • Josh identifies the embedded encouragement as Proverbs 3, whose appeal joins discipline directly to divine love.

    4. 27:16 Forgetting the word produces discouragement

      • Josh notices the link between quitting and forgetting Scripture: a runner who forgets God’s word becomes discouraged and gives up.
      • He urges healthy Bible habits and invites anyone needing a plan to ask Josh, an elder, or staff member rather than pretending to be a scholar or walking alone.

    5. 28:02 Suffering needs more than a coach’s demand for another gear

      Scripture: Hebrews 12:5

      • At verse 5 the imagery moves from athletics to family. A weary athlete needs coaching, but a suffering child also needs a Father’s assurance of love.
      • God is not merely developing performance so the believer can finish a race; he is raising sons and daughters to become like his Son.
      • A coach may invest according to ability, but a good father remains concerned with the child as a person whether that child starts or sits on the bench.

    6. 29:41 Discipline is formation, not an angry score-settling

      Scripture: Hebrews 12:10–11

      • Josh rejects the reflex that hears ‘discipline’ as an angry father repaying wrongdoing. Hebrews says God disciplines for good, shared holiness, righteousness, and peace.
      • God is not settling a score with his children; he is shaping his home.

    7. 30:13 Nolan’s request for grace illustrates loving consequences

      • If Nolan lies, ignoring it is not loving. Josh has taught his son to answer that discipline comes ‘because you love me,’ though Nolan sometimes asks his pastor-father, ‘Can I have grace?’
      • A parent who avoids discipline merely to avoid tears abandons the child to sinful impulses and lets those impulses rule the home.
      • Loving parents agree on consequences not because they enjoy pain but because wisdom sees where unchecked sin leads. A loving father may create limited pain to prevent destruction.

    8. 31:32 The heavenly Father never mishandles the rod

      • Josh says Noel and Madison can testify that frustration, limited time, reluctance, emotion, and pride make his discipline imperfect.
      • God never loses control or disciplines in a temper. Every hardship he permits is measured by infinite wisdom, so hardship is not evidence that God has stopped loving his child.
      • Satan wants sufferers to interpret pain as abandonment; Josh tells them not to buy that lie.

    9. 32:49 Punishment is justice already satisfied for the believer

      Scripture: Ephesians 2:1, 5 1 Peter 2:24 Isaiah 53:5 Romans 8:1 Romans 5:1

      • Josh defines punishment as justice rendered. God does not overlook sin, but for those in Christ the required justice fell on Jesus rather than on them.
      • He layers apostolic and prophetic claims: people dead in sin are made alive with Christ; Jesus bore sins and drank the cup of God’s wrath to the last drop so believers could receive blessing; Isaiah says the punishment bringing peace fell on him; Paul says there is now no condemnation; and believers have peace with God through Christ.
      • Hardship is therefore not God getting even. Jesus paid it all. God may be bringing a wandering child back, but he is not making Christ’s finished payment again.

    10. 33:58 Consequences are natural results, and sin can have victims

      Scripture: Genesis 50:20

      • A bicycle-race pileup pictures the reach of one person’s failure: sin leaves scars and can injure many people.
      • Josh names ordinary consequences rather than divine payback: lies destroy trust, adultery destroys marriages, uncontrolled anger alienates a spouse, sex can produce pregnancy, work idolatry estranges children, and laziness can cost a job.
      • Even consequences can become tools in the Father’s hands; Josh adapts Joseph’s words about intended evil to say that God turns attempted destruction toward sanctification.

    11. 35:31 Discipline feels like weakness while God constructs strength

      • Weight training feels progressively weaker while muscle is being torn, yet the apparent destruction is construction. Josh uses this to explain formative suffering.
      • God tears down self-strength to rebuild dependence on his strength, exposes idols to become the believer’s treasure, and removes false confidence so confidence can rest in him.

    12. 36:26 Do not despise the discipline that ends in holiness

      Scripture: Proverbs 3:11; Hebrews 12:5

      • Josh returns to the command not to despise the Lord’s discipline. God is no longer the Christian’s enemy; even painful discipline expresses adoption and love.
      • The immediate experience may not be pleasant, but the promised result is holiness.

  5. 36:49 Strengthen one another and refuse grace-starving roots

    • The chapter returns to team language: restore weak runners, pursue both peace and holiness, watch over one another, uproot bitterness, and learn from Esau not to exchange eternal inheritance for immediate relief.

    1. 36:49 Strengthen weak limbs and level the path toward healing

      Scripture: Hebrews 12:12–13 Isaiah 35:3; Proverbs 4:26

      • Josh reads the charge to strengthen feeble arms and weak knees and to make level paths so the lame are healed rather than disabled.
      • He hears a team huddle and identifies Isaiah and Solomon behind the coaching language. The church should help weak runners regain the track, fix their eyes on Jesus, and keep moving because God is not finished with them.

    2. 37:18 Pursue peace and holiness while guarding the whole team

      Scripture: Hebrews 12:14–17

      • Josh reads Hebrews 12:14–17: make every effort toward peace and holiness, see that no one falls short of grace, prevent a bitter root from defiling many, and do not imitate sexually immoral, godless Esau.
      • Esau traded his inheritance for one meal and later could not undo the act even with tears.

    3. 37:51 Peace and holiness must remain together

      • Running together requires coordination. The writer does not promise conflict-free life but commands an active pursuit of peace without compromise in holiness.
      • Josh insists that peace severed from holiness and holiness severed from peace both miss the team’s goal.

    4. 38:23 Grace does not fail, but people fail to depend on it

      Scripture: Hebrews 12:15

      • ‘See to it’ makes every believer responsible to notice the condition of others. God’s grace itself never fails, but people can run toward self-made solutions rather than depend on it.
      • Bitterness starts invisibly like a root underground, then produces bitter speech, broken relationships, and division throughout a church.

    5. 38:57 Replace bitterness with Christ-shaped kindness and forgiveness

      Scripture: Ephesians 4:31–32

      • Josh quotes Paul’s command to remove bitterness, rage, anger, brawling, slander, and malice and instead become kind, compassionate, and forgiving.
      • The pattern and power for forgiving one another is God’s forgiveness in Christ.

    6. 39:09 Esau traded inheritance for a moment’s relief

      Scripture: Genesis 25:29–34

      • Josh directs hearers to Genesis 25 and stresses that Esau did not plan a grand renunciation; he was simply hungry and preferred immediate relief to lasting blessing.
      • The warning becomes personal: do not trade tomorrow’s inheritance for today’s bowl of soup.

    7. 39:30 Temptation waits for hunger, fatigue, loneliness, and imbalance

      Scripture: Matthew 4:1–4; Luke 4:1–4

      • Josh asks when Satan tempted Jesus and answers: when Jesus was hungry. The enemy’s playbook still waits for tiredness, loneliness, anger, discouragement, hurt, and imbalance.
      • In that exposed moment temptation whispers, ‘Just this once’ and ‘You do you.’ The eternal cost makes immediate relief a terrible bargain.

  6. 40:05 From terrifying Sinai to joyful Zion and an unshakeable kingdom

    • After a brief prayer, Josh reads the chapter’s climax, contrasts the untouchable mountain of law with heavenly Zion reached through Jesus’ better blood, applies the promised shaking to every rival kingdom, and calls secure citizens to thankful, reverent worship and endurance.

    1. 40:05 Josh prays for eyes, ears, and hearts before the chapter’s climax

      • Moved by the closing passage, Josh pauses to thank the Father for redemption accomplished through Jesus for trusting sinners.
      • He asks God to help people see, hear, absorb, and be changed by what God is doing in the world.

    2. 40:44 Hebrews contrasts two mountains and warns hearers not to refuse God

      Scripture: Hebrews 12:18–29

      • Josh reads Hebrews 12:18–29. Sinai burns with fire, darkness, storm, trumpet, unbearable speech, threatened death, and Moses trembling in fear.
      • Believers instead come to Mount Zion, the living God’s heavenly city, joyful angels, the church of the firstborn, God the Judge, perfected righteous spirits, Jesus the new-covenant mediator, and sprinkled blood speaking better than Abel’s.
      • The passage warns against refusing the heavenly speaker, promises another shaking that removes created things, and commands thankful, reverent worship because the received kingdom cannot be shaken and God is a consuming fire.

    3. 42:48 Jesus takes his people from a mountain of fear to a mountain of joy

      Scripture: Hebrews 12:18–21

      • Sinai declared God’s holiness while sinners remained at a distance before untouchable fire, darkness, thunder, and judgment.
      • Because of Jesus, believers have come not to Sinai but to Zion, the heavenly Jerusalem, and an assembly of innumerable angels rejoicing before God.

    4. 43:24 Christ’s blood speaks a better word than Abel’s

      Scripture: Hebrews 12:24

      • Josh says Abel’s blood cried out for justice, while Christ’s blood cried out, ‘Paid in full.’
      • The contrast makes Zion possible: Jesus’ covenant blood answers guilt with completed redemption rather than leaving sinners at a distance.

    5. 43:35 Zion is the finish line, and every temporary rival will shake

      Scripture: Hebrews 12:26–27

      • The race ends neither in the grave, defeat, nor exhaustion but in God’s presence. Zion is home.
      • Until then, the world will shake. Every human kingdom, economy, government, idol, and throne competing with Christ will collapse.
      • God removes what is temporary until his eternal Son, gospel, church, and kingdom remain.

    6. 44:31 Christians run from security, not uncertainty

      Scripture: Hebrews 12:22–24, 28 Hebrews 12:2 Hebrews 12:10, 28–29

      • Believers are not running in the hope that home might exist. The pioneer has crossed the line, and the perfecter is bringing every purchased saint home without losing one.
      • Every promise will stand. Because the kingdom is already received and cannot be shaken, the fitting response is to fix the eyes on Jesus and run.

    7. 45:01 The consuming fire fell on the Son and now prepares his people

      • Josh returns to ‘our God is a consuming fire.’ God’s holiness did not diminish at Zion; judgment fell on Christ, who stood where sinners deserved to stand.
      • The holiness that would have consumed believers now prepares them. One day every rival will be consumed, every enemy put beneath Christ’s feet, every tear wiped away, and every knee and tongue acknowledge Jesus as Lord.

    8. 45:48 Run like the kingdom is actually unshakeable

      Scripture: Hebrews 12:28 Hebrews 12:2 Hebrews 12:22, 28–29

      • Josh tells believers that receiving an unshakeable kingdom should change their manner of life: fix the eyes on Jesus, rejoice in Zion, run with confidence, and worship together.
      • Reverence and joy meet in the confession that God is a consuming fire.

  7. 46:08 Closing prayer: make the church’s walk an act of worship

    • Josh asks for worship deeper than singing: purified hearts, a fixed gaze, perfected faith, humility, surrendered lives, and one church moving together in God’s will.

    1. 46:08 Purify, cleanse, refocus, and use us

      Scripture: Matthew 6:10

      • Josh wants to worship with his voice until he is out of breath, but he also wants his walk to bring God praise.
      • He asks for help so the whole church can move together: purify hearts, cleanse sin, take eyes off self and circumstances, and fix them on God.
      • The prayer asks Jesus to perfect faith, remove prideful obstruction, restore first-hearing humility, receive every surrendered life, and use the church as he sees fit.
      • Josh closes with Jesus’ prayer, ‘Your will be done on earth as it is in heaven,’ and says, ‘We love you. Amen.’
