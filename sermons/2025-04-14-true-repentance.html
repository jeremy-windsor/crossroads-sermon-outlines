Overview

  1. 00:11 Ezra 7–10: three calls

    Scripture: Ezra 7–10

    • Steve is blessed to bring God’s word as the church wraps up its five-week series through Ezra, and his task is to cover chapters 7 through 10. He lays out three major themes, actions God calls Israel and its leaders to that extend to the church today: a call to stay close and be ready, a call to reclaim and witness, and a call to confess and repent.

  2. 01:07 The firstborn and the perfume bottle

    • Steve is the firstborn, and firstborns had a window with their parents’ full attention until something terrible happens: a sibling arrives, and the glorious throne must be shared, with fewer gifts, fewer photos, and more responsibility.
    • Researchers suggest explicit memories form around three or four, but one of Steve’s clearest came at two, when his baby sister arrived. It is also the first time he knew he had sinned. While his mom’s attention was on the new baby, he snuck into his parents’ bedroom, opened her Estée Lauder perfume, and jumped on the bare mattress with the bottle upside down. Maybe he wanted his mom to enjoy the scent forever; more likely it was vengeance for sharing the spotlight. She says the mattress was never the same.
    • When the last drop left the bottle, he knew he had done something very wrong, and his solution was the same as mankind’s since the Garden of Eden: he covered it and hid. He fetched a stack of paper plates and covered the whole bed. He did get his mom’s attention, just not the kind he or his backside wanted.
    • Steve thought of that first memory of sin while studying the last half of Ezra, where the people’s sin is exposed. Like Israel, through failure and forgetting what is important, we become exiles trying to get back to God, with much worse than spilled perfume and paper plates. Our capacity for sin can take us fast and far from God’s presence, but the good news is that redemption is more than possible through Jesus Christ, and God will reclaim and redeem his people throughout the end of Ezra.

  3. 04:47 First call: stay close and be ready

    Scripture: Ezra 7:1

    • Chapter 7 begins “after these things.” Steve recaps the series: Cyrus decreed the Israelites could return and rebuild, fulfilling God’s prophecy; Zerubbabel led the first group, who laid the foundation, faced opposition that halted construction, and eventually completed and dedicated the temple with joyful sacrifices, restoring worship in Jerusalem. That is where chapter 6 ended.

    1. 05:43 Ezra the scribe

      Scripture: Ezra 7:6 John 5:39–40 Ezra 7:10 Ezra 7:6

      • Up to now Ezra, the man believed to have written the book, hasn’t appeared, but he takes the lead from here. His lineage shows he is a Levite, a priest, and verse 6 calls him a scribe or teacher well-versed in the law of Moses.
      • We may picture a scribe meticulously copying texts, and that was part of it. By Jesus’ time the scribes, closely associated with the Pharisees, had largely shifted from devout, reliable teachers to religious lawyers, and Jesus called them out, as in John 5:39–40: you search the Scriptures because you think in them you have eternal life, yet you refuse to come to me.
      • Ezra is unlike the scribes who followed centuries later. Verse 10 says he devoted himself to the study and observance of the law and to teaching it in Israel. He doesn’t let zeal for God’s law consume him into self-righteousness; that is where the line is. He uses his knowledge to teach, his heart is for God and his people, and in many ways he holds a pastoral role rarely seen in the Old Testament.
      • Verse 6 says Ezra came up from Babylon, the king granted him everything he asked, and the hand of the Lord his God was on him. Growing up among the exiles under Persian rule, unable to serve in temple worship because there was no temple, Ezra devoted himself to seeing God’s word kept and taught to those in exile. Out of that devotion God calls him to lead the next group back. The first group rebuilt the temple physically; Ezra must rebuild God’s people spiritually.

    2. 08:26 A 58-year gap, and Esther

      Scripture: Ezra 7:7 Esther 4:14

      • A little extra study reveals something about the timeline. Verse 7 places this in the seventh year of King Artaxerxes, son of Xerxes I, better known as King Ahasuerus. That matters for two reasons. First, about 58 years pass between Ezra 6 and Ezra 7, easy for the casual reader to miss.
      • Second, and Steve loves this, the story of Esther happens inside that gap: on a timeline, Esther cuts Ezra in half. Mordecai stood against the evil Haman, who sought to wipe out the Jews across the Persian Empire, and when Esther hesitated to use her access to the king, he replied in Esther 4:14: if you remain silent, relief will arise from another place, “and who knows but that you have come to your royal position for such a time as this.”
      • Esther’s faith and boldness did more than save her people in one moment. The reclaiming God does through Ezra and Nehemiah is only possible because of her “such a time as this,” and both men benefited, still having access and favor with Persian royalty years later.

    3. 10:46 Comfortable in Babylon

      Scripture: Ezra 8 Ezra 7:8–9

      • Some estimate the Persian Empire was about 20% Jewish by then. That meant representation, but also a problem: syncretism, the blending of different religious beliefs and practices, a common phenomenon throughout history, had set in.
      • Far from oppressed captives, the Israelites were doing quite well in Persian-ruled Babylon. That is why only about 42,000 went with Zerubbabel and fewer than 2,000 go with Ezra. Life is good, with homes, families, and businesses; why go back? Nevertheless, Ezra gathers a willing band, whose names are given in chapter 8. Chapter 7, verses 8–9, says he left Babylon on the first day of the first month and arrived in Jerusalem on the first day of the fifth, “for the gracious hand of his God was on him.”
      • Steve loves how Ezra constantly credits God’s gracious hand: a humble follower of God, a keeper of God’s law, and a leader of God’s people.

    4. 12:15 Stay close to him and be ready for his next

      Scripture: Psalm 119:9–11

      • The takeaway: God wants you to stay close to him no matter the season and be ready for his next in your life. Imagine the impact on a scribe of copying the Torah word by word; if young people today copied the whole Bible, it would take a long time but be very impactful. We have God’s full, living, active word at our fingertips 24/7. Do we treat it as such?
      • David declares in Psalm 119: how can a young person stay pure? By obeying your word; I have hidden your word in my heart that I might not sin against you. Ezra was devoted to God’s word, reading, studying, memorizing, and teaching it, and we have the privilege to do the same. We can, and we must.
      • Ezra held an important government job but never lost sight of his true role as God’s servant. God’s word kept him close to God in exile, amid Babylon’s distractions and a culture urging him to settle for less than God’s purposes. Though he wasn’t in a traditional priestly role, he held on tight to God and was prepared for what was next.

    5. 13:54 From technical writing to teaching

      • Steve’s college degree, a BS in multimedia writing and technical communication, writing instruction manuals and the like, seems to have nothing to do with his work today. He knew he would never get a job with IKEA, whose instructions are wordless pictures, and the jobs he could get weren’t ones he wanted for life, so he switched to teaching, which he had always loved, and honed those skills for 15 years.
      • Today he leads and teaches the youth and writes about 3,000 words of messages a week to bring them God’s truth. God took seemingly obscure parts of his education and work and wove them into something for his kingdom.
      • To younger people: don’t get hung up on the what, why, or how; follow God’s leading, stay close, and be ready for his next, in season and out of season. To those not so young: reflect on how God used a mishmash of experiences to bring you where he has you, with the people he has put in your life. Staying close to God and growing as a disciple should be a thread easily traced through your story, constant wherever you work, learn, or live, and Ezra exemplifies that.

  4. 15:50 Second call: reclaim and witness

    Scripture: Ezra 7:15–17 Ezra 7:20 Exodus 12:35–36

    • Section two is a call to reclaim, the series title, and to witness. The rest of chapter 7 records Artaxerxes’ letter. Clearly a generous king, he lets any Israelites who choose go with Ezra, and in verse 15 sends silver and gold from the king and his advisers, plus whatever Ezra can obtain in Babylon and the people’s freewill offerings, to buy bulls, rams, and lambs for sacrifice. In verse 20 he adds that anything else needed for the temple may come from the royal treasury.
    • So Ezra could take up an offering from Jews and non-Jews in Babylon. God can use anything and everything for his glory, even a pagan people’s donations. It mirrors Israel leaving Egypt under Moses: in Exodus 12 the Israelites asked the Egyptians for silver, gold, and clothing, the Lord made the Egyptians favorably disposed, and so they plundered the Egyptians.

    1. 17:32 The rummage sale

      • Steve thought about this during the church’s rummage sale the past two weekends. “Don’t worry, we did not plunder anyone,” but the third annual sale set a fundraising record. He thanks the 24 adults who served, the 33 youth headed to camp who worked a total of 314 hours, and everyone who donated their “junk,” he corrects, “priceless treasures.”
      • Watching shoppers each morning, he thought about how God reclaims things in a similar, though better, way. Unwanted items found new homes and became funds to send students to summer camp, some who couldn’t otherwise afford it and some away from home for the first time, to hear about God’s loving kindness in Jesus. The team prayed before the doors opened each morning for every person they could show the love of Jesus to, and people who may not know Christ, or even oppose God, made it financially possible for others to hear about him.
      • Our God is in the business of reclaiming, redeeming, and restoring anything and everything. He gives hope to the hopeless, elevates the lowly, and turns junk into jewels, and he has done the same for Ezra and this trip.

    2. 19:34 Ezra’s praise

      Scripture: Ezra 7:27–28

      • Ezra closes with praise in verse 27: praise be to the Lord, who put it into the king’s heart to honor the house of the Lord and extended his good favor to me; because the hand of the Lord my God was on me, I took courage and gathered leaders to go up with me.

  5. 19:59 Trusting God without hurting his witness

    Scripture: Ezra 8:21–23

    • Ezra also needed to trust God and not hurt his witness. In chapter 8, verse 21, he proclaims a fast at the Ahava Canal to ask God for a safe journey, because he was ashamed to ask the king for soldiers and horsemen after telling him, “The gracious hand of our God is on everyone who looks to him.” So they fasted and petitioned God, and he answered.
    • Having boldly declared how great his God is, reality sinks in: Ezra may be in over his head, moving a huge group on a four-month journey through territory known for danger and bandits.

    1. 20:55 A night in Miami

      • In 2011 the church sent Steve and his wife on a mission trip to Haiti, carrying several thousand dollars in cash for the missionaries at an orphanage in Port-au-Prince, since you can’t just go to a bank there, along with luggage full of projectors and laptops.
      • On a layover at a not-so-great Miami hotel, their door flew open in the middle of the night; light shone in, and Steve saw a shadow flee and slam the door. It still gives him chills: in an unknown city, with all that cash, someone had access to their room. They had never prayed more for their safety. The enemy was working overtime with fear and doubt before they had even left the States. Every piece of furniture went against the door, they tried to sleep counting on God, and of course he protected them and the funds reached Haiti.

    2. 22:23 Others are watching

      Scripture: Psalm 27:1 Ezra 8:31–32

      • Ezra, too, is afraid, knowing that asking the king for help would call into question the trust in God he had so boldly declared, so he calls for fasting and prayer. That should challenge Christians living out their faith before a watching, wondering, often critical world. Do you show a secure confidence in Christ when things get hard? Your response to every circumstance affects your witness to friends and family. Parents, do your children see you tackle challenges with prayer and faith, or with stress and anxiety and “we’re going to get through this on our own strength”?
      • Ezra’s trepidation was justified: about 2,000 travelers carrying silver and gold worth several million dollars today. Perhaps he recited a psalm he knew well: the Lord is my light and my salvation; whom shall I fear?
      • Others are watching, and you can witness to the supernatural hope and confidence found only in Jesus. Ezra would have been ashamed to show a lack of faith by asking the king for help, but we show our faith by acknowledging that God is our help when things get messy and hard, our strength on the mountains and in the valleys. Ezra closes with the good report: they set out on the twelfth day of the first month, the hand of God was on them, he protected them from enemies and bandits, and they arrived in Jerusalem and rested three days.

  6. 24:19 Third call: confess and repent

    Scripture: Ezra 9:1–2

    • Chapter 8 would be an excellent place to stop: the second group is back, the temple has priests, the people know how to follow God, they offer sacrifices, deliver the king’s orders, and have paperwork granting access to every supply. Great place to end. “Let’s pray. We’re done. Not so fast.”
    • Ezra may have felt the joy and godly pride of getting his people home, but he is quickly hit with a huge problem. In chapter 9, verses 1–2, the leaders report that the people of Israel, including priests and Levites, have not kept themselves separate from the neighboring peoples and their detestable practices, have taken their daughters as wives, and mingled the holy race with them, with the leaders and officials leading the way.

    1. 25:44 Why it happened, and why it mattered

      Scripture: Deuteronomy 7:2–3

      • This was no small issue; Ezra knew it violated God’s covenant. The cause is apparent, even somewhat understandable: Zerubbabel’s large first wave was mostly young men, since it was a hostile place with heavy work, so men outnumbered women, and by Ezra’s arrival many had sought wives outside God’s covenant community.
      • It was still a clear breaking of God’s law. Steve reads Deuteronomy 7, which Ezra would have had memorized: make no treaty with them, show them no mercy, do not intermarry with them.
      • This is not racism at work; the issue is religious and cultural identity. The Jewish people were keepers of the line of promise going back to Abraham, and Ezra saw intermarriage as a threat to the lineage through which the promised Messiah would come and to Israel’s identity as God’s holy, set-apart nation. A foreign mother holding on to pagan practices would likely teach them to her children. As William Ross Wallace is famously quoted, “The hand that rocks the cradle rules the world.” The syncretism Ezra was trying to remove the people from in Persia had already gone ahead of him.

    2. 27:50 Ezra sits down appalled

      Scripture: Ezra 9:3–4

      • In verse 3 Ezra tears his tunic and cloak, pulls hair from his head and beard, and sits down appalled until the evening sacrifice, while everyone who trembled at God’s words gathers around him. The Hebrew word can mean astonished or horrified; he is in complete shock.
      • Grief was very public in Jewish culture; recall the mourners gathered where Jesus raised the dead. Ezra’s visible outrage shows both his sorrow and his anger toward the people’s sin, in front of the people who had sinned.

    3. 28:38 Ezra’s prayer

      Scripture: Ezra 9:6–15

      • Steve highlights Ezra’s priestly prayer, prayed aloud before a huge crowd, almost preaching truth and conviction to the people while pouring out his heart to God. From verse 6: I am too ashamed and disgraced to lift up my face to you, because our sins are higher than our heads and our guilt reaches the heavens; yet for a brief moment you have been gracious in leaving us a remnant; we have forsaken your commands; you have punished us less than our sins deserve; you are righteous, and here we are before you in our guilt.
      • Ezra humbly identifies the significance of their sin, the graciousness of their God, and why their sin is sin, and then sets up the possibility of forgiveness.

    4. 30:09 “We,” “us,” and “our”

      Scripture: Ephesians 4:15–16 Isaiah 6 Isaiah 6:5

      • Notice how foreign this is to modern thinking. Ezra hadn’t taken a foreign wife or personally broken the law, yet he repeatedly prays “we,” “us,” and “our.” The Western church mindset says your problems and your sin are your business, but God wants the church to mourn, grieve, and intercede for one another for the health of the whole. As Paul argued, a broken foot tells the whole body it isn’t running a marathon today; the body of Christ must function as a cohesive unit. In Ephesians 4, speaking the truth in love, the whole body, joined by every supporting ligament, grows and builds itself up in love as each part does its work.
      • The youth group just finished a series called Holy Forever in Isaiah 6. Like Ezra, Isaiah trembles before the perfectly holy God and cries in verse 5, “Woe to me! I am ruined! For I am a man of unclean lips, and I live among a people of unclean lips.” This holy model of a man sees he is as filthy as the people around him; God’s holiness exposes all shades of sin equally as sin. So Ezra identifies with the people’s sin, understanding that the sin of the few is enough in God’s eyes to contaminate the many.

  7. 32:19 The people respond

    Scripture: Ezra 10:2–4 Ezra 10:10–13

    • Ezra’s out-loud prayer and sermon works. In chapter 10, verse 2, Shecaniah says, we have been unfaithful to our God by marrying foreign women, but in spite of this there is still hope for Israel; let us make a covenant to send away these women and their children according to the law. Rise up; we will support you; take courage and do it.
    • Ezra puts everyone under oath, steps away to fast and mourn, and three days later gathers the people in the square: you have been unfaithful; honor the Lord and separate yourselves from the peoples around you and from your foreign wives. “Ouch.” The whole assembly answers loudly: you are right, we must do as you say, but there are many people and it is the rainy season, and this can’t be handled in a day or two.
    • Steve calls the scene amazing: men standing in freezing winter rain in Jerusalem, acknowledging their sin and agreeing to separate from their pagan wives. It took selected family heads two months to investigate every case of intermarriage one by one.

    1. 34:12 A hard passage, and mercy in the process

      • This is a difficult section. Some read it and think it harsh toward the women and children sent away from husbands and fathers. But there is good news in the two-month process: Ezra could have ordered everyone to divorce their wives immediately, but instead he allowed each case to be investigated. Why?
      • Read the genealogy of Jesus: is every woman a pure Israelite? No: Tamar, Rahab, Ruth, and Bathsheba. So it might not be a big deal in every case, which is why they needed a process, and God’s mercy comes out here. Old Testament law permitted marriage to a non-Jew on one condition, and Moses had a foreign wife: she renounced her old religion and became a proselyte, fully devoted, with her ties to paganism and her family severed.
      • One man might prove his wife has converted, worships Yahweh alone beside him, and submits to God’s law while teaching their children: she can stay. The next might say she wants the children exposed to all kinds of thinking so they can decide for themselves, and her parents bring a few of their idols when they visit, no big deal: she must go.
      • Old Testament law also required a divorced wife to be cared for financially; they were well supplied, free to remarry, and not thrown to the wolves. Each man is listed by name in Scripture to show he had put away his wife, some with children, and been restored to proper standing before God.

    2. 36:38 Descriptive, not prescriptive

      Scripture: Ezra 9–10 Proverbs 28:13

      • Before applying this to your life or to people you know with unsaved spouses, remember this is a descriptive passage: it tells what happened and why it mattered for this group in Jerusalem at this time under these circumstances. It is not a prescriptive passage modeling something the church must apply today.
      • What Ezra 9 and 10 do show is the broader prescriptive call on God’s people to confess and repent. Proverbs 28:13: whoever conceals their sin does not prosper, but the one who confesses and renounces them finds mercy. God intends for us not to cover or conceal our sin but to confess it and turn from it; that is the only way to find the mercy and grace he is waiting to pour out when we drag our sin into the open.

    3. 37:46 Confession and repentance are not the same

      Scripture: Ezra 9–10

      • The two words are easily confused. Both acknowledge wrongdoing, but confession is admitting the mistake, while repentance is the deeper act of turning from the sin and changing your heart and actions. They need to go together.
      • In Ezra 9 and 10 the people confessed first, but they didn’t stop at “my bad, God” and carry on as normal, or “it’s too late now.” The next step was radical repentance: they turned from their sin by sending away pagan wives unwilling to submit to God’s law.
      • You can confess without repenting, admitting your sin and going right back to it, and that is a dangerous place to live. Repentance declares your intention to turn away in the power of the Holy Spirit and follow through: “God, I don’t want to do this anymore. Empower me to say no.” It requires action, like cutting off access to a habit, ending a relationship, or finding someone to keep you accountable. It isn’t fun or easy; it requires cutting out, tearing away, and sending away, as it did for these men.

    4. 39:15 Only possible because of Jesus

      Scripture: 2 Corinthians 5:21 1 Timothy 2:5–6

      • Confession and repentance are only possible because of Jesus Christ, whose sacrifice lets God declare us not guilty. Jesus became sin for us so that in him we might become the righteousness of God. Because of his death the penalty for your sins is over, and you have direct access to God through Jesus. You don’t need weekly confession with a priest; man has no power to forgive sin against God. As 1 Timothy 2 says, there is one God and one mediator between God and mankind, the man Christ Jesus, who gave himself as a ransom for all people.

  8. 40:01 Two questions

    • Steve asks everyone to answer two questions honestly: What area of my life has sin that has gone unconfessed? Where have I stopped short of full repentance, full turning away, full healing and forgiveness?
    • That sin keeps you disconnected from God. Like mold in a dark, warm closet, it grows and spreads; like an untreated wound, it festers and reeks, and not even the best perfume or paper plates will cover it. It isn’t worth holding on to at the cost of freedom in God’s presence; drag it into the light, cut it out, and let the healing in.
    • In summary, God calls us to divorce ourselves from the things that would consume us and damage our relationship with him. We can’t walk next to those things and pursue Christ at the same time. What are those things for you?

    1. 41:06 Psalm 32

      Scripture: Psalm 32:1–5

      • King David, the man after God’s own heart, knew sin and its consequences well, and Steve often turns to his psalms when he feels the weight of sin. He closes with Psalm 32:1–5: blessed is the one whose transgression is forgiven, whose sin is covered; when I kept silent my bones wasted away; but I acknowledged my sin to you and did not cover my iniquity, and you forgave the iniquity of my sin.

  9. 42:06 Closing prayer

    Scripture: Hosea 6:1 Psalm 103:12

    • Steve prays that as the church sees God’s call in Ezra to be holy and set apart, a call they struggle with, they would remember the forgiveness found in Jesus Christ. Echoing Hosea, “Come, let us return to the Lord,” he prays that would be true for many, if not all, that morning, and that God would richly pour out his mercy and grace when they acknowledge their sin, confess, and repent.
    • God says he will cast our sin as far as the east is from the west; just as Ezra recorded the people’s sin reaching from east to west, the Lord will do the opposite for us. He is in the business of reclaiming and restoring.
