Overview

  1. 00:15 A pastor’s love, church hurt, and the costly work of reconciliation

    Scripture: John 17:20–23 Ephesians 4:3 James 3:2 Matthew 18:15–17

    • Josh opens after the hailstorm with a personal declaration of committed love for the church—not infatuation, but a durable willingness to pray, remain available, and walk with Jesus together even when ministry is difficult. His stated ministry desire is simple: help the congregation know who God is, how God sees them, what God has done for them, and how grace should shape what they do for others. Because pastoral teaching sometimes requires confronting sin, he publicly apologizes for any hurt he has caused, even when harm was not his intention.
    • He recounts the church wound that shaped his youth. After his parents’ divorce, the church in which he had grown up did not visibly support him and his sisters. These were not casual acquaintances but people with whom he had worshiped, prayed, eaten, stayed, and celebrated his baptism. He let their failure produce bitterness, rebellion against God and the church, and more than a decade of sinful choices. His concise diagnosis is that he let their sin create sin in him.
    • Josh then complicates his own account rather than merely blaming the former church. Those people may have thought he did not want contact; unspoken expectations kill relationships. People cannot always perceive an unexpressed need, so someone who needs care sometimes must raise a hand and ask. He asks the congregation to make that commitment, promising that he will be there when they do. Looking back, he believes that if he had asked for prayer he would have received love. Instead, he assumed the worst and treated his burden as uniquely heavy.
    • Becoming a pastor exposed the uneven expectations people place on church leaders and forced him to own his part. He contacted leaders from his childhood church, not first to receive their apology but to give his: he had imposed unspoken expectations, judged them by a stricter standard than himself, and carried a decade-long grudge. He trusted the Holy Spirit to convict them. They, in turn, admitted that they could and should have done more and apologized for not being present. Reconciliation occurred three years earlier. During his family’s present bereavement, that same church is mowing his mother’s lawn, providing food and facilities, and hosting the coming celebration of life.
    • Josh presents this repaired relationship as a picture of the church and of the unity for which Jesus prayed (John 17:20–23, 06:05). Unity is not the absence of every rupture; it is the shared determination to pursue restoration when unity is lost. Whether one is hurt or doing the hurting, both parties bear responsibility to pursue reconciliation. Pointing a finger while refusing that work disgraces the cross. He pleads with the church to learn from his error: do not harbor decades of resentment; instead, “make every effort to keep the unity of the Spirit through the bond of peace” (Ephesians 4:3, 06:47).
    • The transition from reconciliation to the sermon’s primary subject sharpens through two texts. James says that everyone stumbles in many ways (James 3:2, 07:01); therefore, believers should not answer another person’s stumbling by attacking character, kicking the person while down, destroying relationships, or halting the church’s progress. Josh and the elders recommit themselves to building a healthy church. He does not promise that no one will be hurt, but he does promise that restoration can be pursued biblically. He summarizes the process in Matthew 18:15–17 (07:40): go directly to the person; if necessary, take witnesses who seek unity rather than a factional victory; then involve church leadership; separate only after every effort has been exhausted.
    • Transition: At 08:15, Josh identifies the tongue as “the number one cause of all church hurt” and turns from the personal reconciliation frame to James 3.

  2. 08:15 Primary text: mature faith gains power over the tongue

    Scripture: James 1:2–4 James 2:14–26, with a verbal echo of James 1:22–25 James 3:1–12

    • Josh places the day’s text inside the series’ progression through James. James 1:2–4 is summarized as teaching that the mature Christian is patient in trials (08:20). Josh says “James chapter 2” teaches that mature Christians do not merely hear truth but practice it (08:26). The stated chapter points toward James’s enacted-faith argument in James 2:14–26, while the specific hear/do wording echoes James 1:22–25. James 3:1–12 teaches that mature Christians exercise power over the tongue (08:32), and Josh then reads the entire primary unit (08:44–09:59).
    • The reading moves in James’s own order. Teachers face stricter judgment; everyone stumbles, but faultless speech would indicate a person able to govern the whole body. A bit guides a horse and a rudder guides a ship: something small can direct something much larger. A spark can ignite a forest, and the tongue can corrupt a whole person and set the course of life on fire. Humanity can tame every sort of creature but cannot tame the restless, poisonous tongue. The same mouth praises God and curses people made in God’s likeness; James says that such contradiction is as unnatural as one spring producing fresh and salt water or a plant bearing fruit foreign to its nature.
    • Josh’s initial exposition names speech as a God-given instrument with two opposed uses. With it, believers praise, pray, preach Scripture, encourage one another, and point lost people to Jesus. The same instrument can lie, ruin a reputation, and break a heart. It can be a weapon of deliverance or destruction. Mature believers use this power wisely; immature believers use it selfishly.
    • Transition: At 11:07, Josh announces the three-part structure: the tongue has power to guide, to destroy, and to bless.

  3. 11:17 Sixteen thousand daily words: what story is your testimony writing?

    • Josh says the average person speaks about 16,000 words per day—the equivalent of a 64-page book—and turns the statistic into James’s implied question: What kind of story are your words writing?
    • A humorous contrast between internal and external processors makes the application personal. Some people need help stopping; others need encouragement to speak. He invokes Shrek’s complaint about Donkey, then has the congregation identify which style describes them. Couples often pair one internal with one external processor. Josh jokes that two external processors may need the church’s Bind marriage ministry.
    • He uses his marriage as the main illustration. Josh externally processes and appreciates that his wife listens well. When she speaks less, he can assume something is wrong; he must remember that she is processing without needing his volume of words. He briefly invites couples married four years or forty to the upcoming marriage-enrichment course.
    • Transition: A fifth of life is spent talking, and what a person does with that fifth matters. At 14:00, Josh begins the first major power: words guide.

  4. 14:05 The power to guide, part one: teachers must accept humility and stricter accountability

    Scripture: James 3:1 1 Timothy 3:1 James 3:1 2 Timothy 4:2

    • Josh repeats James 3:1 (14:05): not many should presume to teach because teachers are judged more strictly.
    • He reconstructs the church problem behind the warning. Believers were gathering in homes but using speech for gossip, lies, and slander rather than prayer and encouragement. Verse 1 implies that many wanted spiritual influence and the pulpit’s visibility.
    • James is not rejecting the gift or office of teaching. Paul calls church oversight a noble task (1 Timothy 3:1, 14:59). The warning is that gifting is insufficient by itself; leadership must be joined to humility, responsibility, and accountability. The would-be teachers wanted to stand and declare rather than deny themselves.
    • Josh applies the standard to himself and church governance. If his work is to teach biblical truth, his life must demonstrate a desire to obey what he teaches. Congregations often demand leader accountability while leaders themselves evade it. Although he does not make governance a salvation issue, he considers genuine eldership highly significant. He rejects both leaderless churches and token boards made of friendly insiders. Crossroads’ elders are described as godly men who humble him and hold him responsible so that he does not obstruct God’s work; he reciprocates that accountability.
    • He repeats the point of James 3:1 at 17:02: stricter judgment concerns obedience and integrity, not a separate route of salvation. Given a choice between an extraordinary communicator who does not obey and a poor communicator who first applies the Word to himself, Josh chooses the latter. 2 Timothy 4:2 (17:37) defines the teacher’s task: preach the Word, remain ready in and out of season, and correct, rebuke, and encourage with patience and careful instruction. A teacher who is unprepared or unwilling to examine himself should not hold the microphone.
    • Transition: Teachers are not the only people who sin. At 17:52, Josh widens James’s demand from leaders to every Christian.

  5. 17:58 The power to guide, part two: leash the tongue, surrender the helm, and train for holiness

    Scripture: James 3:2 Proverbs 15:1–2 Proverbs 12:22 Zephaniah 1:7 Proverbs 10:19 James 3:3–4 Proverbs 18:21 Psalm 141:3–4 Matthew 12:34–37

    • Josh repeats James 3:2 (17:58): everyone stumbles, and control of speech demonstrates capacity to keep the whole body in check. James includes himself; universal failure does not make failure acceptable. Everyone has weaknesses and blind spots and therefore needs other believers. Josh paraphrases the verse’s force: if a person can control “that big hole in your face,” the rest of the body can also come under control.
    • He argues that the mouth may be the believer’s foundational self-control problem. Addiction, pornography, anxiety, comparison, social-media envy, and substance abuse are real problems, but many remain undefeated because the person has never learned to govern speech. The church is “a hospital for sinners, not a holiday for saints”; wounded people are welcome if they desire healing, while self-satisfied people will resist correction.
    • Proverbs supplies concrete speech disciplines. Proverbs 15:1–2 (20:07): a gentle answer turns away wrath, whereas harshness stirs anger; wisdom handles knowledge rightly while folly gushes from the mouth. When accusations fly, do not retaliate—learn what is possible, speak life, and move on. Proverbs 12:22 (20:25): God detests lying lips and delights in trustworthy people. The standard is what delights God, not what wins human approval. Josh invokes Zephaniah 1:7 (20:39) to counsel silence before God rather than lying. Proverbs 10:19 (21:08): multiplying words does not end sin; prudence restrains the tongue. These practices make the tongue a training ground for resisting temptation. Learning to stop a lie can train a person to stop cheating, pornography, or destructive drinking.
    • The dog-leash illustration tests who is controlling whom. A strong man walking a Chihuahua depicts the desired mastery: the small animal is fully restrained. Josh’s wife being pulled by a Great Dane depicts many people’s relationship to speech: when the tongue bolts, the person goes wherever it leads. His diagnostic question is, “You got that tongue on a leash, or does it have you on a leash?”
    • Josh returns to James’s bit and rudder (James 3:3–4, 22:53). Both small instruments can guide something massive when properly controlled and can permit disaster when not. As bit and rudder resist beast, wind, and waves, Christians resist the old nature, flesh, and sinful desire. An impulse does not automatically deserve expression. Write it down, pray over it, and ask whether speaking would be beneficial or destructive.
    • Control also requires the right master. A bit is useless without a trained rider, and a rudder is useless without a captain. Many have assigned Jesus the role of deckhand while remaining in the captain’s chair. Josh calls them to step down and let the Master of creation lead. Some try to restrain speech without receiving a new heart; others have a new heart but refuse to ask the Lord for help.
    • Scripture ties life-giving speech to heart-level dependence. Proverbs 18:21 (24:45) says the tongue carries the power of life and death. David’s prayer in Psalm 141:3–4 (24:55) asks God to guard his mouth, watch the door of his lips, and keep his heart from evil. Jesus’ warning in Matthew 12:34–37 (25:17) says the mouth speaks from the heart’s overflow and that every empty word will face judgment; words reveal either acquittal or condemnation.
    • Transition: The tongue guides, so believers must guide people in the right direction. At 25:53, Josh turns to its power to destroy.

  6. 25:53 The power to destroy, part one: sparks, burn scars, rhetoric, and gracious testimony

    Scripture: James 3:5 Proverbs 26:20–21 Matthew 11:19 Matthew 12:24 Colossians 4:6

    • Josh repeats James 3:5 (25:55): the small, boastful tongue resembles a spark that can ignite a great forest.
    • The Great Chicago Fire becomes the opening scale illustration. Josh tells the traditional story of Mrs. O’Leary’s cow kicking over a lantern on October 8, 1871, followed by 100,000 people displaced, 17,500 buildings destroyed, 300 deaths, and $40 million in damage. His sermonic point is not the historical cause itself but the disproportion between a small ignition and enormous devastation: one match can burn a house, and one mouth can start relational fires.
    • He translates wildfire “burn scars” into relational damage. Some hearers have left scorched places in a marriage, workplace, or church through their words. Proverbs 26:20–21 (27:02) says a fire dies without wood and a quarrel without gossip; a quarrelsome person kindles strife as fuel feeds flame. Churches can be scarred by members and leaders who cannot govern speech. Yet removing persistent fire starters can allow a green shoot of unity and love to emerge; God can redeem a blackened field.
    • Hurtful words do not merely burn institutions; they wound people. Jesus himself was called a glutton, drunkard, and friend of tax collectors and sinners (Matthew 11:19, 27:57). Opponents attributed his miracles to Satan (Matthew 12:24, 28:10), and mockery continued at the cross. Josh’s point is that hostile tongues injured even the sinless Son of Man.
    • He broadens destructive rhetoric from relationships to societies. Hitler’s communication gathered a furious army under the language of patriotic duty; Josh uses this as an example of gifted rhetoric serving profound evil. He argues that dehumanizing language supports abortion by calling a baby a “clump of cells.” He similarly connects the endurance of American slavery to rhetoric that devalued and dehumanized people. The transition back to ordinary hearers is deliberate: they may not direct wars or systems, but everyone should examine whether their words have broken a heart, damaged a reputation, or turned someone away from the gospel.
    • “Your tongue is your testimony” becomes the corrective. Colossians 4:6 (30:26) calls every conversation to be full of grace and seasoned with salt so the believer knows how to answer each person. Josh defines gracious speech as spiritual, good, wholesome, appropriate, kind, sensitive, truthful, uplifting, gentle, purposeful, loving, and thoughtful. “Seasoned with salt” means preparing and praying rather than unloading unfiltered “verbal vomit.”
    • Transition: At 31:03, Josh returns to James’s animal and poison images to deepen the destructive-power warning.

  7. 31:03 The power to destroy, part two: an untamable animal, deadly poison, and a God-transformed tongue

    Scripture: James 3:7–8 James 3:6 Matthew 28:18–20

    • Josh repeats James 3:7–8 (31:03): people tame animals, birds, reptiles, and sea creatures, but no human being can tame the restless, poison-filled tongue.
    • A family visit to Out of Africa supplies the illustration. Zoo barriers can make wild animals look domesticated. Josh talked to a tiger through the fence until it stood against the barrier and revealed its true size and danger. Without the fence, Josh says, he would have been a snack. The tongue similarly pounces and kills when its nature is left unchecked.
    • Not every dangerous tongue appears large or overpowering. Some look small and harmless but carry enough relational toxin to bring down something much larger. In fifteen years of pastoral ministry, Josh has watched toxic speech harm individuals, families, life groups, and entire churches. His sharp application is that people who avoid a snake on a hiking trail may still converse with one in the church lobby. Believers should avoid people whose loose lips spread poison.
    • He pauses to inventory tongue-enabled sins. Against God: profanity, irreverence, and blasphemy. Against people: deceit, gossip, backbiting, division, verbal abuse, manipulation, and seduction. Additional patterns include boasting, bragging, comparison, complaining, and whining. Speech is the interface among thought, intention, and action, which is why so much sin travels through it.
    • Human inability is not God’s inability. Josh carries forward James 3:6 at 34:48: no one must remain a tongue set aflame by hell. God can instead ignite speech with a “heavenly blaze,” enabling believers to tell sinners about the Savior, carry light into darkness, and serve the Great Commission (Matthew 28:18–20, 34:59). Hate in the heart lets the tongue drag the person wherever it wants; love in the heart gives speech a divine purpose to speak life.
    • Transition: At 35:27, Josh announces James’s final reminder: the tongue also has power to bless.

  8. 35:27 The power to bless: cross-contamination reveals a heart problem

    Scripture: James 3:9–10 Romans 8:11 Matthew 15:18

    • Josh names the contradiction in James 3:9–10 (35:36) “cross-contamination.” Christians praise the Lord and Father and then curse people made in God’s likeness. James is speaking not merely to nominal Christians but to participants who attend worship, seek Scripture, and say they want to obey.
    • Josh stages the contradiction in a Sunday-morning sequence. Worshipers sing that God is worthy, shout amen and hallelujah, take the Lord’s Supper, and then argue in the lobby or curse family members on the drive home. His memorable line is that they have just eaten the Lord’s Supper and now want to “eat a believer.”
    • Cursing an image-bearer is an offense against the God whose image that person bears. A person cannot excuse domestic rage by saying at least it was not directed at God. Spouses, parents, and children who verbally shred one another attack someone marked by the Creator.
    • The hypocrisy is intensified by the believer’s claim to possess the Spirit who raised Jesus from the dead (Romans 8:11, 37:33). Someone comes seeking Christ’s fresh water but receives the Christian’s salt water instead. James’s spring image makes such output evidence of a source problem.
    • Josh locates the source in the heart, not in the tongue as an isolated organ. Matthew 15:18 (38:01) says what comes from the mouth comes from the heart and defiles the person. Persistent criticism, gossip, and character assassination cannot finally be blamed on the target; the occasion exposed wickedness already present in the speaker. “That’s not really me” fails as an excuse: the words had to be within the person before they could come out.
    • The proper prayer is therefore deeper than “help me control my tongue.” Believers must run to God with destructive speech and ask him to soften the heart. Josh relieves the intensity with a joke: a woman wants to put her tongue on the altar, and the pastor replies that the altar is not that big. He then returns to the leash image: for many people, life feels like a constant fight because their tongue drags them. Give God the heart, and he will help with the tongue.
    • Transition: At 39:51, Josh offers practical “ammunition”—sincere, life-giving phrases to replace habitual destruction.

  9. 39:51 Put the tongue to work: six practices of sincere blessing

    Scripture: James 3:5, 9–10

    • Josh warns that these phrases are not mechanical substitutions. They produce relational change only when meant sincerely and spoken from a transformed heart.
    • “I appreciate you” (40:19). People’s lives change when they know what they mean to someone. Related practices include “please,” “thank you,” and, especially in marriage, “I don’t know what I’d do without you.” Children should recognize that their parents’ anniversary is also a blessing to them and say so.
    • “I’m sorry” (40:51). Christians still need to apologize for their actions. Delaying an apology is like skipping a medical checkup: it may save time now but costs more later.
    • “I love you” (41:11). This belongs not only in homes and marriages but among the family of believers. If Christians do not sincerely love one another, something in the church requires repair.
    • “I’m praying for you” (41:26). The phrase is good only if the speaker actually prays rather than using a Christian blanket statement and forgetting the person. Private intercession places people in the Father’s care and makes public cursing less likely.
    • “I see God working in your life” (41:57). Saying this requires enough relational closeness to notice grace at work. Josh offers two related blessings: “Watching you grow in Christ has been a blessing to me” and “I see Christ in you.” Sincere recognition helps form unshakable bonds within the family of faith.
    • Josh recaps James 3:5, 9–10 at 42:20: the tongue is small and often troublesome, but among God’s people it should be a blessing.
    • Transition: Because the tongue insists on being busy, Josh urges the church to put it to work for the Lord. The leash image then leads directly into the gospel invitation.

  10. 42:48 Gospel invitation, baptismal testimony, and closing prayer

    Scripture: Matthew 16:24; Mark 8:34; Luke 9:23 Zephaniah 1:7 Genesis 9:8–17

    • Josh addresses anyone still being dragged by the tongue. The invitation is to confess sin, repent, and put faith in Jesus Christ. The gospel is not a to-do list but good news about what Christ has done. Grace received through faith gives new life and identity, and God begins a work that ends in eternal life.
    • The call is framed as decisive discipleship. Stop and drop the old direction, deny oneself, take up the cross, and follow Christ (Matthew 16:24; Mark 8:34; Luke 9:23, 43:10). Put away the past, release grudges, and walk with Christ. Josh repeats Zephaniah’s summons to silence before the Lord because his day is near (Zephaniah 1:7, 43:17).
    • The ready baptism waters turn transformed speech into public testimony. He invites hearers to use the tongue for its created purpose: testifying to God’s goodness.
    • Josh’s prayer asks God to use Scripture to produce not merely Christian-sounding speech but Christlike lives. He asks God to shape, mold, and use the congregation. The morning rainbow reminded him of God’s covenant never again to deal with earthly wickedness by a flood (Genesis 9:8–17, 44:09). He contrasts that old flood with the “flood of grace and mercy” made available to repentant sinners through the Savior’s blood. At the Communion table, he asks the church to remember promises already given and promises awaiting fulfillment. The prayer closes in longing for Christ’s return and presence, followed by “Amen” at 45:16.
