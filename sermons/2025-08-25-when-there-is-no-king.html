Overview

  1. 00:15 No king in Israel: a loving warning

    Scripture: Judges 21:25 Judges 19–21

    • “In those days, there was no king in Israel. Everyone did what was right in his own eyes.” Elijah says that single sentence is the lens through which we must approach the final pages of Judges: the sound of a society untethered from the Lord.
    • Before reading, he issues a loving warning. Judges 19–21 is one of the darkest, most disturbing passages in all of Scripture: graphic, violent, and heartbreaking. God preserved these events for our instruction, not to entertain our curiosity but to expose our hearts. This is not a boogeyman story to fear-monger anyone toward belief; it shows the inevitable trajectory of sin and rebellion when it goes unchecked.
    • He asks the church to lean in with a sober heart and tells parents to use discretion with children. He refuses to censor what God has preserved: men not just walking apart from God but declaring themselves God and reaping the fruit of a rotting tree of disobedience.

  2. 02:03 Lean in with a hopeful heart

    • He also asks them to take heart. These chapters display the grotesque collapse of Israel and mirror the future of any nation that turns its back on God, but they also point forward to the one who is king, who will never abandon his bride, never compromised his holiness, and never did what was right in his own eyes but lived fully for his Father’s will.
    • Even in the darkness of these closing chapters we can see Jesus Christ, our living hope and King of Kings, showing that even in the darkest night our God can still light the way.

  3. 02:55 Judges 19: a Levite and his concubine

    Scripture: Judges 19 Judges 19:1

    • Judges 19 opens not with a battle, a hero, or deliverance, but with the quiet introduction of a Levite and his concubine, and the story quickly unravels into unspeakable wickedness. This is Israel at her lowest, with no Canaanite influence to blame: rot from within among the people delivered from Egypt and given the law, the temple, and the priesthood.
    • Elijah reads the opening verse and pauses: “Red flag.” The Levites were the ritual caretakers of ancient Israel who served the tabernacle and later the temple, assisted the priests, led musical worship, taught God’s law, and served as judges. In other words, they were pastors. How would the church feel about him taking a concubine? They wouldn’t have time to feel anything before Michaela killed him.

    1. 05:00 God’s design for marriage

      Scripture: Genesis 2:24 Matthew 19:4–6

      • This is the Old Testament equivalent of a pastor failing to uphold a foundational reality of God’s created order. Genesis says a man leaves his father and mother, is united to his wife, and they become one flesh, and Jesus elaborates in Matthew: what God has joined together, let no one separate.
      • Somehow this has become controversial, but God’s design for marriage is one man, one woman, one flesh, one covenant, one life, and there is no other acceptable definition.
      • Calling her a concubine implies the Levite already has a wife; a concubine was basically a legally recognized mistress. He walks from sexual convenience, not covenant faithfulness. If even the pastors walk in unrepentant disobedience, all of Israel is already rotting. The story starts with compromise in the house of the Lord and only goes downhill.

  4. 06:50 Sin begets sin

    Scripture: Judges 19:2–4

    • Elijah reads on: she was unfaithful to him and went back to her father’s house in Bethlehem, and after four months he went to persuade her to return. One translation says she played the harlot against him. The Levite who had been unfaithful to God and to his wife now experiences unfaithfulness in a relationship founded on it.
    • Sin begets sin; it compounds. Rather than running from his sin into reconciliation with his bride, he heads out to chase his mistress.

    1. 07:52 Don’t expect God to bless your disobedience

      Scripture: Romans 6:23

      • Especially for the men, since this is their story, but for everyone: don’t expect God to bless your disobedience. God can and does use it, but don’t mistake that for God condoning it. She was wrong, but before that he had already broken covenant with God.
      • We demand his favor while trampling his commands, but God will not underwrite our disobedience. He often lets us taste the bitter fruit of our choices, not because he has abandoned us but because he is disciplining us. His discipline is not rejection; it is mercy shaking us awake to what Paul says in Romans: the wages of sin is death, but the gift of God is eternal life.
      • Some have had to learn that the hard way, Elijah included, walking in outward disobedience and expecting inward peace. God loves us too much to let us live comfortably in our sin. His discipline is an invitation to turn back before things unravel further, and no matter how far you’ve run, it isn’t too late. But believer, you must turn back.

    2. 09:58 Don’t presume on his kindness

      Scripture: Romans 2:4–5

      • God’s patience surpasses all understanding, but we walk a dangerous road when we presume on his kindness. Paul asks in Romans 2 whether we presume on the riches of his kindness, not knowing that God’s kindness is meant to lead us to repentance.
      • A life marked by consistent unrepentant sin is not a life marked by the Holy Ghost. We are saved by faith alone, but a faith that saves is never alone; it is always accompanied by life change and a desire to obey, and when we fall short we rest in the grace of Jesus Christ. This isn’t fear-mongering; it is the gospel and reality.

    3. 11:01 To the nonbeliever: the gods of this world demand more

      Scripture: Matthew 11:28–30

      • The most hateful thing Elijah could do is act like this isn’t their reality. To nonbelievers wishing they had stayed home: God put you here for a reason. Selfish ambition may grant temporary gratification, but it will destroy you in the long run.
      • The world says, “Follow your heart. You are enough. You do you.” If I could just have this, get this job, look like this, get this girl or that guy, then I’d be happy. You keep laboring in vain until it wears you down and makes you jaded and bitter. The gods of this world always demand more, but the one true God came down into our mess and said, “Come to me, all you who are weary and burdened, and I will give you rest.”
      • Christianity is not behavior modification or rule following. It is a life-changing encounter with the living God who loves us so much he went through the horrors of the cross, so that we would bring our sin into the light, kill it, and live for his glory. Grace is not a license to sin.

  5. 12:49 No such thing as a sin that doesn’t hurt anyone

    Scripture: Judges 19:22–26 Genesis 19

    • When disobedience compounds, sin doesn’t stay private; it spreads and poisons whole communities. There is no such thing as a sin that doesn’t hurt anyone: it offends you and your body, it offends God, and it hurts the people around you even if they don’t know about it.
    • The pair stops in Gibeah in Benjamin, where an old man brings them into his home, and that night wicked men surround the house. With a trigger warning, Elijah reads the account: the host offers his virgin daughter and the concubine, the Levite sends his concubine out, and they abuse her through the night until she falls at the door at daybreak.
    • This is Genesis 19, Sodom and Gomorrah, all over again, with a twist: these aren’t pagan outsiders but Israelites. God’s covenant people have become indistinguishable from the nations. The Levite treats the concubine he traveled so far to get back like bait thrown into a shark tank. Instead of her protector he becomes her betrayer, and he sacrifices her on the altar of self.

  6. 15:19 America has sown the same seed

    Scripture: Romans 1:28–32

    • Gibeah was no accident of history but the rotten harvest of a people who tore up God’s law and planted the seeds of everyone doing what is right in his own eyes. Before anyone shakes their head in disgust, America has sown the same seed and is choking on the same weeds.
    • We have made a mockery of marriage, redefined gender and God’s creation, paraded perversion in our streets, and sacrificed our children by the millions in the name of choice, and we call it progress. God calls it wickedness. Sexual anarchy is slavery to your basest desires, moral relativism is blindness, and we are going a hundred miles an hour toward a cliff. If God did not overlook Gibeah, he will not overlook us.
    • Judgment always falls on nations that spit in his face while demanding his blessing. Elijah reads Romans 1, where God gives people over to a depraved mind and they not only do these things but approve of those who practice them.

  7. 17:20 Judgment begins with the household of God

    • Here it gets uncomfortably close to home: judgment doesn’t begin with Washington, D.C.; it begins with the household of God. The world in rebellion is expected. The tragedy is when the church plays along or sits in the corner twiddling its thumbs because it doesn’t want to be mean.
    • When we wink at sexual sin, treat our marriage vows lightly, and entertain ourselves with the very filth Christ died to save us from, we prove that Gibeah isn’t just out there; it’s in here.

    1. 18:05 Progressive theology in the pulpit

      Scripture: Genesis 3:1

      • Sometimes it comes as progressive ideology and theology slithering into pulpits. The mainline Protestant denominations, once bulwarks of Christian orthodoxy, are now whitewashed tombs with beautiful buildings and rank heresy inside. Elijah asks the church to pray for discernment for those listening and for the repentance of those leading.
      • He refuses to call it any sort of Christianity: it is an entire theology built on the serpent’s question in Genesis 3, “Did God really say?” What kind of Christianity denies the sufficiency of Scripture, final judgment, the resurrection, the virgin birth, heaven and hell, and the deity of Christ? That is deconstructing into unbelief, not a truer form of following Christ.
      • Like the Levite, progressive spirituality seeks comfort and status over truth and obedience. Sin is not just excused but celebrated. Churches that refuse to stand up to it because they think the eleventh commandment is “thou shalt be nice” are just as guilty of compromise. Rather than conceding to the enemy, learn to fight for truth within our four walls.

    2. 20:26 Revival begins with repentance in the church

      • Many are praying for revival in the nation, and Elijah joins that prayer. But if revival is ever going to come, it must begin with repentance in the church before we dare speak of reforming the culture outside these walls.

    3. 20:47 Compromise in the conservative evangelical church

      • The sickness isn’t only in the culture or the liberal churches. Compromise has made its home in the American conservative evangelical church too, with some of the highest-profile pulpits exposed for covering abuse, hiding corruption, and excusing the sins they once preached against.
      1. 21:13 Robert Morris and Gateway Church
        • Taking no joy in it, Elijah describes Robert Morris, former lead pastor of Gateway Church, the ninth biggest church in the US, who is facing five counts of lewd acts with a child. At a previous church he disclosed what he called an inappropriate relationship with a young lady, went through a restoration process, and was handed his ministry back, without disclosing that she was 12 years old. That is not a young lady; that is a child.
        • An investigation found elders knew of the allegations as early as 2005 and failed to act. Attendance and giving dropped and the church vowed to reform; Elijah asks why it took this to root out a bad branch when no one had the spine to say anything. Morris’s response showed no repentance, only finger-pointing and demands for millions in retirement benefits. When someone tells you who they are, listen.
      2. 23:34 Mike Bickle and IHOP
        • Mike Bickle, founder of the International House of Prayer, in addition to peddling false prophecy, has been accused of assaulting and grooming women over decades, documented by an extensive private investigation, not cancel culture. He admitted only vague “inappropriate behavior,” and the investigation showed he exploited his pastoral authority, even telling victims God said his wife would die young.
        • His sister then preached that the investigation was trash and a false narrative, encouraged ignorance of it, and claimed God was giving him a divine pardon, “thus saith the Lord.” Elijah calls that blasphemy, and says the fact God did not strike her where she stood testifies to how merciful he is.
      3. 25:50 The only CEO in God’s kingdom
        • These aren’t outliers but symptoms of a sickness running deep in many churches. It is time to exercise biblical accountability to protect the vulnerable. A leader unwilling to submit to eldership and accountability has no business running a lemonade stand, let alone God’s people.
        • The only CEO in God’s kingdom is the Lord Jesus Christ. Everyone else is expendable, including Elijah, Josh, Chris, Steve, and the rest of the staff; the goal is that they could all be gone tomorrow and the church would go on, carried by the Holy Spirit, not a cult of personality. If anything is built on the personality of the leader, let it burn.

  8. 27:12 Why bring this up?

    • The church has gotten really good at pointing the finger and bad at self-policing. Elijah’s job isn’t only to say what everyone wants to hear, but to call attention to the dark issues of our day in the culture and in our churches.
    • He and Michaela are welcoming a baby girl in December, and he is overjoyed and terrified: he wants the church his daughter grows up in to be a place where she is safe, protected, cared for, and loved, and where she sees God’s people walk out what they say they believe. So much disillusionment with the church comes from unrepentant hypocrisy and unbalanced standards in leadership. It must end. We can’t change where we’ve been, but we can course-correct for our kids and grandkids.

  9. 28:59 How not to respond: the Levite’s outrage

    Scripture: Judges 19:27–30

    • Back to the Levite, to learn how not to respond. Elijah reads how he finds her at the threshold, says, “Get up. Let’s go,” gets no answer, takes her home, cuts her into 12 parts, and sends them throughout Israel, and everyone says such a thing has never been seen.
    • The nation is rightfully horrified, and the shocking act gets everyone’s attention. But he frames the story as if Gibeah alone is guilty and never admits his own guilt; he put her out there, yet in his telling he’s the victim. This is outrage without repentance: wanting justice without looking in the mirror.
    • That is how the world does outrage, stirring people up about others’ sins while holding on to our own compromise. Scroll X or TikTok and you find a world full of Levites angry at everyone else’s wickedness and silent about their own.

    1. 30:58 Beware the Levite’s instinct

      Scripture: Matthew 7:3–5

      • No one on this teaching team will shy away from their own shortcomings. Elijah isn’t speaking from a moral high horse: “I’m a dead man walking,” nothing without the grace of Jesus Christ. He loves the Lord’s church and believes she will thrive, but that requires men and women pursuing personal holiness so they can exercise discernment and root out bad branches.
      • So he pleads: beware the Levite’s instinct. If your moral outrage outpaces your personal holiness, you are walking in his footsteps. It is always easier to blame them than to repent of me. Jesus says in Matthew 7, first take the plank out of your own eye. Revival starts when we all acknowledge how we’ve sinned against God and ask for his grace and mercy.

  10. 32:30 God as consultant, not king

    Scripture: Judges 20:18–23 1 John 1:9

    • When we operate from outrage, chaos ensues. The tribes unite against Benjamin and demand the guilty men of Gibeah; Benjamin refuses, and now there is civil war. Elijah reads how Israel inquires of God, “Who of us is to go up first?”, and loses 22,000 on the first day.
    • The irony: Israel inquires of God when it is convenient. They want justice without accountability and repentance. It isn’t “Should we go up, Lord?” but “Who shall go up first?” They want God to bless their anger while keeping their idols hidden, and his guidance on a dark path he never sent them on.
    • Many Christians operate from the same place. We don’t seek God’s will; we seek confirmation of what we’ve already decided, then ask him to bless it. We want him as our consultant, not our king, our personal cheerleader rather than the one we seek. Before you ask God to fight for you, ask if you’re right with him.
    • If that’s you, you have nothing to run from. Our God is merciful and faithful, and as the apostle John reminds us, if we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us. You can turn right now.

  11. 35:30 Outrage without repentance always overshoots

    Scripture: James 1:19–21

    • Israel is defeated twice. On the third day they weep, fast, offer sacrifices, and worship, and only then does God grant victory. But they take it too far, rampaging through every town in Benjamin. Their quest for justice became a bloodbath.
    • This is the tragedy of outrage ungoverned by repentance: even after God grants victory, they are governed by the same emotions that led them into the mess rather than the forgiveness God freely gave. It always escalates; it never stops at justice but slides into vengeance, and God says vengeance is his, not ours.
    • Cancel culture, online mobs, political witch hunts: outrage is intoxicating, and without the fear of the Lord it becomes destructive. Elijah reads that everyone should be quick to listen, slow to speak, and slow to become angry, because human anger does not produce the righteousness God requires. Outrage without the cross will only leave a trail of ruin.

  12. 37:30 Guilt, not repentance

    Scripture: Judges 21:2–3 Judges 21:25

    • The end is not much better than the beginning. Israel swore no one would give his daughter to a Benjamite, and after nearly wiping Benjamin out only 600 men are left. They weep at Bethel, crying, “Why has this happened to Israel?” Elijah’s answer: “Riddle me this, Batman.” They just don’t get it, and sometimes neither do we.
    • Their sorrow is not over their sin against God but over the consequences of their actions. That is guilt, not repentance, and there is a difference: you can feel guilty and still have no desire to right the wrong.
    • In the rest of the story Israel doubles down on its sin to fix the self-inflicted population problem, leading to more depravity, rebellion, idolatry, and death. The book ends not with a bang but a whimper: “In those days there was no king in Israel. Everyone did what was right in his own eyes.”

  13. 39:10 The true king who would come

    Scripture: John 6:38

    • Elijah closed the series with this passage so the church would see the depth of their own depravity apart from the Lord, and because Israel’s disobedience gives more reason to celebrate the faithfulness of Christ and the mercy of the cross. Israel’s failure is no surprise to God; this book prepares us for the true king, not Saul, David, or Solomon, who would also fail.
    • Unlike Israel, unlike us, Jesus never did what was right in his own eyes; he always did what was right in his Father’s eyes. Where Israel abandoned covenant, Christ fulfilled it; where Israel indulged sin, Christ resisted temptation; where Israel descended into chaos, Christ brought peace. “I have come down from heaven, not to do my own will, but the will of him who sent me.”

    1. 40:13 The king who lays down his life for his bride

      Scripture: Judges 19

      • This is the king we need: not one who takes advantage of those entrusted to him or abuses his authority, but one who lays down his life for his bride, the church.
      • Think of the horror of Judges 19: a woman abused, abandoned, and butchered, a symbol of sin’s devastation in our lives and culture. In the gospel we find the opposite. Christ takes a defiled bride, sinners, and instead of handing us over, takes our place. On the cross he was treated like the concubine: abused, mocked, stripped, killed. But he rose victorious to redeem and cleanse his bride forever.
      • Unlike the days of the judges, we do not live in an age with no king. He is risen, he is reigning, and he is coming again to judge the living and the dead and make all things new.

  14. 41:18 Is Christ your king?

    Scripture: Matthew 16:18

    • America doesn’t need another politician with empty promises, the church doesn’t need more pragmatism, and our families don’t need more self-help. We need King Jesus ruling in our hearts, homes, and churches. Is Christ your king, not just your savior or helper, but the one who defines right and wrong and rules your desires, money, sexuality, politics, and worship?
    • Judges ends in despair, but your story doesn’t have to, because the true king has come. Tired of compromise? Submit to Christ the King. Crushed by guilt? Run to him. Longing for hope in a collapsing world? Lift your eyes, voice, hands, and heart to Christ the King.
    • So we don’t retreat, surrender, or curl up waiting for rescue. We advance: raising our families in the Lord, building churches that preach Christ without apology, and proclaiming the gospel in the face of darkness, knowing the gates of hell shall not prevail. The church advances and King Jesus wins. That is our marching song.

  15. 42:56 Closing prayer

    • Elijah thanks God for being faithful when we are not and for living the life we couldn’t and dying the death we deserved. He prays the church won’t use that truth as an excuse for complacency but will be bold and unapologetic in the face of darkness, advancing against gates of hell that stand no chance against the church.
    • He asks God to help them worship in spirit and truth, set aside distractions, doubts, and anxieties, and focus on who he is and what he has done and promised. He blesses everyone listening with boldness and a fire in their bones to spread the hope and truth of the gospel in their families, workplaces, and every place they touch.
