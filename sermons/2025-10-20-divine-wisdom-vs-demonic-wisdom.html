Overview

  1. 00:21 James tests claimed wisdom by the life it produces

    Scripture: James 2:26 James 3:13–18

    • Josh introduces the second half of James 3 and immediately reconnects it to the whole letter. The earlier “faith without works is dead” message was not an isolated hurdle to clear; James repeatedly distinguishes genuine faith from a manufactured version by whether knowing becomes doing. The same test now applies to wisdom: the wise do not merely identify themselves as wise; wisdom governs their decisions, values, priorities, and identity. Application: read all of James during the week so the individual teachings remain connected.
    • He places the congregation in the position of James’s first-century hearers and asks, “Who among you is wise?” The silence is appropriate because a claim cannot establish wisdom. A person’s life must reveal whether the governing wisdom comes “from above” or “from below.”
    • Josh reads James 3:13–18, which supplies the sermon’s controlling contrast: heavenly wisdom appears in a good life, humble deeds, purity, peace, consideration, submission, mercy, good fruit, impartiality, sincerity, and a harvest of righteousness; false wisdom appears in bitter envy, selfish ambition, boasting, denial of truth, disorder, and evil practice; James names the lower source as earthly, unspiritual, and demonic.

  2. 03:05 Wisdom is applied truth, not intelligence or polished religious knowledge

    Scripture: Luke 10:25–37

    • Josh defines wisdom as the ability to use knowledge and experience to make good decisions and judgments. Christian wisdom knows God’s truth and applies it to ordinary situations and choices. The unwise may possess the same information yet be unable—or unwilling—to act rightly. Therefore intelligence and wisdom are not interchangeable: “the fool didn’t miss an email” or lack a puzzle piece; truth simply fails to govern the fool’s attitudes, priorities, values, and actions.
    • He uses playful audience categories to separate knowledge from wise practice. A book-smart person might understand quantum physics but be unable to cook ramen. A street-smart person might build computers but fail to manage a budget. Looking back, people often wish they could put present knowledge and experience into their twenty-year-old selves; their scars came partly from thinking they saw the whole picture when they did not. Some learn from those collisions, while others continue making the same destructive decision despite repeated bruises.
    • Wisdom, like faith, is not immediately visible. Eye color can be sorted at a glance; genuinely wise people cannot. Wisdom must work itself out over time, so James compares the eventual “fruit baskets” of true and false wisdom.
    • Josh reconnects the passage to James 3’s warning about would-be teachers. Desire to lead God’s people can be noble, but anyone can stand up and say attractive things. A polished sermon can miss God’s heart and therefore miss faithful application.
    • Illustration: a distorted Good Samaritan sermon. Josh recalls a preacher promising “a different take on an old classic,” which made him wary of novelty supposedly missed by every believer since Pentecost. The preacher centered the innkeeper, even though Jesus’s parable calls God’s people to stop when religious elites pass by, tear down hostile boundaries, love the stranger, and bear the cost of helping the wounded. Josh’s corrective is, “We don’t charge to house the hurting. We foot the bill.” The preacher likely knew the story and possessed greater academic credentials, yet missed its point; this is precisely the knowledge without wisdom James confronts.
    • Transition: Josh says the two kinds of wisdom will be tested in two areas—where each originates and what each creates.

  3. 09:55 False wisdom originates below and eventually collapses

    Scripture: James 3:15 Genesis 11:1–9 1 Samuel 17:38–40, 45–47 James 3:15 Ephesians 2:1–3 1 Corinthians 1:20–21 Ecclesiastes 1:2 Ecclesiastes 1:2 1 Corinthians 1:1–2:16

    • Josh begins with the source of false wisdom and rereads James 3:15. Heavenly wisdom comes from God; earthly wisdom is made by humanity apart from God. Man-made wisdom can look, sound, smell, and taste good, pass normal tests, and even work temporarily, but it eventually fails.
    • Tower of Babel illustration: the project likely began with energy, unity, diversity, ambition, and visible progress—“Let’s build a name for ourselves”—but ended in failure and confusion.
    • David and Saul’s armor illustration: ordinary prudence said an outmatched David should use Saul’s armor, but God intended to use David’s particular gifts and presence with him was more than enough. Josh’s principle: common sense often looks foolish beside wisdom from God.
    • Crossroads Cares illustration: While the ministry was being launched, Josh feared that designated generosity to practical needs might weaken the church’s general fund. Earthly wisdom worried about the bank account even though God had placed the ministry on their hearts. Since Crossroads Cares began in 2021, giving has risen slowly and steadily, the church has opened a care center, avoided layoffs, paid its utilities, maintained its budget, and did not need its contingency that summer. The congregation’s proper response is gratitude and praise to God.
    • Josh connects James’s “earthly, unspiritual, and demonic” description to the believer’s three enemies: the world, the flesh, and the devil. As the Trinity gives wisdom from above, this “unholy trinity” offers wisdom from below. The world has more freely available knowledge than ever but little wisdom.
    • He quotes 1 Corinthians 1:20–21 to show that God exposes worldly wisdom as foolish and saves believers through what the world calls foolish preaching.
    • Human progress has not produced moral or spiritual peace. Despite extraordinary communication technology, humanity is not meaningfully closer to ending poverty, hunger, imbalance, ignorance, racism, hatred, crime, or immorality than in Paul’s day. Steve Jobs created transformative devices yet reportedly kept them from his own children because he understood the danger of replacing human interaction with technology; many parents nevertheless use the same technology as a babysitter. Josh repeats Ecclesiastes’ verdict that everything is “meaningless” to distinguish increased knowledge from enduring wisdom. Medical and technological advances can serve good, but progress without morality also multiplies evil: communication improves while listening declines; knowledge of the mind increases while mental-health problems rise; knowledge of human nature increases while crime, war, hate, and racism persist.
    • Human wisdom treats surface symptoms; God’s wisdom reaches the root. Even resistance to injustice can press so hard that reformers become what they sought to eradicate. God answers injustice through humility, but human wisdom considers humility foolish.
    • Application and transition: read the first two chapters of 1 Corinthians during the week. Man’s wisdom arises from reason and fades; God’s wisdom arrives by revelation and lasts forever.

  4. 17:25 Seek wisdom from above through Scripture, Christ, prayer, and the Spirit

    Scripture: Proverbs 9:10 Romans 3:10–18 Genesis 3:1–6 James 1:17 Philippians 3:20 Matthew 6:19–21 John 3:3, 6 Romans 5:8 Romans 10:13 Philippians 2:10–11 Colossians 2:2–3 Matthew 7:7 Deuteronomy 4:29 2 Timothy 3:15 James 1:5 Hebrews 12:2

    • Josh laments statistics indicating that fewer than half of Christians regularly read Scripture outside church. He celebrates increases among Gen Z (11% to 15%) and millennials (12% to 17%) but insists the numbers must continue climbing.
    • Practical application: replace worldly inputs with God’s Word. Josh describes deleting social media for thirty days and feeling as though he had “taken a shower from the world.” He replaced Instagram with the Glorify app, which offers a daily quotation, verse, and devotional. His family also uses weekly Scripture cards from teachingschool.com at the dinner table. A different person leads on four days using one of four approaches: silent meditation, discussion of motivation, attention to proper meaning, and memorization. The humorous threat that the last person to recite the verse loses dessert is not their actual rule. The goal is one text supplying more heavenly wisdom to the home throughout the week. Commands: get into the Word; bathe children, grandchildren, and spouse in it; teach family members to wash themselves in the Word and away from the world; lead the home into something meaningful instead of letting YouTube raise the children.
    • Josh states that turning from God has cost the world its wisdom, and increasing advancement only makes the underlying problem more visible. He quotes Proverbs 9:10: fear of the Lord begins wisdom. He contrasts it with Romans 3:10–18, where humanity neither understands nor seeks God, practices deceit and violence, does not know peace, and has no fear of God. Humanity’s disorder comes from taking its eyes off the Holy One; believers must fix their eyes on Jesus or risk clinging to demonic wisdom.
    • Genesis 3 establishes the old pattern of demonic wisdom. Satan deceived Eve, and since the fall this self-centered wisdom has opposed God’s wisdom. The serpent’s lie persuaded Eve to seek godlike autonomy. Josh gives the issue a stark either/or: those who do not cling to God’s wisdom will fall for Satan’s wisdom.
    • Transition to wisdom from above: Josh quotes James 1:17, where every good and perfect gift comes down from the unchanging Father. Wisdom in James 3 is one of those gifts, and the Father supplies more than his children deserve. Josh locates Christian identity above: heavenly citizenship, primary allegiance to God’s kingdom, and heavenly treasure outrank every human institution and possession.
    • Christ is the content and source of divine wisdom. Josh recalls Jesus’s teaching that one must be born again and that Spirit gives birth to spirit. He expands into the gospel: God demonstrated love in Christ; Jesus lived perfectly, bore the curse and condemnation at the cross, rose, holds the keys of death and Hades, saves all who call on him, gives eternal life, will return, make all things new, and receive the confession of every knee and tongue. The emotional point is not abstract: Josh says he wakes smiling because human rejection cannot undo God’s demonstrated love and purchased freedom.
    • Josh quotes Colossians 2:2–3: the mystery is Christ, in whom all treasures of wisdom and knowledge are hidden. Hidden treasure must be sought. Jesus’s “ask, seek, knock” promise and Deuteronomy 4:29 agree that wholehearted seekers find God.
    • Concluding application for the source question: Get into the Word and seek the Lord; Scripture makes people wise for salvation. Those who lack wisdom should ask God. Do not settle for worldly, demonic counterfeits; seek Christ alone, receive wisdom from God, remain in Scripture, and walk by the Spirit.
    • Transition: Josh moves from where the two wisdoms originate to what they create.

  5. 26:36 False wisdom first produces bitter envy

    Scripture: James 3:14–16 James 3:1 Mark 9:33–35 Jeremiah 9:23–24; 1 Corinthians 1:31

    • Josh rereads James 3:14–16 and begins inspecting the “fruit basket” of false wisdom.
    • The first fruit is bitter envy, also rendered bitter jealousy or zeal. It naturally joins selfish ambition. James 3 began by warning believers against ambition for positions of teaching authority.
    • Personal testimony about calling: Josh says he has been called throughout his life to preach and lead, but he did not arrive in his role because he wanted the job. Because the role rests on calling rather than a honeymoon phase, difficulty will not make him quit. God changed his life and gave him purpose; he will go elsewhere if God calls, but until then he remains where God placed him.
    • Worldly wisdom instead says to climb the ladder, chase promotion, insist another person is not special, and imagine, “That should be me.” This pressure appears in workplaces, politics, relationships, and churches. Even Jesus’s disciples argued over who was greatest in the kingdom.
    • Josh reads Mark 9:33–35. The disciples’ embarrassed silence shows that they already knew their argument did not honor God. Jesus overturns worldly rank: whoever wants to be first must be last and servant of all. This marks the difference between disciples and Pharisees: both possessed religious information, but the elite missed the Messiah because their wisdom came from below and desired human praise. Human praise is the only reward such ambition receives; the servant shares in God’s glory.
    • Diagnostic application: ask, “Why do you do what you do?” Is the motive title, visibility, ego, influence, or relevance? Josh invokes Jeremiah and Paul: boasting belongs only in the Lord. He tests envy through paired questions: gratitude for one’s mission or resentment of another’s place; gratitude for one’s gift or desire for hers; grief and help when another falls or relief that it was someone else; kingdom use of one’s resources or preoccupation with a different lot.

  6. 31:41 Selfish ambition matures into rivalry, dishonesty, and judgment

    Scripture: Philippians 2:3–4 1 Corinthians 4:5 Matthew 7:21–23 Romans 12:21

    • The next false fruit is selfish ambition. Josh explains the Greek term as language used for a politician working a crowd for votes. False wisdom builds a following and forms strategic alliances, like the television game Survivor, to preserve leverage and position. In church this creates rivalry and division and turns pastoral work from joy into a nightmare.
    • Josh counters it with Philippians 2:3–4: do nothing from selfish ambition or vain conceit, humbly value others, and attend to their interests. False wisdom cannot do this because it has not grasped how Jesus attended to sinners’ interests. True wisdom has no need to compare Christians because it looks at Christ; everyone has far to go beside him.
    • The third false fruit is dishonesty, which follows a recognizable sequence. Envy wants what another has and assumes it is deserved. Selfish ambition resolves to obtain it, using other people as tools. Dishonesty lies when necessary to complete the campaign.
    • Self-advertising cannot determine the final verdict. No one speaks at his own funeral, and a self-written “puff piece” will not stand on the day of the Lord. Josh quotes 1 Corinthians 4:5: wait for the Lord, who exposes darkness and heart motives and assigns praise. He notes from Matthew 7 that the final disclosure will not result in praise for everyone.
    • Pastoral encouragement to people wounded by false wisdom: Although schemers seem to escape consequences, they are not getting away with anything. Do not take revenge; “hold the line” and trust the Lord to repay. Overcome evil with good. Believers need one another because anger would rather overcome evil physically; the community restrains that impulse while waiting for the Lord.
    • Transition: Josh turns from the fruit of false wisdom to the fruit of true wisdom.

  7. 35:36 True wisdom begins in humble deeds and produces purity and peacemaking

    Scripture: James 3:13, 17 James 3:13 Matthew 5:5 Matthew 5:8 Matthew 5:9

    • Josh joins James 3:13 to James 3:17 so the character list remains attached to deeds done in humility. James’s description aligns with Jesus’s Beatitudes in the Sermon on the Mount. Josh notes that James heard Jesus before believing and uses that fact to urge Christians to continue preaching the gospel to family members even through rejection.
    • The ESV renders James 3:13 as “the meekness of wisdom.” Meekness is not weakness but power under control; wisdom is knowledge used properly. Jesus says the meek inherit the earth. A truly wise person does not work for human praise but seeks God’s glory, and this orientation can be heard in how the person describes experiences and accomplishments.
    • True wisdom is pure. Because God is holy, wisdom from him is pure. False wisdom leads to sin; God’s wisdom leads to purity. Josh connects this to Jesus’s promise that the pure in heart will see God.
    • True wisdom is peace-loving. False wisdom yields competition, rivalry, and backbiting; God’s wisdom yields peace, unity, and calm in a storm. Josh rejects a common church counterfeit: ignoring sin or sweeping it under the rug is not biblical peace. Jesus blesses peacemakers, not passive “peacekeepers.” Wisdom-made peace does not hide or ignore sin; believers confess to one another, creating opportunities to show mercy. Showing mercy is central to the church’s identity.

  8. 38:51 Consideration, submission, mercy, good fruit, impartiality, and sincerity complete the portrait

    Scripture: Matthew 5:3 Matthew 5:7 Luke 10:25–37 Matthew 5:6 Ephesians 4:15

    • True wisdom is considerate. Josh says this word alone needs to be recovered by the church. He cites Matthew Arnold’s phrase “sweet reasonableness”: extending to others the kind consideration one wishes to receive. He links this posture with poverty of spirit and the kingdom of heaven.
    • True wisdom is submissive. It is charitable rather than stubborn, like a soldier receiving an order instead of organizing a mutiny. It listens, works toward unity, knows when rigidity is inappropriate, lays down the sword, and embraces a brother.
    • True wisdom is full of mercy. Josh connects it to Jesus’s blessing on the merciful. Mercy supplies evidence of saving faith by forgiving wrongdoers and reaching down to help them when they suffer. He returns to the Good Samaritan: the wise believer is not an innkeeper eager to charge but a compassionate neighbor concerned for anyone who needs support. The result is deep love among believers.
    • True wisdom is full of good fruit. The Spirit’s fruit pours from the believer. These works are not disguised selfish ambition but the overflow of a divinely transformed heart—a godly ambition that hungers and thirsts for righteousness.
    • True wisdom is impartial. Because God shows no favoritism, believers treat one another equally. Instead of hunting for faults, they build fellow believers up and spur them on in the good fight.
    • True wisdom is sincere. A false appearance can be maintained only temporarily. Josh explains “hypocrite” as a mask-wearer or actor; eventually the wearer must come up for air. God’s children do not need masks. Wisdom from above makes them open, genuine, transparent, truthful, and able to speak the truth in love.

  9. 42:06 Earthly wisdom makes schemers; heavenly wisdom makes servants and peacemakers

    Scripture: James 3:18 James 2:17–18, 26 Proverbs 6:16–19

    • Josh warns that a church, life group, or Christian circle characterized by pretending, backbiting, undermining, and criticism is saturated with false wisdom; the hearer should retreat.
    • He condemns religious politics and personal campaigns. God’s hatred must be preached alongside God’s love. God’s children do not scheme or build names for themselves; they kneel with towel and basin, wash feet, and treat others as more valuable—not merely in speech but in action.
    • Josh states the contrast in compact form: earthly wisdom creates schemers; heavenly wisdom creates servants; false wisdom leads to cursing; true wisdom leads to blessing.
    • He quotes James 3:18 about peacemakers sowing in peace and reaping righteousness. This remains consistent with James’s letter: professed Christianity must be demonstrated by works. Genuine faith bears godly wisdom, and godly wisdom lets the Lord use a believer’s life to bring righteousness and peace to others. The warning is equally serious: a believer’s life can become an instrument through which Satan spreads sin, war, disorder, division, and every evil practice.
    • Central diagnostic: “What are you sowing—peace or discord?” The Lord’s house should be full of peacemakers. Josh recalls Proverbs’ condemnation of wicked schemes and conflict-stirring because the church’s familial bond matters to God. He suggests unity may be the only godly measure of a healthy church that ultimately matters.
    • Transition to invitation: exchange false wisdom for true wisdom and return to the Word, because needed wisdom is found in the person and work of Jesus Christ. Jesus is wisdom, and obeying him is everything.

  10. 45:16 Find wisdom in Jesus, lay down the old life, and pray for visible fruit

    Scripture: Proverbs 3:13 John 10:10

    • Josh quotes Proverbs 3:13 in the KJV—“Happy is the man that findeth wisdom”—and interprets the invitation christologically: happy is the person who finds Jesus.
    • He joins it to Jesus’s promise of full, abundant life.
    • Invitation to ashamed and wounded hearers: A person’s past sin will not shock the congregation; the church is a dysfunctional family of misfits whose polished present often hides disastrous former lives. Josh includes himself in that testimony and tells newcomers not to compare themselves with long-time Christians. Stop looking at Christians and stare at Christ, who writes a distinct story in each believer; envy of another person’s story is another form of the false wisdom just exposed. Everyone came to Jesus as a mess. Bring whatever burden or sin remains, lay it at Jesus’s feet, and do not take it back. Read about Christ, find his promises, memorize them, and remember both what he has done and what he promises to do. Josh cannot predict the details of the hearer’s future story, but he insists encounter with Christ will not leave the person unchanged.
    • Closing prayer (47:18–49:18): Josh thanks the Father for what happens when God’s children gather around his promises, submit to his Word, and desire to look like Jesus. He points to needs being met naturally through transformed lives and to people entering church eager to report progress in their fight against addiction. He asks God to make Crossroads a place where broken people resist a world that calls sin acceptable, listen to God, and put the sin down. The prayer renounces love for the world’s possessions: God may take it all; retaining Christ is gain. Josh thanks God for saving him, saving the congregation, and shaping the church; he asks for continued focus and closes in God’s name with “Amen.”
