Overview

  1. 00:55 Part seven: the servant king and the servant judge

    Scripture: Mark 11 Romans 5:6

    • This is part seven of an eight-part series through Mark. Mark 11 begins the final week of the Lord’s life, which starts with a somewhat underwhelming coronation and ends with the cross and the resurrection.
    • Josh wants the church to notice two things: Jesus the servant king and Jesus the servant judge.
    • The year is AD 30, the month is Nisan; Jesus arrives on the 10th and is crucified on the 14th. The timing of Holy Week is best described in the apostle Paul’s words: “at just the right time.”

  2. 02:04 Jerusalem at Passover

    • Jerusalem at Passover was like Phoenix hosting the Super Bowl, or better, like everyone who watched the Super Bowl on TV coming in person. Millions of devout Jews came to the holy city with hearts full of excitement and national spirit.
    • It was amazing if you were a Jew and annoying if you were a Roman. The population more than tripled, the military was on high alert, and a Roman soldier’s PTO request was always denied, to lower the chances that a Zealot would try to kill an official and incite a riot.
    • On Friday tens of thousands of Passover lambs would be slain, none of which could take away anyone’s sin. But this Friday was a good Friday: one sacrifice would be made for the sins of all who have ever believed, the sacrifice of God’s true lamb.

  3. 03:34 The triumphal entry

    Scripture: Mark 11:1–11

    • We call this the triumphal entry, but it is an odd title; there is nothing grand or royal about it, and Jesus does something that seems a head-scratcher on the surface.
    • Josh reads Mark 11:1–11: Jesus sends two disciples for a colt no one has ridden, “the Lord needs it,” cloaks and branches on the road, the shouts of “Hosanna,” and Jesus looking around the temple courts before going out to Bethany with the Twelve because it was already late.

  4. 05:03 Something is off here

    • The coronation of a king isn’t humble or unexpected. Where was the planning meeting? It seems unofficial, spontaneous, thrown together, even temporary: what kind of king rides into town to cheers only to be executed there four days later?
    • Jesus goes from being exalted to being executed. He is the real King, worthy of all honor, worship, and praise, but we have to ask what is really going on.

  5. 05:44 A king on a little donkey

    Scripture: Zechariah 9:9

    • Jesus doesn’t ride in on a war horse. The Greek word is pōlos, a baby horse or colt, possibly a little donkey: a grown 33-year-old king riding something you might give your niece for her twelfth birthday, maybe holding his feet up. Jesus takes the path of a king while keeping the posture of a servant.
    • What seems strange is significant: Jesus claims to be the one Zechariah prophesied about some 550 years earlier, the king coming “lowly and riding on a donkey.” In effect: I’m the king, just not the king you were expecting.

    1. 07:03 Why the prophecy matters

      • Mark doesn’t quote Zechariah because he is writing to Gentiles, but the fulfillment matters for two reasons: Jesus declares himself Israel’s King and Messiah, and he challenges the religious leaders. This moment was the tipping point that led to the plot to take his life.
      • That is another display of God’s sovereignty. The Jewish leaders didn’t want to arrest Jesus during the feast because it was a PR nightmare and they feared the crowds, but it didn’t matter what they wanted. God’s servant was going to die at Passover, at just the right time.

  6. 07:56 Cloaks, branches, and “Hosanna”

    Scripture: 2 Kings 9:13 Psalm 118:25–26

    • Laying outer garments and branches on the road was customary when welcoming a king, as was done for King Jehu in Second Kings. It is an ancient gesture of submission: I’m below your feet. Palm branches are often symbols of joy, so this is joyful submission.
    • They shout “Hosanna,” which means “save now,” from Psalm 118. Jesus knows they are quoting a messianic psalm, and he doesn’t stop them.
    • By not saying “Hold up, I don’t think you’ve got this figured out,” he openly and publicly affirms his kingship as the Son of David. Up to this point he never allowed a public demonstration declaring him Messiah; he told people to go home, because he came first to die, and not until he returns will he establish God’s kingdom on earth.

  7. 09:43 Not the same crowd

    • Josh corrects something he may have said himself: some say the people who cried “Hosanna” on Palm Sunday ended up yelling “Crucify him” on Good Friday. That isn’t true. The crowd that wanted him crucified came mainly from Judea and Jerusalem; the Jews from Galilee who had traveled with him were sympathetic and supported his ministry.
    • It is not so different in the church today. Crowds still gather, but not everyone lays down their coat and says, “Jesus, you’re king of my life. Walk all over my life, leave your tread marks, take my life and make it yours.” Too many are observing, waiting to see how this plays out.

  8. 10:46 How it plays out: the ascension and the white horse

    Scripture: Acts 1 Philippians 2:9–11 Revelation 19:11–16

    • If the triumphal entry doesn’t feel triumphant, Josh tells about the ascension and return. In Acts 1 Jesus ascended into heaven, and Paul told the Philippians he took his seat at the right hand of God and was given the name above every name, “Lord,” to which everyone who has ever existed will bow.
    • Jesus entered Jerusalem as a king and the religious elite crashed his party, but it came as no shock to heaven; it was the plan all along. This king wasn’t sent to conquer Rome but sin and death. God wove the gospel through Roman oppression and the leaders’ exploitation, and his providence demands our praise.
    • Jesus knew the donkey was waiting in that town, but he also knew the white horse waiting in heaven. Josh reads of the rider called Faithful and True, with many crowns and a robe dipped in blood, named King of kings and Lord of lords. If you are wondering where the victory parade is, there it is: the triumphal entry gets its name from a spiritual victory, not an earthly one.

  9. 13:03 Victory looks different in the kingdom

    Scripture: Matthew 27:54 Hebrews 12:1

    • What did the Romans think of this Passover parade? Probably mixed reviews. Josh often thinks of the centurion in Matthew 27, who after the earth shook and the sun went dark said, “Truly this was the Son of God,” and wonders if he thought back to this parade. At the start of the week it would have looked like a joke.
    • Romans were experts at victory parades: a returning general displayed trophies and captives, rode a golden chariot while priests burned incense, and ended at the arena where captives fought wild beasts.
    • Our Lord’s entry didn’t look like much, and spiritual victories are often hard to see in the moment. Josh says those being baptized today need to hear it most: victory looks different in the kingdom of God.
    • A Roman general had to kill 5,000 people to earn his parade. Peter preached to thousands who came to faith and received new hearts, and instead of a parade he got a custom crucifixion. What you don’t see is Peter’s victory parade in heaven. We are surrounded by a great cloud of witnesses, so let us run the race of faith with endurance.

  10. 15:39 Verse 11: Jesus surveys the temple courts

    Scripture: Mark 11:11 Mark 11:12–14

    • Verse 11 ended weird: Jesus looked around at everything in the temple courts and then left. He saw something.
    • The temple courts were the business operations of the temple. We have the stock market; they sold livestock, and the smell must have been hideous. Josephus says during one Passover week 25,000 lambs were bought, sold, and sacrificed there.
    • Jesus went to Bethany for the night, maybe to hang out with Lazarus, and came back the next day. Josh reads of Jesus, hungry, finding a fig tree with nothing but leaves and saying, “May no one ever eat fruit from you again,” and his disciples heard him.

  11. 17:11 Jesus the servant judge: the fig tree

    Scripture: Mark 5 Mark 11

    • On the surface this looks bad for Jesus. He is hungry, goes to a tree in April that doesn’t bear figs until June, and gets mad. Some worry Jesus is mistreating the tree, but he isn’t dealing with a tree at all. He is dealing with the nation of Israel, and with you and me. It is an object lesson: you have no fruit, and your temple is filled with corruption.
    • Jesus’ ministry isn’t marked by judgment, but at some point those who produce no fruit and invite corruption into the temple must be dealt with.
    • There are only two occasions where the Lord uses his divine power to destroy something in nature, and the series has seen both: the drowning of the pigs in Mark 5 and the fig tree in Mark 11. Two lessons surround the fig tree, fruit and faith, and Josh honors the way Mark splits the story around the temple.

    1. 18:59 Lesson one: Jesus wants his people to bear fruit

      Scripture: John 15:16 John 15 Matthew 3:10

      • John 15 is all about it: if you aren’t rooted in Christ you will wither, and apart from him you can bear no fruit. Israel had nothing but leaves; their roots were planted in self-righteousness, not Christ’s righteousness, a recipe for stink fruit. They looked right on the outside but produced no fruit.
      • John the Baptist said the axe is laid to the root of the trees, and every tree that does not bear good fruit is cut down. In other words, listen and respond: repent and believe. There is no fruit without repentance and no Christian without Christ.
      • It might look like a tree, have leaves, even give shade, but is there fruit? It might look like a Christian, or a house of prayer, but is it really?

  12. 20:22 The temple cleared: a house of prayer or a den of robbers

    Scripture: Mark 11:15–18 John 2 Mark 9:42 Jeremiah 7:9–11

    • Josh reads Mark 11:15–18: Jesus drives out the buyers and sellers, overturns the money changers’ tables, and says, “My house will be called a house of prayer for all nations, but you have made it a den of robbers,” and the chief priests look for a way to kill him.
    • With the fig tree and the temple, Jesus is calling out shallow religion and exploitation. It isn’t the first time; in John 2 he made a whip and drove out the herds, saying stop making my Father’s house a market.
    • Josh loves that Mark mentions those selling doves. The dove was one of the few sacrifices poor people could afford. God didn’t price the poor out of making a sin offering, but the religious leaders tried to, and that angered the Lord. Josh thinks of Mark 9: better a millstone around the neck than causing a little one to stumble.
    • A place of prayer is where someone goes when they want to be changed; a den of thieves is where someone goes when they want to hide. Jeremiah faced the same in his day: steal, murder, and commit adultery, then stand in this house and say “We are safe.” “But I have been watching, declares the Lord.” The leaders were using the temple as a disguise, appearing holy while doing evil.

  13. 23:04 A busy temple with no prayer

    Scripture: Isaiah 1:13 Jeremiah 7:11

    • This is where the text hit Josh like a ton of bricks; he sat with it all week, and now it is the church’s turn. Jesus walked into a busy religious place: lots of events, noise, sign-ups, people, transactions, a whole day of entertainment for the family. What wasn’t happening? Prayer.
    • Isaiah and Jeremiah both warned their people to stop bringing meaningless offerings; God said, “I cannot bear your worthless assemblies,” because he wants true heart reform. The greatest temple filled with wickedness is worthless; one man seeking God’s heart reform is priceless.
    • Before we point fingers at the religious leaders, we must examine this place to make sure we aren’t making merchandise of the gospel. Do outsiders think of this place as a house of prayer or a den of robbers? Do our neighbors know that no matter who they are or what they’ve done, they are welcome, that the doors slide open for everyone? Are Christians using the church as a disguise: appearing holy, then going home and doing evil?
    • If Jesus walked in here, or into your home, what reforms would he make? What would he remove, add, keep? “I have been watching, declares the Lord.” He isn’t impressed by how many life groups you attend or perfect attendance at events. Jesus didn’t die so you could fill your calendar with church stuff; he died so you could have a new heart.
    • “Josh, I just finished my Bible in a year.” That’s cool, but are you different? If you are smarter but not holier, go back, repent, and read again. Jesus doesn’t leave things how he finds them; he is always ripping stuff out, turning things over, making things new. Can others give testimony to Christ’s reform in your heart?

    1. 26:38 One or two: checking our hearts

      • Some do more self-evaluation at one eye doctor appointment than in a lifetime of church attendance. The optometrist asks, “One or two?” We check our eyes but not our hearts.
      • If you came to Jesus angry, an addict, insecure and looking for validation, or selfish, are you seeing improvement, not perfection? Did you overcome it, or is it overcoming you? One or two? Is there actual heart reform led by the Holy Spirit, or just a jam-packed Christian calendar led by guilt?

    2. 27:59 Be involved, but don’t lose the one thing

      • Josh wants people involved. Everyone needs a life group for accountability, but signing up and never going is like getting married and not hanging out with your wife. If you are an addict, go to the recovery program on Monday nights. Serve, give, teach, do all the things.
      • But if all the things replace the one thing, clear out the whole temple to bring it back. If you aren’t praying, you will be overcome. We cannot lose our connection with Christ for stuff. No church programming, preaching, or church relationships can replace what happens with you and Jesus in the morning.
      • Some are surrounded by church people and wondering why it isn’t working. Where is your connection with Jesus? Start on your knees in repentance; everything else can wait, because the church doesn’t do heart reform. Only the Holy Spirit can.

  14. 29:48 The fig tree withered from the roots

    Scripture: Mark 11:19–21

    • Josh reads Mark 11:19–21: in the morning they saw the fig tree withered from the roots, and Peter said, “Rabbi, look! The fig tree you cursed has withered!”
    • The tree withered from the roots, confirming God’s judgment on Israel’s fruitlessness, and eventually ours. Now the Savior shows the connection between his divine power and our lives: we have witnessed his power; how do we live in it?

  15. 30:30 Lesson two: have faith in God

    Scripture: Mark 11:22 Ephesians 2:8

    • “Have faith in God,” Jesus answered. The call is for all of your faith, and it must be in God alone.
    • We expect little faith in the culture; if God hasn’t given it to them, how can we expect it? What is shocking is walking in here and finding a lack of faith. It is by grace you have been saved through faith, and according to Paul this faith is the gift of God. Believers rarely struggle to believe what Jesus has already done; we lack faith in what he will continue to do, and we quickly doubt his presence in trials.
    • It sounds ridiculous: he saved me, how could I doubt him now? But some are afraid, ashamed, worried. Jesus says have faith in God. Have it holstered; don’t leave home without it; when you forget it, preach it to yourself.
    • We are saved by faith, but the second the rain pours we forget what we have. Jesus doesn’t say you can have too much, so give him all you’ve got: now, tomorrow, here, at home, at work, in the hurting marriage and the broken home, when you lie down and when you wake up. God gave it to you, so use it, and the more you use it the more it will grow.
    • We came to him weak, wandering, and defeated, and he sends us off powerful and ready for battle. Nothing can overcome your faith because the one who gave it already overcame everything. If you feel you don’t have enough faith, then have more. Could it be that simple?

    1. 34:08 Faith in God, not in what he may give

      Scripture: Mark 11:23–25 Mark 14:36

      • Have crazy faith in God. Become like a child and have faith that makes no sense, that has people scratching their heads: “I don’t know, I just believe God’s going to do it.”
      • Josh reads Mark 11:23–25 about the mountain thrown into the sea, believing you have received what you ask in prayer, and forgiving anyone you hold something against. “That’s impossible.” No, nothing is impossible for God.
      • To pray effectively you need faith in God, not in what he may or may not give you. If your focus is on what you hope to receive and you don’t get it, you will be disappointed, but no one loved by God is ever left disappointed. If everything were taken and all Josh had left was faith, he would consider it net gain.
      • Jesus, our example, prayed, “Abba, Father, everything is possible for you. Take this cup from me. Yet not what I will, but what you will.” Our desires often lead us to prayer, but God’s desires need to finish our prayers.

    2. 35:48 What’s weighing on you?

      • What is weighing on you? Is something in your life too much to bear? Have faith in God. Is it possible you have miscalculated your capacity for faith and have more storage than you ever thought?
      • Some are carrying health issues, praying for God’s will while afraid of what it might be: he will give you strength to endure. Some are fighting and praying for their marriage: lay it at his feet and have faith God will heal your marriage and home. Some lost a job: you are a believer, so have faith in God. Why spend one moment in worry when we can make requests?
      • Some think, “Josh, you have no idea what I’m going through.” He doesn’t, and they don’t know all the troubles he has had. But if someone told him in his darkest hour, “Josh, have faith in God,” he would thank them for being a good brother or sister and pointing him in the right direction.

  16. 37:59 For those with no God to trust

    • Josh’s heart is heavy for those with no God to trust. He doesn’t know how they suffer so much with no hope of the world to come, no heaven to look to, no view of paradise after incredible loss.
    • He has been praying that the mountain between them and God would be removed today and that God would grant them faith in Christ as he did for Josh. If God is working on your heart, don’t walk away and act like nothing happened: repent, change, leave your life of sin, and be baptized. Don’t run into a future without Jesus.

  17. 39:00 Forgive before the table

    • To those who trust in Christ: forgiving others is tough. We love forgiveness when it is for us, not so much when it is for them. As you grow in Christ, your capacity for forgiveness will grow.
    • But if you pray while holding a grudge, you are no different than a tree without fruit or a temple without prayer. True faith changes the heart; real prayer breaks down pride and vengeance and replaces them with love. For powerful prayers and powerful ministry, the church has to show radical forgiveness.
    • Josh’s action step: if you aren’t right with someone here, go to them before you even think about communion. You cannot hold the body and blood of the Savior and your unforgiveness at the same time; drop one. If they aren’t here, let the plate pass, go outside and call them, then come back. Communion will be here all morning. It’s time to release our grudges and forgive. Have faith in God.

  18. 40:58 Closing prayer

    • Josh prays that a room that just saw a temple full of transactions that have nothing to do with the heart would fill with heart transactions; that names, unspoken situations, and hurts never given forgiveness would be let go, since God didn’t require a big discussion to embrace and forgive us, only humility and repentance.
    • If we cannot offer forgiveness, what kind of church are we? “We don’t want to be a den of thieves; we don’t want to be a fruitless tree.” He asks the Lord to convict them, give courage to follow through, and that they would never go back.
