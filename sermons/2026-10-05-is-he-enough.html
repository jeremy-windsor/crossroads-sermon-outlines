Overview

  1. 00:17 Last week’s question: Peter, is he worth it?

    Scripture: Luke 5:5 Luke 5:11

    • Last week Jesus climbed into Peter’s boat after a night of catching nothing and told him to go back out to the deep water and let down the nets. Peter said, in effect, “This makes absolutely no sense to me, but because you say so, I’ll let down the nets.” The nets broke, the boats began to sink, Peter fell at Jesus’ feet, and Luke tells us they pulled their boats up on shore, left everything, and followed him.
    • We read that and respond the way we think we should: “Amen. What faith. What devotion.” But Josh asks the church to imagine actually doing it. Peter has a business, employees who depend on a paycheck to feed their families, partners with families, and a family of his own. He grew up learning the trade to provide, and he leaves it.
    • The question Josh has been thinking about all week: “Peter, is he worth it? You left everything, man. Is he worth everything?” Following Jesus sounds great when he is filling the boat with fish. What happens when the boat is empty, when following Jesus costs you something, when obedience results in less fish instead of more?

  2. 02:04 Luke 6: empty hands reaching toward Christ

    Scripture: Luke 6 Luke 6:19

    • That question brings us to Luke 6, where Jesus tells people from all over, including his disciples, something that overturns what many would call the good life. Josh sets the scene with verse 19: the people all tried to touch him because power was coming from him and healing them all.
    • Picture it: Jesus surrounded by needy, sick, hurting, desperate people, reaching for him because he has something they need. Josh asks the church to picture all those empty hands reaching toward an all-sufficient Christ. Then Luke says Jesus makes eye contact with his disciples and speaks.

  3. 02:59 The blessings and the woes

    Scripture: Luke 6:20–26

    • Josh reads Jesus’ words: blessed are you who are poor, who hunger now, who weep now, and when people hate, exclude, insult, and reject your name as evil because of the Son of Man; rejoice and leap for joy, for great is your reward in heaven. Then the woes: woe to you who are rich, who are well fed now, who laugh now, and when everyone speaks well of you.

  4. 03:48 Luke draws up a T-chart

    • Luke draws up a T-chart. The world says the blessed person is the person who doesn’t need anything, and Jesus is surrounded by people who know they need something. To have a heavenly view of the good life, we have to reverse our thinking.
    • Your need doesn’t disqualify you from Jesus. The greatest danger comes when we are full, comfortable, and so self-sufficient that we no longer believe we need him. After all, why would you receive a new king when you already live in a palace?

  5. 04:28 How we use the word “blessed”

    Scripture: James 1:17

    • If the church were asked by push notification, “How are you blessed?”, most would point to family, health, job, house, country, food on the table, money in the bank, and good friends. Those are blessings; James tells us every good and perfect gift comes from above, so thank God for them. But when Jesus describes the blessed life, he says poor, hungry, weeping, hated, rejected.
    • Josh is off social media, but he didn’t see a lot of people posting an eviction notice on Instagram with “#blessed,” or leave the doctor’s office after devastating news and decide it needed a gratitude post with Brandon Lake music. That isn’t how we use the word.
    • Usually “God has blessed me” means things are going the way I want them to right now, and we should thank God when they do. The problem is when they don’t. Most Christians don’t need a sermon on how to rejoice when God gives; we need constant reminders on how to rejoice when God takes.
    • If blessing means good circumstances, does God stop blessing when circumstances turn? If I’m blessed because the test came back negative, what do I tell the person with a positive test? If I’m blessed because my business succeeded, what do I tell the faithful Christian whose business failed? Our use of the word is flimsy.

    1. 06:33 A definition of blessing that survives suffering

      • Jesus looks his disciples in the eye because those who will fulfill the Great Commission and launch his church cannot miss this. He gives them a definition of blessing that will survive suffering, because if blessing can’t survive suffering, there is no Crossroads Church in Anthem, Arizona in 2026.

  6. 07:02 Jesus is talking to his disciples

    • Who does Luke say Jesus is looking at? His disciples: people who belong to him. He is not giving everyone a four-point sermon on how to get into the kingdom: step one, become poor; step two, get hungry; step three, start crying; step four, make everybody hate you. That would be ridiculous.
    • This isn’t a gospel presentation; it is a perseverance presentation. Poverty doesn’t save you, hunger doesn’t save you, suffering doesn’t save you. Jesus is describing the blessing of people who already belong to the king.
    • Think how crucial that was for Peter, who had just walked away from everything. Jesus is discipling Peter into a kingdom mindset so he can launch the church. If Peter, walking away from the boat, still needed to change his thinking about what it means to be blessed, so do we.

  7. 08:29 Verse 20: blessing is about who possesses you

    Scripture: Luke 6:20

    • Josh returns to verse 20. Greek words typically have multiple meanings, at least five, which only becomes a problem when someone takes one and teaches it as the only definition. We hear “blessed” and think “happy,” and that is one meaning, but it is bigger than that. Jesus isn’t saying “happy are the poor” or speaking to the emotional state of people living in poverty.
    • We call people blessed by inspecting what surrounds them. Jesus determines blessing by looking at who possesses the person. We think of happiness as possessions, position, pleasure, and popularity; Jesus flips that upside down and says those things can never substitute for a relationship with God through Jesus Christ.
    • If blessing depends on what I have, I can lose it. The market can take money; disease and death can take health; people, or I, can ruin a reputation; failure can take success. But what if being blessed isn’t a gift God gives me? What if the blessing is God himself?

  8. 10:17 The beatific vision

    Scripture: Revelation 21–22 Revelation 22:4 Revelation 12:11

    • Reversing that mindset is one of the most beautiful things in the world. One of the great doctrines of Christian history is the beatific vision. Over the years Christians realized they all shared a common ultimate hope in God and gave it a name: we will see him, know him, and enjoy his presence without sin, death, disease, separation, or anything standing between us and the one who made us.
    • The apostle John shares this vision throughout Revelation, especially chapters 21 and 22. Josh’s homework: go read both chapters, not to inform your eschatology, but to see King Jesus.
    • He shows one phrase he thinks summarizes the entire book: “They will see his face.” Who are “they”? Those who overcome by the word of their testimony and the blood of Jesus.
    • Heaven isn’t glorious because the streets are gold, or because it is shaped like a cube and we wonder how gravity works, or even because suffering is gone and what is broken in your body will work again, though we praise God for those things. Take Christ out of heaven and heaven isn’t heaven anymore, because the greatest gift God can give you is God.

  9. 12:26 “Yours is the kingdom of God”

    Scripture: Luke 6:20

    • With the beatific vision in mind, Luke 6 begins to make sense. Blessed are you who are poor, not because being poor is wonderful, but because yours is the kingdom of God. You may not have anything to your name, but you have a king. You may possess very little in this world, but the kingdom of God belongs to you.

  10. 12:54 Jesus confronts our comforts

    • Last week Josh asked who had been comforted by Jesus and who had been confronted by him. This week Jesus is going to confront our comforts.
    • Don’t miss the caution tape: you can spend your entire life asking God to give you stuff and never realize that he is the treasure. Bless the career, the marriage, the kids, the finances, the plans, the future, the church. We should pray for, desire, and believe God for these things. But what if he doesn’t? What if something you already thanked him for is taken, or following Jesus costs you something? Are you still singing? Is God still good? Is Jesus still enough? Are you still blessed?

    1. 14:07 Jesus cares for the poor, but poverty doesn’t earn heaven

      Scripture: Luke 6:20

      • When Jesus says “Blessed are you who are poor,” he never ignores physical needs. Josh points to what Mike had just shared: the heart of this church is to meet needs, because that is what Jesus was about. He heals, provides, steps into messy lives, and shows mercy. Luke shows again and again that Jesus cares about the poor, and because he does, we should too.
      • But an empty bank account doesn’t earn someone heaven. Poverty isn’t a prerequisite for heaven, and wealth isn’t a fast pass to hell: Judas was broke, and Zacchaeus was rolling in it. The deeper question is where your security is. What brushes away your anxiety? What could God take from you that would make you believe he was no longer enough?
      • Before anyone tosses out the church answer they know how to give, Josh asks them to sit with that, and instead of letting him explain the passage, to let Jesus read them today.

  11. 15:26 Two columns, two value systems: now and later

    Scripture: Luke 6:21 Luke 6:25

    • Josh puts the four pairs on a T-chart and asks which side people would pick without being told what Jesus calls blessed. The right side: money instead of poverty, a full stomach instead of hunger, laughter instead of tears, respect instead of rejection. That sounds like the good life.
    • We are walking a tightrope between two value systems: a life built on what I can possess now, and a life built around the kingdom of God later. Not the candy squares. The “now” prizes power, comfort, success, and recognition; the “later” doesn’t require any of that to possess the good life.
    • The word “now” defines a temporary existence: blessed are you who hunger now, who weep now; woe to you who are well fed now, who laugh now. Jesus is teaching his disciples to evaluate their lives with eternity in view. The world says now is everything; Jesus says there’s a later.
    • Josh has seen many Christians robbed of joy here. If you forget the not-yet of Christianity, you will spend your entire life trying to make now feel like heaven, and only one event will turn now into heaven: when King Jesus splits the skies.

  12. 17:45 “Blessed are you who hunger now”

    Scripture: Luke 6:21

    • Hunger isn’t necessarily holiness, but it is a useful tool unto holiness. Hunger accomplishes something fullness never could: it reminds you that you need something, while fullness can make you forget.
    • Josh remembers a restaurant from growing up that served bread in flower pots, and asks the room whether they are top-bread or bottom-bread people. By the time the food arrives you have wrecked three pots of bread. He argues appetizers are the worst invention ever: food to warm up for food, at the price of a meal. His wife loves them, gets too full to eat her meal, boxes it to take home for lunch, and guess who ends up eating it.
    • Many of us do the same thing spiritually. We are full of entertainment, possessions, schedules, soccer leagues, and Netflix. We stuff our faces with the appetizers of this world and lose our appetite for God.
    • Remember the crowd reaching for Jesus because they needed what only he could give. The danger isn’t having nothing to give Jesus. The danger is becoming convinced Jesus has nothing you need.

  13. 20:11 “Blessed are you who weep now, for you will laugh”

    Scripture: Luke 6:21

    • Christianity isn’t misery. Josh is having fun and enjoying the Christian walk; this isn’t the Christian Oscars with an award for the most miserable-looking Christian. The Christian life has laughter and joy.
    • Laugh at what? Reading Luke from this side of the resurrection, how can we not hear these words in light of an empty tomb? It is like watching a movie when you already know how it ends: the conflict is real and the painful scenes still hit, but knowing the ending changes how you feel throughout. Christian, you know how this ends.
    • There will be funerals, goodbyes that come too soon, diagnoses, betrayals, and nights of questions without answers. Jesus never promised an easy life, but he did promise your tears have an expiration date. In light of eternity and his victory over the grave, how can we not laugh? Josh laughs at himself for worrying: “He empties graves, bro. Stop.”

    1. 22:05 For those who are hurting today

      • Some walked in hurting, exhausted, at the end of the rope, grieving, looking around at lives that seem full while theirs feels empty. Stop the comparisons, Josh says, and start listening to Jesus. Your circumstances don’t determine how God looks at you; Jesus is your advocate and represents you. This isn’t a sermon from Josh; it is a sermon from our Savior.
      • Josh knows people in the church who were homeless and experienced poverty, hunger, nowhere to lay their head, tears, and rejection. But Jesus says blessed: you have all you need in the resurrected Christ.

  14. 23:01 “Because of the Son of Man”

    Scripture: Luke 6:22

    • Jesus isn’t finished: blessed are you when people hate you, exclude you, insult you, and reject your name as evil, because of the Son of Man. That last phrase is the qualifier for all that came before.
    • If people don’t like you because you are a jerk, arrogant, a diva who only talks about yourself, or someone who thinks you and your kids can do no wrong and always points the finger, that hatred is your problem. Don’t put that on this text.

    1. 23:47 Unconditional love isn’t unconditional approval

      • Often love requires us not to approve. In the counseling Josh does with Kristen, he tells parents who don’t discipline their kids that they may love their kids, but not as much as they love themselves, or they would discipline them; they just don’t want the hardship. Don’t dress that up as love.
      • If something is destroying you, affirming it isn’t love. Love tells the truth, not to condemn or take the high ground, but because it desperately wants you rescued. We don’t tell the truth instead of loving people; we tell the truth because we love people. Sometimes people don’t like Christians because we are faithful to that and to Jesus, and allegiance to Jesus will cost approval.

    2. 25:00 Whose approval matters most?

      Scripture: Luke 6:23

      • We want people to like us. Josh is 40 and still wants his dad to be proud of him. But following Jesus will force one question: whose approval matters most? If theirs matters most, it is only a matter of time before God’s matters less.
      • Josh is clear up front: he is more concerned with disappointing God than disappointing the church. He will call out selfishness. He will call abortion murder in the face of an angry mob, and also counsel the woman in the grace of God. He will call homosexuality a sin in the face of the mob, and also say the murderer can be redeemed on death row; whether you are 80 or 8, you can experience the life-giving mercy of Jesus Christ. He will always tell the homosexual he can repent and choose celibacy. He will call out sin and always call the sinner to repentance.
      • If they hate him for it, he will remember Jesus’ words: rejoice in that day and leap for joy, for great is your reward in heaven. You can rejoice when they reject you. Whatever title this world gives you, poor, hungry, weeping, hated, insulted, none of them gets the final word. The king does.

  15. 26:47 “Woe to you who are rich”

    Scripture: Luke 6:24

    • For some, the question to wrestle with is different: what if this is all you get? What if now is as good as it gets? Josh reads verse 24 and calls it the second scariest verse in all Scripture. Any time you see “woe,” slam on the brakes; Jesus is warning us.
    • Don’t turn this verse into a civil war between rich and poor, or Jesus into a campaign surrogate for your preferred party. He isn’t giving an economic platform on capitalism versus socialism. Josh is grateful to live in America, blessed with extraordinary prosperity, maybe too much, which has enabled Christians and churches to give, send missionaries, feed people, build hospitals, fund ministries, respond to disasters, and carry the gospel worldwide. He suspects the church is winning that comparison with government, and invites a fact check.
    • Prosperity can be an incredible gift, but it can also be spiritually dangerous. The problem isn’t having things; the problem is when things have you.

    1. 28:26 “You have already received your comfort”

      • There is something horrifying about choosing this world as your portion and treasure, enjoying it, and then discovering this is it. Imagine getting everything: the career, the money, the house, the kids you are proud of, the retirement you planned, admirers around you at the end, and then Jesus says, “Hi, I hope you enjoyed your comfort.” That is terrifying.
      • People think the nightmare is not getting what they want. What if the nightmare is getting everything you want?

  16. 29:36 The rich fool in Luke 12

    Scripture: Luke 12:18–21

    • Jesus tells a story in Luke 12 about a man whose land produces so much he runs out of storage, so he plans to tear down his barns, build bigger ones, and tell himself to take life easy: eat, drink, and be merry. That sounds like the good life. But God said, “You fool, this very night your life will be demanded from you,” and Jesus says this is how it will be for whoever stores up things for themselves but is not rich toward God.
    • Covetousness is a nasty sin we don’t talk about much; we kind of forget the tenth one. It is an unquenchable thirst for more of something we think we need to be truly satisfied. The man’s issue wasn’t the barns or a lavish savings account; it was that the barns were never enough. He prepared for retirement but never considered a legacy or eternity.
    • Josh recommends a favorite song, a little old and Christian rap, by Beautiful Eulogy called “If.” Its question: what if God takes away the things I call blessings, the money, the career, the marriage, my spouse, my child, my health, or doesn’t fix what I’ve asked him to fix? Is he still good? Is he still enough?

  17. 31:19 Let Jesus read what makes you feel secure

    • Josh invites the church to let Jesus open them up: What makes you feel secure? What could make your life better? What are you hungry for, afraid to lose, holding so tightly you refuse to let go? What is your hidden treasure? What do you truly desire?
    • If your definition of being blessed requires God to give you something, God isn’t your treasure; the life you want is your treasure, and you are using God as a means to get it.
    • It is a scary text, and everyone must examine themselves. If God took the comfort, the applause, the influence, the money; if he doesn’t remove the cancer, the relationship ends, or obedience thins the crowd, would you still worship? Would you still have a song? Or would you say, “God, after everything I’ve done for you, how could you do this to me?”
    • If losing the gift makes you believe God failed you or owes you more, it reveals that somewhere you stopped worshiping God as your treasure and started worshiping him because he gives you treasures. It is one thing to say Jesus is enough when the marriage is good, the kids are healthy, and the bills are paid; it is another when things start disappearing.

  18. 33:02 Enjoy the gifts, but never turn the gift into a god

    • You came into this world with nothing, and you will leave the same. Josh isn’t shaming anyone for loving God’s good gifts. He loves his wife with a furious love and his kids so much it hurts; when a boy comes along to date his daughter, all he can think is, “I loved her first. So watch it.”
    • Enjoy God’s gifts, protect what you love, thank him, laugh, celebrate, and love your family, but never turn the gift into a god. The gifts are temporary; the giver is eternal.
    • If he gives, worship him; if he takes, worship him; if the answer is yes or no, worship him. Loss hurts, but we never lose hope. Christians grieve, just not like everyone else. Under every changing circumstance stands an unchanging Christ, and if you have Jesus, you still have your treasure and everything you need.

  19. 34:59 When we don’t like what we see, look at Christ

    • Closing pastorally, Josh says that when we truly examine ourselves we don’t always like what we see. Some may discover they have fallen in love with the gifts more than the God who gave them. That isn’t okay, and they are right to be convicted. Often we don’t just want Jesus; we want Jesus and comfort, success, approval, security, and our plans, and when those are threatened we discover how attached we are.
    • What is the response? Sell everything to prove money has no hold? Beat ourselves up black and blue? No. When things are out of balance, we stop looking in the mirror and look at Christ. The good news isn’t that if you look deep enough into your heart you will find something wonderful. The good news is that when you finally realize how poor you are, there is a king who welcomes the poor, who have nothing to offer and reach out with empty hands.

    1. 36:34 The king became poor

      • The king of heaven doesn’t only welcome the poor; he became poor. The one through whom everything was created entered his creation in the humblest circumstances. He knew hunger, tears, rejection, and homelessness, with no place to lay his head. Crowds praised him until they screamed “Crucify him”; his friends abandoned him; he was mocked, beaten, spit on, rejected, and hung on a cross.
      • On the cross Jesus doesn’t just show sacrifice; he makes atonement, a propitiation, and takes our place. Christ swaps positions with us, taking the rejection sinners deserve so that those united to him by faith are accepted before God. This exchange is the special boots you need to walk in the upside-down kingdom.
      • He became poor so that in him you could inherit a kingdom. He entered sorrow so that your tears would one day expire. He was rejected so that you could be welcomed. He bore our sin, guilt, greed, pride, idolatry, self-sufficiency, covetousness, worship of created things, need for human approval, and rebellion against the holy king. The judgment we deserved fell on Christ; Jesus bore our woe so sinners could say, “No matter what happens, we are blessed.”
      • Three days later the tomb was empty. Our hope isn’t that things will probably work out or life will get easier; it is a crucified and risen king. If you belong to him, the kingdom is yours because the king is yours: your portion, your treasure, your reward.

    2. 38:44 Open hands

      • That is why Christians can hold the things of this world with open hands: not because they don’t matter, but because they aren’t everything. No matter what loss we grieve, we can heal, because we never have to grieve the loss of Christ.
      • Life may not look like you thought it would. Josh has had evenings looking up and asking, “God, where is the blessing in this?” Maybe you are weeping now: you will laugh again. Maybe you are hungry: you will be satisfied. Maybe you have lost relationships because you would rather disappoint them than disappoint Christ: your reward is great. You might not have much to your name, but if you are in Christ, there is a kingdom Jesus says is yours.

  20. 40:14 To the unbeliever and the skeptic

    • Josh speaks to the unbeliever and skeptic. He is glad they are here, and he has been praying all week that the Lord’s words would scare them, which he insists is the nicest prayer anyone could pray for them. Some already know Jesus isn’t their treasure; their life, success, family, career, or what they call freedom is.
    • Some in the room are beneficiaries of God’s common grace but won’t give God credit. Josh is specific: he has seen men who don’t love Jesus benefiting from God’s work by dating or marrying women who do. It drives him insane as a father and a pastor. She is wonderful because Jesus is wonderful. You want God’s gifts more than you want God, and you will never be for her what she is for you unless Jesus becomes your treasure. Women, you don’t need to settle.
    • To these men: repent of being lord of your own life and of asking this world to give what only Christ can give. Stop trusting in your goodness, abilities, success, and religion, and in your freedom, if that is what you want to call it; Josh thinks it is a mirage. Put your faith in the king who lived the life you couldn’t live, died the death your sin deserved, and rose from the grave.

    1. 42:17 A prayer anyone can pray

      • You don’t need impressive words. Faith can pray: “Jesus, I’ve made your gifts into gods. I don’t want created things; I want you. I want you to be my treasure. Will you have me?” He will have you.
      • If you aren’t there yet, start smaller: “Jesus, I want to want you. Help me.” Don’t hide your unbelief; bring him your poverty, emptiness, and sin, and ask him to help you be thirsty, because only the thirsty drink from the fountain of life.
      • Remember the scene in Luke 6: needy people reaching toward Jesus because he had what they didn’t. King Jesus isn’t waiting for you to get things together or to have full hands. Come with empty hands now.

    2. 43:25 Follow him publicly

      • If today is the day you are ready to trust Christ as Lord and Savior, don’t hide it. Jesus calls his people to follow him publicly, and the waters are ready; if you are ashamed of him now, he said he will be ashamed of you later. Josh invites people to talk with him about baptism and what it means to make Jesus not only Savior but Lord.
      • Christianity isn’t adding a sprinkle of faith to a life you have already organized. It is surrendering before the king and saying, “My life belongs to you.”

  21. 44:01 Back to the beach: was he worth it?

    Scripture: Philippians 1:21

    • Picture Peter, James, and John pulling the boats on shore for the last time, leaving everything to follow Jesus. Back to the question: Peter, was he worth it? Peter’s life wasn’t going to get easier: failure, heartbreak, persecution, tears, even execution. But Peter got Jesus, and Jesus was teaching his disciples that everything they would face would be worth it.
    • The good life isn’t the absence of pain, trouble, or even loss. The good life is a life with Jesus. If he gives, worship; if he takes, worship him. Full or hungry, laughing or weeping, loved or hated, worship him, and never stop, because he is worthy of it all and no applause on earth compares to approval from the king of heaven.
    • Remember the beatific vision: one day your faith will become sight, we will see his face, and it will all be worth it. To live is Christ; to die is gain. The greatest gift God could ever give us is himself. That is the good life.

  22. 45:46 Prayer before the Lord’s Table

    • Josh prays as the church comes to the table. He asks anyone not yet trusting Christ to let the meal pass, since it is for believers who have made Jesus their portion, and urges believers who have gotten sideways to look down at the bread before taking it, considering his body broken and his blood poured out for the forgiveness of sins, and tell him, “Lord, you’re my portion. You’re my treasure.”
    • He calls them to repent, drop other treasures at his feet, return to walking with him as when they first felt his presence, and walk out with a new treasure, holding on to it like rubies. They come to the table to worship and lift up their lives: “Have your way. We want your will to be done.”
