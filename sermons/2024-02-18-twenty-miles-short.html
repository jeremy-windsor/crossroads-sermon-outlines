Overview

  1. 00:10 A book about dysfunctional families

    • Years ago Steve’s publisher asked him to write a book about dysfunctional families just when his own family was going through craziness. He accepted, only to discover that a huge slice of his research came from pulling on the branches of his own family tree.
    • Part of that research was sitting down with a therapist (“not for me, obviously”), who introduced him to the genogram, a family tree on steroids that also tracks diseases, dysfunctions, and disorders, and urged him to write one for his family.

    1. 01:26 What Steve’s genogram showed

      • It was a gut punch. One branch has a dad who sexually abused all three of his kids; another has leaf after leaf of alcohol and drug abuse, DWIs, rehab, jail, and arrests. There is a nephew who lived openly homosexual back when that could get you killed, teenage pregnancy, abortion, porn addiction, chronic drinking, and a branch with an 85 percent divorce rate across three generations.
      • Beyond the scandalous there is the tragic: a triple amputee, a kidney transplant, blindness, chronic depression, bipolar disorder, panic disorders, multiple suicides, two Gold Star families, Down syndrome, and OCD. There was hardly any white space left, and Steve realized the family he deeply loves is “one hot mess, and some of that mess is me.”

  2. 03:39 Was any family ever not a hot mess?

    • Writing Stuck in a Small World, Steve needed to go back farther than three generations to find out whether there was ever a time when doing family wasn’t a hot mess.
    • He suspects some have already started their own genogram, and it isn’t pretty either. If you don’t want to do one, that tells him your clan “puts the funk in dysfunction.” But before you die in a pile: your messed-up family is way more biblical than you might imagine.

  3. 04:43 The human family has always been a train wreck

    • Taking the generations back to the beginning, Steve found the human family has always been a train wreck with more twists than a Wetzel’s Pretzel.
    • Earth’s first family: Eve led her husband into disobeying God’s direct command, then their older son killed the younger because he thought God liked him better, and Cain became “the first face on a milk carton.” Noah’s family was saved from global catastrophe, and Noah thanked God by getting drunk and naked; Ham blabbed, Noah cursed him, and “Ham skipped Thanksgiving that year.”
    • Abraham was called God’s friend and the father of all who believe, but his family was a fiasco too: when Sarah couldn’t conceive, Abraham slept with a slave girl at Sarah’s suggestion, and then Sarah kicked Hagar and Ishmael off the family plantation. Barely out of the gate, the human genogram includes murder, infidelity, drunkenness, half brothers, mistresses, neglect, and abuse, and Ishmael’s legacy of anger is why the Middle East is on fire today.

  4. 07:13 Isaac breaks the family chain

    Scripture: Genesis 25:21

    • Peek behind the tent flaps of Isaac, the favored and promised son, and there is more of the same pain. He married at 40 a drop-dead gorgeous woman just like his mama, and just like Mama, Rebecca couldn’t have babies.
    • Thankfully Isaac didn’t repeat his father’s sin with Hagar. Instead he prayed to the Lord on behalf of his wife: he didn’t seek a substitute under the guise of helping God; he put his hand on his wife’s belly and said, “God, give us a baby.”

    1. 08:35 An explanation isn’t an excuse

      • It is practically a proverb: if you were harmed by your parents’ foolish sins, chances are good you will do the same to your kids. Your mom got pregnant by her boyfriend, and there you go doing the same thing; your dad drank and broke things and hit people, and now he is you.
      • There are environmental factors, such as those who abuse likely having been abused. But while environment may explain you, if explaining is all you care to do, your explanation is just an excuse. Isaac refused to do it the way his daddy did: “Daddy played, Isaac prayed.”

  5. 09:51 Two nations in her womb

    Scripture: Genesis 25:21–23 Genesis 25:24–26

    • The Lord answered, and Rebecca got pregnant with twins who jostled each other; one translation says wrestled, another kicked. It wasn’t fun for her: “Lord, why is this happening to me? I’m a first-time mama. Is this how this goes?”
    • God said two nations were in her womb, one stronger than the other, and the older would serve the younger. In other words, the conflict was just getting started: through Jacob would come Israel, and this family feud had just begun.
    • The first boy came out red and hairy like a garment, “so they named him Elmo; I mean, Esau.” The second came out grasping Esau’s heel and was named Jacob, which means trickster, “and as we shall see, his name fit.”

  6. 11:17 Two hints of a family that went nuclear

    Scripture: Genesis 25:27

    • Verse 27 gives the first of two hints why this nuclear family went nuclear. Esau was a skillful hunter, a man of the open country: picture a gun-toting, F-350-driving, Duck Dynasty man’s man with a hairy chest, an even hairier back, and a taste for wild game: “Slay it, filet it, buffet it.”
    • Jacob was his total opposite, a quiet man content to stay among the tents, probably streaming the cooking shows, whose favorite thing was puttering around the kitchen with Mama.

    1. 12:32 When affinity becomes favoritism

      Scripture: Genesis 25:28

      • The second hint: Isaac loved Esau, but Rebecca loved Jacob. Isaac saw himself in Esau, and that affinity was fine. But when affinity becomes favoritism you cross a line, and both parents crossed it. Different doesn’t mean better; the spark that lit this family fire was when both parents picked sides.

    2. 13:39 Your kids aren’t you

      • Steve asks parents to realize how selfish it is to expect kids to be just like them. One is creative, another aggressive; one intelligent, another athletic; you want football and he prefers guitar. It is wonderful that God creates each little one to be his own unique self.
      • Jacob didn’t take to what Daddy loved, so Daddy lost interest; the same happened with Rebecca and Esau. Both boys were damaged, especially Esau, who spent his life acting out because he never heard the words he desperately needed to hear.

  7. 14:46 A birthright for a bowl of stew

    Scripture: Genesis 25:29–34

    • Jacob was cooking stew when famished Esau came in from the open country: “Quick, let me have some of that red stew.” Jacob, always working the angles, said, “First sell me your birthright.” Esau said, “I’m about to die. What good is this birthright if I’m dead?”
    • He wasn’t that hungry. Steve tells him to dial back the drama, cook his own grub, or grab some goat jerky. Teenage boys don’t have discriminating palates; Steve’s son was a walking garbage disposal.

    1. 16:13 What Esau gave away

      Scripture: Genesis 25:23

      • The stakes were so colossal Esau should have walked. The birthright, reserved for the oldest son, meant twice the inheritance, the family name, becoming patriarch, a special prophetic promise before the father died, and, in a family waiting for the Messiah, a place in the lineage of the promised one. Esau traded it all for a bowl of soup.
      • Jacob takes his impulsive brother to the cleaners, though it was unnecessary: God had already promised the older would serve the younger. “So why is he being a jerk?” Esau swore an oath, “what a meathead,” trading shekels, land, the family name, and the bloodline of Messiah for soup, plus some bread. He ate, drank, and despised his birthright: “What I know now is I am hungry.”

    2. 18:17 Not deception, a bad deal

      Scripture: Hebrews 12:16

      • Some say Jacob deceived his brother, but this part isn’t deception; Esau cut a bad deal and knew the score. Jacob was a jerk, but the takeaway is that Esau recklessly despised God’s eternally significant blessing to satisfy a fleeting physical impulse. “Make note of that, men.” He traded his birthright for his belly, which is why Hebrews 12:16 calls him profane.

  8. 19:11 Chapter 27: the blessing

    Scripture: Genesis 25 Genesis 27 Genesis 27:1–4

    • Chapter 25 is about the birthright; chapter 27 is about the blessing. Old and nearly blind, Isaac calls Esau, his older and favorite son: “I don’t know the day of my death.” (He would live another 80 years, but once parts start breaking down you start wondering.) Go hunt wild game, prepare me tasty food, and I will give you my blessing before I die.
    • The blessing wasn’t wishful thinking but a prophetic, God-inspired look into the future, and God had already clearly promised it to Jacob.

    1. 20:16 Father and son, co-conspirators against God

      • The birthright and the blessing are two sides of the same coin. When Esau swore the oath, he gave away the blessing too, and both father and son knew it, making them co-conspirators against God.
      • How does Steve know? The blessing was always a huge family celebration, like a wedding, yet they are sneaking around planning a secret ceremony. Isaac set in motion a scheme that would forever divide, deceive, and destroy his family.

  9. 21:15 Rebecca was listening

    Scripture: Genesis 27:5–10

    • Meanwhile Rebecca was listening on the other side of the tent flap, “probably recording the whole thing on her iPhone.” If Jacob had issues, wonder why.
    • Rebecca tells Jacob, “Your daddy’s trying to give to Esau what God said belongs to you. Listen carefully and do what I tell you.” These people didn’t even try to hide it.

    1. 22:22 “Do what I tell you”

      • Jacob is in his mid-40s, and Mama says, “Do what I tell you.” Steve remembers his dad’s methods of corporal punishment and his mom’s paddle-ball paddle: the rubber string broke within an hour, “so I lost a toy, but Mom gained a weapon.” It worked at five, but at 13 he laughed while she kept swinging. There comes a time when parental tactics have to change.

    2. 23:38 Jacob can’t say no to Mama

      • Having never had a healthy bond with his dad, Jacob had to keep the only decent relationship he had intact. He must have wondered why, if God promised him the birthright, they had to pull these stunts instead of letting God work it out. But, like The Waterboy, “Mama said.”
      • Mama said to bring two choice goats so she could make Isaac’s favorite food and Jacob could take it to him for the blessing.

  10. 24:39 Confession isn’t repentance

    Scripture: Genesis 27:11–12 Genesis 27:13

    • Jacob isn’t sure: his brother is hairy like Chewbacca, and he has smooth porcelain skin and SPF 4000. “What if father touches me? I would appear to be tricking him.” Appear? Of course he was tricking him.
    • When Jacob admits the plan is sketchy, he isn’t repenting, just confessing. He is still going to do it; he is only worried about the repercussions. Confessing a sin you still plan to do just says, “I hope I don’t get caught.” Repentance admits what I want to do is wrong, so I stop, drop, and stroll the other direction, living for the Lord instead of my own desires.
    • Jacob’s solution was to be sneakier: “Is there anything we can do to sin better?” Mama said, “Let the curse fall on me. Just do as I say.” Don’t bother with what God has said.

  11. 26:39 Goatskins and the hairiest man contest

    Scripture: Genesis 27:14–16

    • They are tricking a blind man. Rebecca puts Esau’s best clothes on Jacob, “he’s in his 40s and Mama is still dressing him,” and covers his hands and neck with goatskins. Big brother must have been one hairy dude.
    • On a book tour in Dallas, Steve went to a Mavericks game whose halftime featured a hairiest man contest. The winner, who would need a weed whacker on his back, ran to a cheerleader for a hug that wasn’t happening, and won free laser sessions. That’s Esau: so hairy Mama had to glue a goat to Jacob’s back.

  12. 28:17 Lies and blasphemy in Isaac’s tent

    Scripture: Genesis 27:17–20

    • Jacob goes in: “I am Esau your firstborn,” an outright lie; “I’ve done as you told me,” lie number two; “Sit up and eat some of my game,” lie number three. Asked how he found it so quickly, he says, “The Lord gave me success.” Now we’ve stumbled into blasphemy: “I just stood there and, thank you Jesus, meat.”
    • Have you ever sinned and then justified it because it worked? “God was in this, Daddy. Everything worked like a charm.”

    1. 29:26 The blessing given

      Scripture: Genesis 27:21–29

      • Isaac touches him: “The voice is the voice of Jacob, but the hands are the hands of Esau.” He eats, asks for a kiss, smells the clothes, and blesses him with heaven’s dew, grain and new wine, nations serving him, lordship over his brothers, and cursing and blessing on those who curse and bless him.
      • It is a prophetic promise. Isaac thinks he gave it to Esau, which would have been a sin, but Jacob tricked his dad into giving the blessing God had already promised.

  13. 30:46 Esau’s bitter cry

    Scripture: Genesis 27:30–36

    • Esau comes back with the grub; Isaac realizes he’s been duped and trembles violently, “I’d call it a panic attack.” Esau bursts out with a loud, bitter cry: “Bless me too.” Only one blessing per family, and it went to Jacob. Esau says, “Isn’t he rightly named Jacob? He took my birthright and now my blessing.”
    • Jacob was a snake, but the first time wasn’t deception, just a bad deal. This time it was deception, but wasn’t deception what Esau was planning too, by secretly stealing what God had promised Jacob?

    1. 31:57 Retelling the story as the only victim

      • Have you ever retold past events so you appear as the only victim? That’s Esau: “Jacob did this to me.” No, Steve says, you should never have attempted that stunt; grow up, hairy man. Had Esau never traded the birthright, he might never have lost the blessing. He had no one to blame but himself, yet he held a grudge the rest of his life, and this broken family never did heal.

  14. 32:44 The main thrust: Lord of all

    • What do we do with this? We could talk about family relationships, favoritism, sibling rivalry, or the family blessing, one of Steve’s favorite topics, but all of that is sidebar to the main thrust of the Genesis story.
    • Longtime missionary to China J. Hudson Taylor said, “Christ is either Lord of all or he is not Lord at all.” Steve agrees: you have either fully submitted to Jesus Christ or not bowed the knee at all. To say Jesus is Lord is to acknowledge he is the boss, in control, and you aren’t. Our Lord demands supremacy in everything, not just some things.

    1. 34:07 Compartmentalized lordship

      Scripture: Matthew 6:24

      • We try to compartmentalize Jesus’ lordship: as long as I declare it with my mouth, it’s all good, even with slippage in how I practice it with the rest of my body. But if Jesus is to have supremacy in all things, and he isn’t Lord of all, he isn’t Lord at all.
      • “Most everything else is in alignment, just this one little exception”: too much alcohol, certain chemicals, an affair, porn, flirting at the bar, lying to my wife and ignoring my kids. Compartmentalizing leads to duplicity, a distracted, divided mind, and Jesus said nobody can serve two masters. A divided life always seeps, and once a leak begins it is hard to get it back in the bottle. Making Jesus Lord of some parts but not all is dangerous and never works.

    2. 36:03 The Super Bowl quarterbacks

      Scripture: James 3:10

      • After the Super Bowl it was heartening to hear both quarterbacks profess faith in Christ, but Steve struggled because one routinely drops F-bombs as emphatically as his linemen. He isn’t saying it is an unforgivable sin, but the Bible says out of the same mouth come praise and cursing, and this ought not to be.
      • Some call that soft commitment. Hudson Taylor says it is a lordship problem; the apostle Paul says Christ does not have supremacy.

  15. 36:55 Jacob the con man on the run

    Scripture: Genesis 27:41 Genesis 28:10–11

    • Back to Jacob, who spent his whole life on the ragged edge: double dealing, scamming, the ultimate con man and flimflam fraud who taped goat parts to his neck. Esau watched and never forgot, and that bitter root is about to cause a world of trouble: “The days of mourning for my father are near, and then I’m going to kill my brother.”
    • Jacob and his father finally agree on something: Jacob needs to get out of Dodge. He leaves Beersheba for Haran and reaches a place so remote they didn’t even name it, “although someone did write a song: he went through the desert to a place with no name.” It shows how far Jacob had fallen; he went to nowhere.

    1. 38:24 A rock for a pillow and a famous dream

      Scripture: Genesis 28:13–15 Genesis 28:16–19 2 Timothy 2:13

      • Your life has hit bottom when you use rocks for pillows. But Jacob dreams, and God repeats the promise first given to Abraham, in spite of Jacob’s horrific duplicity. Steve reads it with emphasis: I will still give you the land, your descendants will still be like the dust of the earth, all peoples will still be blessed through you, I am still with you, I will still bring you back, and I will not leave you until I have done what I promised.
      • Jacob wakes, says the place is awesome, sets up a rock, and names it Bethel, the house of God. These events remind us that even when we are faithless, he is faithful.

  16. 40:00 The map: Bethel to Haran and back

    Scripture: Genesis 28:20–22

    • On a map Steve shows where Jacob receives the promise at Bethel, not far from Gaza, “where the world is on fire,” and returns a promise of his own: if God will be with me and bring me back safely to my father’s house, the Lord will be my God and this stone will be God’s house.
    • His destination is Haran in the far northeast. The deal at Bethel: God says, “If you go, I’ll bring you back”; Jacob says, “When I go, I will come back.” Twenty years later he does come back with wives and kids, just like God said. Except not exactly.

  17. 41:50 Twenty miles short

    Scripture: Genesis 33:18–20

    • After Jacob came from Paddan Aram he arrived safely at Shechem. Wasn’t he going to Bethel? He didn’t just camp; he bought land, pitched his tent, and set up an altar called El Elohe Israel. He had traveled all the way to Haran and all the way back, but Shechem isn’t Bethel. Shechem is 20 miles short of Bethel.

    1. 43:29 Jacob’s lifelong shortcut

      • Jacob did what he always had done: took a shortcut. Fake hair, a lowered voice, and God still blessed him and was still with him. He lived on the edge of obedience, it always seemed to work out, and he had good intentions but never did as he intended. “No harm, no foul. I got within 20 miles. That’s the general vicinity.”

    2. 44:21 What pleases the adversary

      Scripture: John 10:10

      • That thought process is what most pleases the adversary: the thought that God is okay with you as long as you are in the general vicinity of where he wants you. “I’m only compromising this. Just this, not everything.”
      • Satan’s stated mission is to steal, kill, and destroy you, but his first tactic is never to send you off the deep end into hating the church and renouncing the faith. His strategy is to get you to stop this short, because if you stop a little short this time, it will be even shorter next time.

    3. 45:26 The cost of Shechem

      • Jacob eventually figured it out, but, as the church will find out next time, not before his daughter was raped, his sons became murderers, and the family wealth was taken. Where did that happen? Shechem. It all happened because Jacob stopped just 20 miles short.

  18. 45:51 Where am I stopping short?

    Scripture: James 5:9

    • As the church bows and prepares for the Lord’s Supper, Steve asks the burning question: Where am I stopping short? It is in the small things we think aren’t a big deal, exactly what Jacob thought, and he had no idea what his decisions would cost him and others.
    • Steve believes the time is short and the coming of our Lord is at hand. The world is at a tipping point, and while he has no inside information, he believes it because Jesus said it, and the righteous judge is standing at the door. Wouldn’t you rather Jesus find you right in the center of his will than living just 20 miles short of where he wants you to be?
