Overview

  1. 00:35 “My family’s Christian and my grandparents are missionaries”

    • Studying the text this week, Steve was haunted by words he spoke as a ninth grader to his youth leader, Mr. Landis, who had asked, “How about you, Steve? Who do you say that Jesus is? How’s your relationship with him?” Steve answered that he had led a boys’ small group for two years, his family was Christian, and his grandparents were missionaries. What started slow and hesitant ended with pride and confidence: surely that was what Mr. Landis wanted to hear, so he could focus on the guys who lacked Steve’s upbringing.
    • He had never been more wrong. It was the late ’90s and he was sporting at least three “What Would Jesus Do” bracelets, but his thoughts betrayed him: “That was close; you’ve got to do better next time.” Better at what? The game, the show, the image, keeping everyone thinking all is good.
    • Steve opens with that memory because it is a clear example of how poorly he answered a question about Jesus, and because the text takes us to the most important question anyone will ever be asked.

  2. 02:34 Mark 8: the turning point

    Scripture: Mark 8

    • The primary text is Mark 8, mostly the second half. This is week five of the eight-week series Come Follow Me, and just past halfway, Mark’s gospel reaches its pinnacle and turning point: a shift from Jesus’ miracles to his ultimate mission, from crowds clamoring to see him to a few faithful followers, with the rest of the account under the shadow of the cross.
    • Steve introduces himself: he has served Crossroads students in grades 6–12 for the last four years and is on the church’s teaching team. He prays that God would help him proclaim the word boldly and open hearts, minds, and ears to be challenged, encouraged, and changed.

  3. 04:08 Mark’s emphasis on serving

    Scripture: Mark 10:45

    • Of the four gospels, Mark stands out for its emphasis on serving. He writes from the perspective of a servant of God and directs readers to their Savior as a suffering servant who loves others by serving even the least of these. Mark 10:45 summarizes the theme: even the Son of Man did not come to be served, but to serve and to give his life as a ransom for many.
    • For note-takers, Steve divides the study into three parts.

  4. 04:49 Part one: sight to be restored

    Scripture: Mark 8:22–23

    • Steve reads from verse 22: at Bethsaida some people bring a blind man and beg Jesus to touch him. Jesus leads him by the hand outside the village, spits on his eyes, puts his hands on him, and asks, “Do you see anything?”
    • All along Jesus has been showing how the in-breaking kingdom flips assumptions on their heads. You might expect him to do the miracle in front of everyone so more would believe and follow, but that isn’t his concern. He shows personal, intimate care for the sick, the disenfranchised, and now the blind. With the disciples likely present, the way he heals is intentional: he asks the man, “Do you see anything?” and very soon he will ask the disciples what they can see spiritually. This run-of-the-mill miracle sends ripples of truth that become waves of meaning in the next section.

    1. 06:14 A two-stage healing

      Scripture: Mark 8:24–26

      • In verse 24 the man says, “I see people; they look like trees walking around.” Jesus puts his hands on his eyes again, his sight is restored, and he sees everything clearly. Some wrongly suspect Jesus messed up or wasn’t strong enough to do it in one try. Far from it: it shows his full power under full control and teaches the gradual revelation of spiritual truth. The disciples had seen so much but still saw in part what he wanted them to understand in full.
      • Being left partly sighted could be annoying, like dilated eyes or migraine blind spots; the man might have preferred his original condition. But the point is that the disciples’ understanding of who Jesus is and what he came to do is still blurry, and that is often true of the church today. How many of us have set our focus on Jesus only to have it smudged by the things of the world?

    2. 08:00 “Now we see only a reflection”

      Scripture: 1 Corinthians 13:12 1 Corinthians 13:12

      • This reminds Steve of Paul in 1 Corinthians 13:12: now we see only a reflection as in a mirror, then face to face; now I know in part, then I shall know fully. The King James says we see through a glass darkly. This side of heaven we can’t see everything plainly, but we have the promise of complete clarity. The question is whether we pursue the truth and beauty we do have in Christ now.

    3. 09:00 From one degree of glory to another

      Scripture: 2 Corinthians 3:18

      • Jesus doesn’t want to leave us with partial vision or partial healing, or have half our hearts. Like the blind man, our transformation takes time: salvation is received all at once, but sanctification is gradual. One of Steve’s favorite passages is 2 Corinthians 3:18: beholding the glory of the Lord, we are being transformed into the same image from one degree of glory to another.
      • So the call to come follow me first requires seeing and following Jesus, becoming a disciple in the truth, desiring to be with him and be transformed into his image.

  5. 09:34 Part two: a question to be answered

    • Jesus and the disciples set off on a 25-mile journey, and Jesus will ask and share some heavy things. This is the bulk of the study.

    1. 09:51 On the way

      Scripture: Mark 8:27 Deuteronomy 6:4–7

      • Jesus wastes no time leading his closest followers; unlike the crowds, the disciples had 24/7 access. In verse 27, on the way to the villages around Caesarea Philippi, he asks, “Who do people say I am?”
      • Steve loves “on the way.” It recalls the Shema in Deuteronomy 6, Hebrew for “hear”: love the Lord your God with all your heart, soul, and strength; impress these commandments on your children; talk about them when you sit at home and when you walk along the road, when you lie down and when you get up. It is an expectation for parents, grandparents, and anyone who cares for the spiritual growth of those younger: make the things of God part of your regular rhythms. Jesus models it; he doesn’t travel silently saving his teaching for the next town (and a mic check, Philip). He redeems every opportunity.
      • To help parents, the youth ministry sends out a family discipleship guide every Thursday with a summary of what students are learning and questions to open discussion at the dinner table, on the drive to soccer practice, in the doctor’s waiting room, or in the school drop-off line, which is the longest.

    2. 12:11 Jesus asks questions

      • Jesus starts this conversation with a question. Steve didn’t count them himself, but Jesus is recorded asking 307 questions in the gospels, from “Do you want to be made well?” to “Why are you so afraid?”, to last week’s “Who touched me?”, to this chapter’s “Do you see anything?”
      • After 15 years in public education and four in youth ministry, Steve has learned the value of a good question; every Wednesday he carefully writes small group questions for students. We are an answer-driven society; some data shows the average person asks a search engine or AI 20 to 30 questions a week. But Jesus knows that letting people wrestle with a question is the best tool to transform hearts and minds.
      • Jesus isn’t interested in the answer to “Who do people say I am?”; he already knows it. It is a tool to get the disciples thinking, like asking students why most teens struggle with Bible reading, which takes the pressure off. The next question is the one that does the heart work: “What keeps you from God’s word?”

    3. 14:18 John the Baptist, Elijah, or one of the prophets

      Scripture: Mark 8:28 Mark 6:16 Malachi 4 John 1

      • Verse 28: some say John the Baptist, others Elijah, others one of the prophets. Herod perpetuated the first idea; having had John beheaded over a twisted promise to his stepdaughter, he heard of Jesus’ ministry, recorded in Mark 6, and said, “John, whom I beheaded, has been raised from the dead.” That wasn’t possible, since Jesus and his cousin John had overlapping ministries and John baptized him.
      • Elijah never died but went up in a chariot of fire, and the scripturally well versed held this view because Malachi 4 says Elijah must first appear to announce the Messiah. Steve finds it interesting that this is why those at the cross thought Jesus was crying for Elijah, possibly mocking him further as though Malachi’s prophecy had failed. But Jesus identified John the Baptist as a type of Elijah who would prepare the way of the Lord, in John 1.
      • The last rumor, one of the prophets, shows the people’s desire for a political reformer to set the wrongs right and bring Israel back to justice and peace. Something Steve noticed this week: Old Testament prophets began with “Thus says the Lord,” but Jesus began, “Truly I say unto you.” Jesus was much more than a prophet of old.

    4. 16:56 “But what about you? Who do you say I am?”

      Scripture: Mark 8:29

      • With the warm-up out of the way, Jesus asks the most important question in verse 29: “But what about you? Who do you say I am?” Peter answers, “You are the Messiah.” It is the most important question a person will ever answer: narrow, personal, and confrontational. Knowing what others thought was fine, but Jesus had to ask each of them what they believed.
      • Some translations say “the Christ.” The Greek Christos means the anointed one, the Messiah; it isn’t Jesus’ last name. Peter claims Jesus, Yeshua, “the Lord of salvation,” is the long-awaited Messiah, the Son of God, more than a national reformer, miracle worker, or prophet. Put together: others think you are a mighty, wise predecessor to the Messiah, but we know you are the Messiah himself.

    5. 18:31 Correct, but not yet complete

      Scripture: Acts 4:8–12

      • Peter’s answer is correct but not complete. The disciples knew Jesus’ divinity, sonship, and authority, but like the blind man their vision was unfinished; what that authority meant and what his fast-approaching mission was were still blurry, and the next section shows the painful process of seeing it clearly.
      • The good news is they would come to understand. In Acts, Peter, filled with the Holy Spirit, boldly proclaimed his revised answer to rulers and elders: the lame man stands healed by the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth, whom you crucified but God raised; he is the stone the builders rejected, and salvation is found in no one else.

    6. 19:48 Knowing about Jesus isn’t enough

      Scripture: James 2:19 Matthew 8:29

      • Peter’s sight would be made whole, but the question is asked of each of us, and it is more than having a good theological or academic answer in your back pocket. James warns that head knowledge isn’t enough: you believe there is one God; good, even the demons believe that and shudder. Legion knew Jesus’ name and nature: “What do we have to do with you, Son of God?” (Matthew 8:29).
      • Demons and Satan can truthfully answer who Jesus is; the enemy’s answer may be the crispest of all, since you can know a great deal about someone you despise. But that knowledge changes nothing without a desire to submit. Facts are surface level; someone could agree with every truth in Scripture and never know Jesus as Lord, Savior, Redeemer, and friend. That was the difference between the crowds and the true disciples 2,000 years ago, and it still exists in the American church, though the lines are muddy.

    7. 21:43 Why the answer matters: salvation rests on it

      Scripture: Acts 4:12 Romans 10:9

      • This answer is crucial because our salvation rests on it. As Peter said, there is salvation in no other name, so you’ve got to get this identity right. Salvation is by grace through faith in Christ alone.
      • The clearest description Steve knows is Romans 10:9: if you declare with your mouth “Jesus is Lord” and believe in your heart that God raised him from the dead, you will be saved. Two things: you declare that Jesus is something, and you believe he did something, and the “and” means both are required.
      • Steve used to think that sounded too easy; couldn’t any Joe Schmo off the street do that and be saved? Yes, and that is the beauty, simplicity, and power of the gospel. But honestly declaring Jesus is Lord means releasing the throne of your life to him, and believing God raised him means proclaiming that God was pleased with his Son’s sacrifice as sufficient to cover your sin and the sin of the whole world.
      • So the question cuts deeper: salvation depends on it, and a proper personal answer yields a further response. Knowing who I am, will you truly come follow me?

  6. 24:11 Part three: a cross to be taken up

    • Having helped the disciples see in part who he is, Jesus pushes the conversation deeper. This is the first of three times in Mark that he reveals the path of suffering he must endure.

    1. 24:27 “He spoke plainly about this”

      Scripture: Mark 8:31–33

      • Verse 31: the Son of Man must suffer many things, be rejected by the elders, chief priests, and teachers of the law, be killed, and after three days rise again. He spoke plainly, and Peter took him aside to rebuke him. Jesus, looking at his disciples, rebuked Peter: “Get behind me, Satan! You do not have in mind the concerns of God, but merely human concerns.”
      • “Plainly” means Jesus was frank and left nothing to interpretation. This was the first time the disciples heard such talk. A suffering servant? The Messiah was supposed to be strong, with the elders subject to him, not rejecting him. Amid the shock, Peter pulled Jesus aside; the Greek word for rebuke means to share disapproval or try to quiet down: “Jesus, what are you saying? This is crazy talk.”

    2. 25:46 “Get behind me, Satan”: the offer of a shortcut

      Scripture: Matthew 4:8–10 Isaiah 53:3–5

      • Why such an intense response? Satan had already shown his hand in the wilderness. After 40 days without food, Satan tempted Jesus three times, including Matthew 4:8–10: showing him all the kingdoms of the world, “All this I will give you if you will bow down and worship me.” Jesus said, “Away from me, Satan!”
      • Satan was offering a shortcut. Jesus knew his mission to seek and save the lost would walk the narrow road; he knew he was the promised Messiah, the reason for all his “musts.” He knew Isaiah said he must be despised and rejected, a man of suffering, pierced for our transgressions, crushed for our iniquities, and by his wounds we are healed. He had to fulfill every word to bear the sins of mankind, suffering and dying on the way to becoming king of the kingdom of God, not of the world.
      • So Satan offered the glory without the suffering for a little worship; Satan always wanted to be God, which is why he fell. But Jesus knew there were no shortcuts to the will of God. The Father had already promised him the real kingdom; the price would cost him everything, but the power was greater, the goal higher, and the victory sweeter.
      • Steve is so glad Jesus wasn’t tempted by momentary fame and glory, but followed God’s plan for three more years and gave it all up to endure the cross of shame, so that we could enter his joy and have unshakable hope.

    3. 28:41 Peter, a pawn in the enemy’s game

      • Back on the road to Caesarea Philippi, Peter is trying to get Jesus to skip the cross. His mind was on human concerns, mostly his own: what does this mean for me? He was acting as a pawn in the enemy’s game, because Satan hates the cross and knows the only way to defeat God’s plan of redemption is to get Jesus off the path to it.

    4. 29:13 The way of the cross for those who follow

      Scripture: Mark 8:34–35

      • What does the way of the cross look like for those who follow him? Verse 34: Jesus calls the crowd and the disciples: whoever wants to be my disciple must deny themselves, take up their cross, and follow me; whoever wants to save their life will lose it, but whoever loses their life for me and the gospel will save it.
      • The disciples likely imagined lives of favor and popularity once Jesus took control, with privileges and honor along the way. But he had been showing them an upside-down kingdom: the first shall be last, the weak strong, the poor truly rich, the persecuted blessed, to be free is to be a servant, and now, to save one’s life it must be counted as loss. The eternal rewards are beyond compare, but the cost here and now is set very high.
      • In The Cost of Discipleship, Dietrich Bonhoeffer writes that discipleship is not an offer man makes to Christ but an offer Christ makes to man, and that when Christ calls a man, he bids him come and die.

    5. 31:03 Deny yourself

      • The world has always been about self, but Jesus bids us deny ourselves, take up our cross, and follow him. Satan wants us to avoid the way of the cross, take shortcuts, and skip suffering, which leads to a Christless kingdom. This passage flies in the face of the “live your best life now” mentality in American churches: we want Jesus, but a lot of other things too, and will follow him as long as he contributes to our success and happiness.
      • Christians often hear “deny yourself” as denying sin and temptation, which we must do. But Jesus simply said, “Deny yourself”: you will be called to lay down your rights on the narrow road. What idols give you value and security? They can’t come with you. It isn’t settling for a mid house, car, and job and calling that self-denial.
      • Denying yourself is so much more: sometimes not saying what you want to say, sometimes saying what needs to be said despite the consequences; laying down your rights to control, perfection, calling the shots, and being right; letting go of your pride, dreams, and desires simply because they are yours. Your best-laid plans are best laid down at Jesus’ feet for the lasting hope only he can give. Your happiness is at stake, Jesus says, but he is the only one who can make your joy full.

    6. 33:42 The dentist’s voicemail

      • While writing this section Steve missed a call from his dentist’s office. Maybe it is a millennial thing, but he hates voicemail; send a text, an email, even a letter. The voicemail confirmed an appointment they had already texted and emailed about and asked him to call if his insurance had changed. It had, so he hatched a diabolical plan to call after hours and leave his group number one digit per voicemail. Then he realized that even in silly, trivial frustrations there is opportunity to deny yourself what you think is right or want to do.

    7. 34:56 Take up your cross

      Scripture: Matthew 19 Matthew 19:21–22

      • “Take up your cross” shows the extent of self-denial, even to death. If you were literally carrying your cross to crucifixion, your deal was sealed; there was no turning back, and nothing held back, just as Jesus held nothing back for us.
      • The same idea appears with the rich young ruler in Matthew 19, whose list of laws kept since youth reminds Steve of his answer to his leader. Jesus answered, “If you want to be perfect, go, sell your possessions and give to the poor,” implying all of them, “then come, follow me,” and the young man went away sad because he had great wealth.
      • True faith requires a willingness to surrender all at Christ’s bidding. Our struggle is wanting to hold on to something rather than surrender fully, but the only way to full joy and a grasp of Jesus is to release every other possession, physical, mental, or emotional, that would hinder the pursuit.

    8. 36:28 Gain the whole world, forfeit your soul

      Scripture: Mark 8:36–37 Mark 8:38 Romans 1:16

      • Jesus ends: what good is it to gain the whole world yet forfeit your soul? What can anyone give in exchange for their soul? If anyone is ashamed of me and my words in this adulterous and sinful generation, the Son of Man will be ashamed of them when he comes in his Father’s glory. Without Christ, it is possible to have everything in this world and still have nothing. The human soul is priceless to God, who desires all to come to a saving knowledge of him.
      • Paul riffs on not being ashamed in Romans 1:16: “I am not ashamed of the gospel, because it is the power of God that brings salvation to everyone who believes.”

    9. 37:31 Gospel culture: “fully, freely, forever forgiven”

      • The youth group just finished a four-week series called Gospel Culture on how the good news should transform us individually and together. Steve realized we can get so close to the gospel that we lose sight of its beauty, form, and function.
      • Many students have memorized this definition: the gospel is all your sins, past, present, future, fully, freely, forever forgiven in the person and work of Jesus Christ. It shows why denying self is to our eternal advantage even if not our earthly one: it matters who Jesus is and what he has done, and what the cross accomplished for those willing to take up theirs. Remove any one word, all, past, present, future, fully, freely, forever, and the good news becomes just news.

  7. 39:17 Mr. Landis, and who Jesus is to Steve now

    Scripture: Hebrews 13:7

    • We need our sight restored, each of us has a question to answer personally, and there is a cross to be taken up by anyone who would truly come follow Jesus.
    • As many suspected, Mr. Landis wasn’t thrilled with Steve’s freshman answer. Hebrews 13:7 tells us to remember our leaders who spoke the word of God to us, and Mr. Landis is one; Steve owes much of his spiritual growth to his faithfulness. He let Steve off the hook that night, but pushed and pursued him for the next four years until Steve could honestly declare who Jesus is, and his answer shifted from facts and other people’s relationships to firsthand experience. Following Jesus means discovering more on the way, and there is joy in the journey.
    • So how about you, Steve, who is Jesus to you? He is Lord, Savior, Redeemer, friend, the Son of God, the only one worthy of praise, time, and full pursuit, the one Steve will follow with all his life, who gives access with confidence before the throne, the bearer of his sin, remover of his shame, and lifter of his head.
    • Steve leaves two questions everyone must answer: Who do you say Jesus is? And just as important, what are you going to do about it?

  8. 41:02 Closing prayer

    Scripture: Jeremiah 15:16 Matthew 7:13–14

    • Steve thanks God for his word and Spirit, which convict, challenge, and encourage at once. As Jeremiah says, “your words were found and I ate them,” and he prays the church would find joy and delight in them.
    • The road to following Jesus is narrow and hard to find, while the path to destruction is wide and easy, but God gives strength, grace, and power to live in pursuit of him. He prays everyone in the room and online would be challenged to take up their cross, deny themselves more, and fully follow him.
