  1. 00:23 From self-reliance to the danger of wealth

    Scripture: James 4:13–17 Matthew 27:57–60 Proverbs 10:22 Deuteronomy 8:18

    • Josh reconnects the sermon to the preceding treatment of James 4:13–17: Christians must stop planning as though God does not exist and instead live in humble dependence on him. He frames James’s recurring series-wide rebuke as a call for Christians to grow up, stop pointing fingers and making excuses, and receive life as God’s means of growth. The proud planner says where he will go and what he will do; James exposes that independence as evil because no one reaches the destination apart from God’s will.
    • Josh says the same pride, greed, arrogance, and independence are especially tempting to the rich. Money reveals whether professed love for Christ is genuine. The independent heart asks why it needs God when it can provide for itself. Materialism from the surrounding culture is seeping into the church, so a faithful pastor must confront it rather than avoid a difficult subject.
    • He announces the opening address of the day’s text: “Now listen, you rich people.” These people are not merely prosperous; they obtained and retained wealth selfishly by harming others. A joke—“If money talks, all it ever says to me is goodbye”—contrasts ordinary financial frustration with the affluent oppressors James addresses.
    • Josh previews the final chapter’s two themes: trouble now and prayer in the following week. Together they show that mature believers pray in trouble and endure by asking God for help; immature people simply muscle through in self-reliance.
    • He carefully distinguishes wealth from sin. Abraham was wealthy and walked with God; the danger is that wealth makes humble dependence difficult. Josh cites the Bible’s extensive attention to money and possessions to argue that wealth can obstruct spiritual growth and entrance into God’s kingdom. Scripture is not fixated on account balances but on the way possessions shape the heart. Joseph of Arimathea is his clearest narrative example of a wealthy person serving God: he prepared Jesus’ body for burial. Proverbs 10:22 and Deuteronomy 8:18 establish that God may give wealth and gives the ability to produce it; wealth becomes a problem when the recipient forgets the giver.
    • Transition: Josh will read six verses and use them to expose areas where the congregation may be out of balance.

  2. 05:59 James 5:1–6 and the heart diagnostic of greed

    Scripture: James 5:1–6 1 Corinthians 5:9–11 Proverbs 11:28 Matthew 6:24 Matthew 6:19–21

    • Josh reads James 5:1–6 in full. Wealth rots, clothing is moth-eaten, and gold and silver corrode. The corrosion testifies against the rich because they hoarded in the last days. Unpaid wages and the harvesters’ cries reach the Lord Almighty. The rich lived in luxury and self-indulgence, fattened themselves for slaughter, and condemned and murdered the innocent.
    • He identifies two controlling issues for the first half of the sermon: How they acquired wealth. How they utilized wealth.
    • Before listeners dismiss this as another message about money, Josh stresses the urgency. Paul places greed beside sexual immorality, idolatry, slander, drunkenness, and swindling and tells the church not even to eat with a professed believer who persists in such sin. Greed therefore demands repentance just as adultery does; it is not a respectable lesser sin.
    • Josh leads a practical self-examination: Are you content with what you have, even if you now have less than before? Does a credit-card balance reveal habitual purchases you cannot afford—having “blessed yourself into debt”? Is your joy centered on what you receive or increasingly on what you can give? When work competes with worship, family, and church, which priority wins?
    • Proverbs 11:28 warns that those who trust riches will fall. Josh’s purpose is pastoral: he does not want his brothers and sisters to fall.
    • Jesus explains why divided allegiance fails: no one can serve both God and money. Josh then quotes Matthew 6:19–21: earthly treasures decay and can be stolen, but heavenly treasures endure; the location of one’s treasure exposes the location of one’s heart.
    • Application and transition: money is a God-given tool for building God’s kingdom, but people become miserable when they use it as a security blanket or as a hammer against others. The issue is the heart.

  3. 10:33 The oppressive rich, unjust acquisition, and Christian work

    Scripture: James 5:1 James 5:3–5 Amos 8:4–6 Amos 8:10 Leviticus 19:13 Jeremiah 22:13 1 Corinthians 5:13 Matthew 6:33

    • Josh addresses an interpretive objection: because James’s warning is so severe, some claim it cannot be directed toward people in the church. Josh argues that James knew his letter would circulate among churches and also knew that many people claim to be Christians without possessing humble dependence on Christ. Some sermons gather crowds; others thin them. Even if James is confronting wealthy pretenders within the church, every listener must heed the warning lest the same trap capture them.
    • Josh returns to James 5:3–5 to show the chain of sin: They hoarded wealth. They withheld workers’ wages. They used the proceeds to live in luxury and self-indulgence.
    • Amos supplies the prophetic parallel. The affluent trampled the needy, commercialized the Sabbath, used dishonest scales, and cheated people out of hard-earned money. Josh translates “selling on the Sabbath” into a contemporary example: using church chiefly to promote one’s business. Amos’s judgment—festivals turned to mourning and songs to weeping—shows why James speaks so fiercely: God will shut down the party. Josh’s summary: praising the almighty dollar dishonors Almighty God.
    • The rebuke creates concrete workplace obligations for Christians and Christian businesses. Do not oppress workers. Pay them on time. Deal honestly and with integrity because employees depend on their wages. Leviticus 19:13 forbids fraud, robbery, and withholding a worker’s wages overnight. Jeremiah 22:13 pronounces woe on the person who builds through injustice and unpaid labor.
    • A humorous “Model S chariot with custom seat warmers” pictures the absurdity of defrauding workers to finance luxury.
    • Josh presses the church’s public witness: how can Christians reach people after robbing them? Echoing 1 Corinthians 5, he says the church must remove unrepentant evil because greedy professing Christians damage the reputation of generous believers. His intentionally severe pastoral warning is that anyone who makes money number one and refuses to repent should worship that god elsewhere; the gospel is too important and the time too urgent for the church to shelter the wicked rich.
    • Application: put God first regardless of income and trust him to supply what is needed.

  4. 15:34 Hoarding versus saving: possessions are entrusted for use

    Scripture: James 5:3 1 Timothy 5:8 Matthew 25:14–30, especially 25:27 Matthew 6:19–21 Matthew 19:16–22; Mark 10:17–22; Luke 18:18–23

    • Josh turns from unjust acquisition to misuse, repeating James 5:3: “You have hoarded wealth in the last days.” He calls hoarding one of the most overlooked sins of the day. God entrusts material goods to believers so those goods will be used for God’s glory.
    • He distinguishes responsible saving from sinful hoarding. First Timothy 5:8 requires provision for relatives and one’s household. In the parable of the talents, Jesus rebukes the servant who buried the money and says even bank interest would have been preferable. Planning and saving for the future are not sins; motive determines the difference. Holding resources for legitimate family provision is saving. Accumulating merely to possess, boast, gain clout, secure an image, or feel significant is hoarding.
    • The plastic-wrapped couch becomes Josh’s main illustration. A couch exists to provide a place of rest, not to be preserved so completely that no one can use it. Rather than protecting it from children and grandchildren, sit with them and tell them what Jesus has done. The illustration turns stewardship from preservation toward meaningful, gospel-shaped purpose.
    • He then applies the same principle to an overfilled walk-in closet. The clothing itself is not automatically the problem; the owner’s attitude toward it is. The imagined house fire tests whether the possessions have become a source of identity requiring grief counseling. James’s rich guarded food, money, and clothing for security and status while walking past unclothed people.
    • Josh notes that Jerusalem fell to Rome about fifteen years after James wrote: accumulated treasure proved temporary.
    • He rejects a simplistic command that everyone must sell everything. A believer may do so if called, but must still care for family. Laying up treasure in heaven means good stewardship and viewing possessions through an eternal lens. Jesus’ command to the rich young ruler exposed that man’s ruling sin; Josh says it was not a universal prescription for identical behavior.
    • Josh asks the room to imagine the congregation’s combined net worth. The likely surprise leads to his central stewardship claim: the church possesses many things but owns nothing; God owns everything, and believers manage the “divine family trust.” Therefore, use what you have for God’s glory and trust his provision.

  5. 21:24 Luxury, waste, exploitation, and coming judgment

    Scripture: Luke 12:15 Romans 5:3–4 James 5:6 1 Corinthians 5:13 James 5:3 Matthew 12:36

    • Josh identifies the final hazard in James 5:5: luxury. He acknowledges that luxury differs greatly between first-century Jerusalem and the modern world.
    • The Antilia residence in Mumbai supplies the extreme illustration. Josh describes its 27 stories, roughly 400,000 square feet, $2 billion construction cost, 168-car garage, theater, snow room, pools, spa, health-care center, floating gardens, and temple. The joke about the owner having these things “because he can” exposes capacity detached from necessity.
    • Josh does not condemn enjoying God’s gifts or owning a comfortable home. The moral question is whether an alleged “need” is extravagant while someone else lacks something basic. Jesus’ warning in Luke 12:15—life does not consist in abundant possessions—sets the standard.
    • Josh contrasts suffering and luxury: if suffering produces character, unchecked luxury can ruin it.
    • James 5:6 shows how far the lifestyle can go. The rich used courts to silence and destroy the poor people they had defrauded. Such conduct has no place in the church; persistent, unrepentant exploiters must be removed and left to the Lord’s judgment.
    • Application: Enjoy what God has given, but never at another person’s expense. The scale of unmet need leaves no justification for waste. Every dollar, possession, and gift must serve God’s purposes. James says corroded wealth will stand as evidence at judgment; Josh adds that not only wasted words but wasted resources will be counted.
    • Transition: James moves from the destruction of greed to the power of patience.

  6. 24:50 The farmer, Christ’s return, and the harvest formed through hardship

    Scripture: James 5:7–8 Revelation 21:1–5 Revelation 19:6–9 John 2:1–11 James 5:7–8 Galatians 5:22–23

    • Josh reads James 5:7–8: believers must patiently stand firm until the Lord’s coming, as a farmer waits for the valuable crop and seasonal rains.
    • He names Christ’s return as the Christian’s blessed hope across two millennia. Until all things are made new, believers patiently endure heartache, loss, trial, tribulation, and trouble. This opposes the demand for instant gratification both outside and inside the church.
    • Two jokes about children thinking milk comes from the fridge by DoorDash and farmers should buy vegetables at Walmart expose cultural distance from agricultural patience. Crops do not appear overnight. Farmers cannot control rain, sun, early winter, or the precise timing of harvest. They must remain patient and prepared.
    • Josh explains why believers can become impatient by reading Revelation 21:1–5. John sees the new heaven, new earth, holy city, and God dwelling with his people. God wipes away every tear; death, mourning, crying, and pain end; the enthroned One makes everything new.
    • Josh responds personally to that promise. It fills his thoughts at the Lord’s Supper and when watching a sunset. All suffering moves toward the marriage supper of the Lamb: a real feast and joyful, everlasting union of Jesus the bridegroom with his bride, the church. Cana offers only a preview of the celebration to come.
    • Therefore, believers should hold tight. Josh does not know when Christ will return, but trusts the divine guarantee and is willing to give everything for it.
    • Returning to the farmer image, Josh says God is cultivating the Spirit’s fruit in his people for a coming harvest. Growth requires rainy, dry, cold, and hot days—hardship and trials. Rather than becoming impatient with God or one another, Christians must trust God with their lives, watch the fruit grow, and spur one another toward fruit that honors him.

  7. 29:46 Work while waiting, and refuse to grumble against the church

    Scripture: Acts 1:9–11 James 5:9 James 5:9

    • A farmer does not stand around staring at the sky; he keeps working toward the harvest. Josh connects this to Acts 1: the disciples were not allowed to remain staring upward after Jesus’ ascension. The returning Christ is the reason to work, not an excuse for passivity. His compact charge is: keep working while you wait; witness and worship while you wait.
    • Farmers also model neighborly cooperation. When one person’s tractor fails, the community helps rather than passing by. Josh recalls visiting his farmer uncle during harvest and seeing the entire community rally around the work.
    • The church must practice the same patience with one another. Impatience with God becomes impatience with people. Fighting one another can prevent completion of God’s assigned work. Josh quotes James 5:9: do not grumble against one another, or you will be judged; the Judge stands at the door.
    • Josh says every believer is “a handful,” but Satan tempts each one to regard somebody else as the real problem. Muttering about others devours Christian effectiveness.
    • C. S. Lewis’s The Screwtape Letters becomes the extended illustration. Screwtape tells Wormwood not to despair when the “patient” becomes a Christian because the visible local church can become a demonic ally. The new Christian cannot yet see the church across time and eternity as a terrible army with banners; he sees an unfinished building and ordinary neighbors he previously avoided. Off-key singing, squeaky boots, double chins, and odd clothing make faith seem ridiculous when the convert fixates on surface defects. Disappointment with imperfect church people can turn professed humility into condescension.
    • Josh applies Lewis directly: looking for fellow believers’ weaknesses and grumbling about them ruins patient endurance and plays into the devil’s hand.
    • He closes the movement by returning to James 5:9: everyone is unfinished, the true Judge is at the door, and until he comes believers must watch their mouths.

  8. 35:39 Prophets and Job: patient suffering under a compassionate Lord

    Scripture: James 5:10–11 2 Timothy 3:12 Jeremiah 37:11–21; 38:1–13, 28 Revelation 22:12 Romans 15:4 1 Corinthians 10:13 Job 2:7–8 Job 42:5 James 5:11 Job 5:18

    • Josh reads James 5:10–11. The prophets who spoke in the Lord’s name model patience in suffering. Those who persevere are called blessed. Job’s perseverance reveals that the Lord is full of compassion and mercy.
    • The prophets matter because they suffered while obeying God. Faithful preaching brought persecution, undermining the assumption that suffering necessarily reveals sin. Paul tells Timothy that everyone desiring a godly life in Christ will be persecuted. Jesus’ obedience led to the cross; suffering can therefore be a result of faithfulness rather than disobedience.
    • Josh gives Jeremiah as the detailed example. Jeremiah was arrested as a traitor and lowered into a cistern to die. God preserved and fed him through Jerusalem’s siege. Josh briefly adds Ezekiel and Daniel as sufferers whom the Lord delivered.
    • He qualifies the promise: not everyone who suffers will be delivered in this life, but those who suffer for Christ will be rewarded. Revelation 22:12 grounds the claim: Jesus is coming soon with his reward and will repay each person according to what he has done.
    • Josh challenges the congregation to become self-feeding students of Scripture rather than consumers who only arrive to be fed. Romans 15:4 explains that what was written in the past teaches and encourages believers so they may have hope. Like the farmer, keep working; like the prophets, keep preaching.
    • Job supplies the final pattern. Satan’s intended temptation did not defeat God’s purpose; God provides a way through. Josh pictures Job bruised, sitting in ashes, scraping his sores, and surrounded by unanswered questions. In the end Job saw the Lord—not the wrathful tyrant imagined by his friends or a distant deity unable to sympathize, but the compassionate and merciful Lord.
    • Application: Do not measure God by pain, emotion, or frustration; measure him by his heart, work, and faithfulness. God is not merely compassionate but full of compassion. Delay and discipline do not mean his heart has grown cold. Josh says Job’s ashes became altars and his tears became testimony; mercy had been working throughout the wait. When prayers seem unheard, do not judge God by life’s shadows or blame him for evil. Wait longer, and God’s mercy will become visible.

  9. 40:11 Truthful speech while suffering and the merciful Judge at the door

    Scripture: James 5:12 Hebrews 6:13–18 Matthew 5:34–37 James 5:9 Revelation 21:4–5; 22:12

    • Josh identifies James 5:12 as the last verse and reads it: do not swear by heaven, earth, or anything else; let a simple yes or no suffice, or face condemnation.
    • Though the command may appear disconnected from wealth and patient suffering, Josh supplies the link: Trouble tempts people to say what they do not mean and make commitments they do not sincerely intend to keep.
    • He clarifies that Scripture does not ban every oath. The problem is deceptive, foolish, or half-hearted swearing. Hebrews records that God himself swore an oath to Abraham. People in James’s setting tried to avoid direct accountability to God by swearing instead by heaven, earth, Jerusalem, or their own heads.
    • Jesus’ teaching in Matthew 5:34–37 closes that loophole. Heaven is God’s throne, earth his footstool, Jerusalem the great King’s city, and even one’s own hair lies outside personal sovereignty. A truthful person needs only a simple yes or no; manipulative excess comes from the evil one.
    • Josh develops the contrast between integrity and performance. The genuine do not perform; actors are always trying to persuade. Truth can wait to be found, while falsehood restlessly spins delicate but deadly verbal webs. Integrity needs no stage, but deception must keep acting lest the curtain fall. When Christ rules the heart, simplicity becomes eloquence and one’s word becomes testimony; a divided heart multiplies smoke-like words that cannot bear weight.
    • He repeats the warning that the Judge stands at the door, then identifies the Judge as Jesus. This is the same Jesus who bore judgment on the cross and rose so mercy would have the final word. The Judge’s nail-scarred hands hold righteousness together with compassion and mercy.
    • Final invitation: “Come and see” the one who knows people fully and loves them still. Christ’s coming is not terror for those who trust him but the fulfillment they long for. Josh urges the church to remain patient, keep trusting, take heart, and hold on: Christ will reward his people, wipe away tears, right wrongs, and make all things new.

  10. 44:08 Prayer, the woman at the well, and communion by grace

    Scripture: John 4:6–30, 39–42 Luke 22:19 Matthew 26:27–28

    • In the closing prayer, Josh pictures the woman of John 4. Shame led her to draw water at a time when she could avoid others. Multiple failed marriages and her present unmarried relationship left her expecting rejection. At the well she met not an ordinary man but the God-man. After a brief conversation, she left her water jar, returned to the very crowd she had avoided, and proclaimed, “Come and see the man who told me everything that I’ve ever done.”
    • Josh turns the narrative into a believer’s testimony. Jesus showed the woman she was worth loving. Grace changes a person who went alone in shame into someone who runs publicly to tell others what Jesus has done. He asks God to give the church that joy and confidence, then thanks Jesus for his sacrifice and blood shed for sinners.
    • Josh moves directly into the Lord’s Supper. Jesus broke the bread and said, “This is my body given for you.” He took the cup and identified it as his covenant blood, poured out for many for the forgiveness of sins.
    • Communion is the weekly reminder that standing with God does not rest on performance, devotion, or resolve but entirely on Christ’s finished work—his body given, blood poured out, and mercy extended. The bread and juice possess no saving power and do not guarantee a better week. They provide an opportunity to remember the Savior’s work. Those who have not trusted Christ may let the tray pass without shame. Believers eat and drink not as people who earned a seat, but as people welcomed by grace; the meal should lead to gratitude, humility, and worship.
