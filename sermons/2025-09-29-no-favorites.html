Overview

  1. 00:38 The Word must govern life, not merely Christian talk

    Scripture: 1 John 4:20

    • Josh begins by restoring the weight of Scripture and clarifying his posture toward the church. He is not speaking down from a place of having “cracked the code”; he is a man who sits under the Word, loves Jesus, and wants the church to reflect Christ’s goodness, grace, mercy, and faithfulness. His pastoral responsibility is to bring God’s Word before believers so they will wrestle with it and ultimately submit to it. He asks the congregation to honor the fact that he has wrestled first: preparation is not an attempt to make the sermon pleasing, but an attempt to align his own life before pressing the text upon them.
    • He draws the sermon’s opening dividing line: immature Christians talk about what they believe; mature Christians strive to live what they believe. The room includes mature believers, immature believers, and unbelievers, so each person will receive the Word according to his or her position before God. Last week’s warning continues: a person can look and sound Christian, display the expected Sunday behaviors, and still possess only a tradition-shaped “Sunday show,” not life-altering faith. James will press mature believers but may feel crushing to immature believers or unbelievers. Josh promises to walk arm in arm with them as they wrestle and submit; the series will not get easier.
    • The YMCA workout with his fourteen-year-old nephew illustrates calibrated spiritual pressure. Josh did not give the boy a workout built for Josh’s strength or drop a 225-pound bar on him; he gave him enough weight to make him strain, improve, and grow. Likewise, James is not satisfied with hearing or discussing God’s Word. Those activities cannot substitute for doing it (James 1:22).
    • A statement of faith is useful for defining agreement, but agreement alone cannot produce Christian character. Josh recounts serving beside someone who agreed with him on essentials: Scripture’s authority, the Trinity, Christ’s person and work, the gospel, and historic creeds. They also agreed on secondary and tertiary matters: baptism, the Lord’s Supper, church governance, women in ministry, gifts, covenant theology, worship styles, communion frequency, eschatology, politics, alcohol in moderation, schooling, and indifference to the young-earth/old-earth argument. Josh uses humor—including the flat-earth joke—and then reveals the point: the man was his theological “twin” but had an enormous ego and treated people badly.
    • The transition into James 2 is relational and diagnostic. Beliefs do not by themselves reveal one’s view of God; treatment of other people does. Human relationships cannot be detached from relationship with God. Josh quotes 1 John 4:20: claiming love for God while hating or demeaning a visible brother or sister makes the claim a lie. He asks the church to hold that verse in mind as the sermon moves into favoritism.

  2. 10:00 James 2:1–4: giving human beings the glory that belongs to Christ

    Scripture: James 2:1–4 Luke 14

    • Josh states the primary passage and defines the problem before reading it. James 2:1–13 is one continuous treatment of favoritism and discrimination. Discrimination is not limited to race; it is unjust treatment of someone because that person differs from you. Prejudice is the inward attitude that may be hidden; discrimination is that attitude made visible in action. James is retraining believers away from worldly preferences and into God’s preferences for how people are treated.
    • He reads James 2:1–4: believers in the glorious Lord Jesus Christ must not favor the finely dressed, gold-ringed visitor over the poor person in filthy clothes.
    • Josh lingers over James’s title, “our glorious Lord Jesus Christ.” He understands it as pointing to Christ’s exalted heavenly status: Christ sits at God’s right hand and will return to judge and save. The title is strategically opposed to the church’s error. They are giving excessive glory to people, so James directs glory to the One who deserves it.
    • Their beliefs are not informing their behavior. They infer inward worth from outward appearance and permit worldly markers to determine spiritual value. Favoritism mistreats someone who fails to meet personal standards even while meeting God’s standards. Josh exposes the pride: the discriminator effectively claims the place of God by devaluing someone who pleases God but does not please him.
    • The desire for recognition and relevance is dangerous. Josh points to Jesus’s table parable in Luke 14 and its conclusion that self-exaltation ends in humiliation while humility ends in exaltation. He refuses to let the local congregation blame only the wider institutional church. Clique-seeking and attempts to climb a church ladder for visibility are present “in this very church.”

  3. 15:02 Break the circles: see people as Christ sees what they can become

    Scripture: Matthew 22:16 John 1 John 7:24

    • Josh confronts people who rarely leave their favorite group to help newcomers connect. The “discipleship” defense can become an excuse for maintaining only deep relationships with existing friends. The Great Commission speaks of nations being taught what Jesus commanded; one need not be a person’s best friend before teaching the Lord’s words. The church needs new relationships to fulfill its mission, not only tighter circles among existing buddies.
    • He acknowledges the natural pull toward similarity and ease. People gravitate to those who look, talk, think, work, serve, or recreate like they do: wealthy people toward the wealthy, military members toward military members, musicians toward musicians, and fitness enthusiasts toward fitness enthusiasts. Harder people demand more and may feel draining, which makes the easy affinity group attractive. His bald-men joke briefly lightens the rebuke, after which he asks forgiveness and returns to the point.
    • The cost is visible at the church door: seekers enter and see everyone’s back. Josh asks when listeners last sought out a newcomer, took that person to lunch, invited the person into a life group, heard the person’s story, and offered a blessing. That shared work belongs to the congregation, not merely to the pastor.
    • Jesus models freedom from status judgments. Josh quotes Jesus’s opponents in Matthew 22:16: even they admitted that Jesus taught truth without being swayed by who people were. The widow’s two small coins outweighed the rich people’s large gifts in kingdom valuation; sacrificial poverty reverses ordinary arithmetic.
    • Jesus sees usefulness and potential rather than only a person’s present shame. Peter could move from denial to being a “rock.” Mary Magdalene could become a witness at the empty tomb. Levi the tax collector could become Matthew, leading people toward gospel treasure. Josh asks when listeners last saw a brother’s or sister’s potential rather than only shortcomings. Even confessed sexual sin and embarrassment can become effective testimony in Jesus’s hands rather than grounds for exclusion.
    • Christ himself destroys appearance-based valuation. Jesus had nowhere to lay his head, came from despised Nazareth, was known as a carpenter’s son, lived in poverty, and possessed no outward appearance that naturally attracted people—yet he is heaven’s champion. Josh applies John 7:24: stop judging by mere appearances and judge correctly. Because people cannot see the heart directly, they should observe fruit and name both harmful fruit and evidence of change—patience, kindness, generosity, and gentleness—in a way that honors God’s work.
    • The controlling practice is to view everyone through Christ’s eyes and remember that God may use anyone he desires for his glory. That claim transitions into James 2:5.

  4. 21:46 James 2:5–7: grace overturns wealth, nationality, and status

    Scripture: James 2:5–7 Romans 8:33–34 Matthew 5:3 Revelation 3:17–18 Romans 2:11 Acts 10 Acts 15 Romans 2:12 1 Timothy 6 Matthew 25:40

    • Josh reads James 2:5–7 and gives the theological charge: Partiality dishonors God and displays ignorance of how God works and saves. God sovereignly saves people who cannot earn and do not deserve salvation, entirely on the basis of Christ’s cross rather than personal impressiveness. Romans 8:33–34 grounds his confidence: God justifies his chosen people, and the crucified, risen, enthroned Christ intercedes for them. Josh’s hope rests not in occasionally hitting the target but in Christ as his righteousness.
    • He distinguishes two senses of poverty. In Matthew 5:3, being poor in spirit is humility: awareness of sin, deserved death, dependence, and the necessity of grace. In Revelation 3:17–18, Laodicean self-sufficiency is a wicked poverty: a person claims wealth and needlessness while actually being wretched, poor, blind, and naked; Christ alone supplies true riches. A person may be materially poor now and rich in eternity, materially rich now and poor in eternity, poor in both, or rich in both. The decisive issues are one’s response to Christ and stewardship of material wealth. God promises the kingdom to those who love him, not those who love his things. Josh lets the room’s discomfort stand and names ordinary signs of wealth, such as a new car or expensive purse.
    • He prevents an equal-and-opposite misreading. James is not replacing favoritism toward the rich with favoritism toward the poor, nor commanding hatred of the rich. Jesus pressed the rich young ruler but still offered him a real opportunity to put wealth in its proper place; the man walked away sad. The command is no partiality, not redirected partiality: love people as Jesus wants rather than as the world expects.
    • God’s impartiality extends through nationality and social status. Romans 2:11 states that God does not show favoritism; therefore believers must not. Acts 10 shows God sending Peter to share fellowship with the Gentile Cornelius despite the predominantly Jewish church’s suspicion. Acts 15 then resolves whether Gentiles must become Jews to become Christians; the Spirit-led answer is no. Josh summarizes Paul’s equal verdict on Jew and Gentile in condemnation and salvation, then quotes Romans 2:12 about judgment both apart from and under the law. Grace makes the wealthy unable to depend on wealth and makes the poor heirs of every spiritual blessing in Christ; it is the great equalizer.
    • James’s accusation against rich oppressors sharpens the contradiction. The same wealthy people who exploit believers, drag them into court, and blaspheme Christ’s name receive privileged treatment in the gathering. Josh loosely cites the love of money as the root of evil and argues that Christ, himself poor and victimized by wealthy corrupt leaders, taught his people to care for the poor rather than profit from them. Matthew 25:40 identifies service to “the least of these” with service to Christ. Ephesians 2 declares the dividing wall destroyed. Rebuilding divisions of wealth, age, ethnicity, education, or nationality is sin against the God who tore those walls down.
    • Josh pauses—“Breathe”—and summarizes the negative case: partiality sins against God and neighbor. Verse 8 will now state the positive way.

  5. 30:15 James 2:8: the royal law turns the question toward the neighbor in need

    Scripture: James 2:8 Leviticus 19:18 Luke 10 1 Thessalonians 4:9 Romans 13:10 Luke 6:31–36

    • Josh reads James 2:8: keeping the royal law, “Love your neighbor as yourself,” is doing right. James reaches back to Leviticus 19:18, where love of neighbor is opposed to revenge and grudges.
    • The Good Samaritan reframes neighbor love. Josh’s “pop quiz” asks what question prompted the parable: “Who is my neighbor?” Jesus’s answer makes the neighbor anyone whose need one can meet, regardless of origin, location, or prior activity. The right question is not “Who is my neighbor?” but “Who needs me to be a good neighbor?”
    • The State Farm illustration challenges church mission. Josh jokes that a large insurance company’s “good neighbor” slogan can sound more biblical than vague church slogans about discovering one’s best self, “reach in/reach out,” or mere acceptance. He contrasts those with a church commitment to change how people see the church by serving the needs of Anthem, Phoenix, and the world, and invites the congregation to join that work.
    • He gives three ordered reasons “love your neighbor” is the royal law. The King gave it. The Father gave it in the law, the Son taught it to his disciples, and the Spirit fills and convicts God’s children to share divine love. First Thessalonians 4:9 says believers have been taught by God to love one another. It is the law of laws. Romans 13:10 says love does no harm to a neighbor and therefore fulfills the law; universal obedience to the royal law would remove the need for the other restraints. It keeps the royal court open to seekers. Jesus is the way to eternal life, and Christian love is meant to direct people to him rather than close the door.
    • Luke 6 raises love beyond ordinary reciprocity. Doing good only to those who reciprocate, lending only when repayment is expected, and loving only those who love you do not distinguish disciples from sinners. Jesus commands love for enemies, generosity without expected return, and mercy patterned after the Father’s kindness to the ungrateful and wicked.
    • Josh crystallizes the application: A person only truly believes what he or she is willing to do. Obedience means loving neighbor, stranger, and enemy as oneself. Anyone in need deserves from the believer what the believer never deserved from God.

  6. 36:58 James 2:9–11: favoritism belongs in the category of lawbreaking

    Scripture: James 2:9 1 Timothy 4 James 2:10–11

    • Josh reads James 2:9 and warns that the sermon is becoming heavy again. Favoritism is not merely an unfortunate fairness problem or a small weakness excused by saying, “At least I have not killed anyone.” James calls the “isms” sin: classism, culturalism, racism, favoritism, sexism, and ageism. Those sins remain inside churches because churches do not consistently treat them as sin.
    • Josh uses his own experience of ageism as a lead pastor. Others look down on him because of his age. He interrupts the argument for an urgent pastoral appeal: no one should be allowed to throttle another person’s usefulness in God’s kingdom. God gives gifts to whom he wants, when he wants, and uses them for his glory. Young people must not let someone assume God’s authority by declaring what they cannot do. The wording deliberately echoes 1 Timothy 4:12: do not let anyone look down on you because of your youth.
    • He returns to James and reads James 2:10–11. Breaking one point makes a person a breaker of the whole law because the same God forbids adultery and murder. Josh extends James’s logic in sequence: murderer—lawbreaker; adulterer—lawbreaker; discriminator—lawbreaker; racist—lawbreaker; ignoring the person on the street who needs help—lawbreaker. The intended result is humility, not a ranking system that leaves respectable church people above others.

  7. 40:30 James 2:12–13: speak and act as people accountable to the law of freedom

    Scripture: James 2:12–13 1 John 3:24 2 Corinthians 5:10 Matthew 5:7

    • Josh reads James 2:12–13: believers must speak and act as those who will be judged by the law that gives freedom; judgment without mercy comes to the unmerciful, while mercy triumphs over judgment.
    • Christ’s return includes judgment. The Apostles’ Creed affirms that Christ will judge the living and the dead even where Christians disagree over timing and sequence. Josh distinguishes condemnation for sin from evaluation and reward: Christians are accepted through Christ, yet what they do, say, and display toward others still matters. He repeats Matthew 25’s compassion test, quotes 1 John 3:24 about command-keeping as evidence of abiding union, and quotes 2 Corinthians 5:10 about everyone appearing before Christ’s judgment seat.
    • Grace does not abolish obedient concern. Believers are not condemned by the law and possess freedom through Christ, but the law remains the standard by which conduct is judged. James’s command is practical: “Act like it.”
    • Josh turns the doctrine into a Spirit-enabled inventory. What has the believer accomplished with the Holy Spirit’s help? Whom has the believer blessed? When mercy and judgment were both possible, is the person increasingly choosing mercy? Where hate was once the default, is love increasingly being chosen?
    • James 2:13 echoes and reverses the Beatitude in Matthew 5:7. Jesus states the positive: the merciful will be shown mercy. James states the negative: the person who withholds mercy will face judgment without mercy.
    • The core issue is understanding what Christ has done, not accumulating moral achievements. A person who claims Christ’s forgiveness and peace through his life, death, burial, and resurrection but refuses to treat others as God treated him must examine whether God’s love is present at all. A merciful disposition is evidence of Christ within. Union with the One who fulfilled the law gives confidence in judgment. Josh repeats the controlling line: “Mercy triumphs over judgment.”

  8. 44:05 Gospel 101: you were the filthy poor visitor, and God became the poor man

    Scripture: Philippians 2 Isaiah 53:5 Philippians 2:8

    • Josh closes the exposition with a guided picture of the two men from James 2:2. He asks listeners to imagine the poor visitor in filthy, cheap, borrowed, unclean, smelly clothes—an appearance that is revolting and uncomfortable. If that man enters the Lord’s house and a believer protects a saved seat, sends him elsewhere, refuses courtesy, or will not treat him as an equal, James sends that believer back to “Gospel 101.”
    • The reason is not sentimental hospitality but spiritual identity. The believer looks like that poor man before God. Human righteousness is like filthy, discarded rags (Isaiah 64:6); every sinner is the shabby visitor hoping someone will extend a hand. Until a person sees himself in that light, he will neither notice the poor nor treat them as equals.
    • The gospel moves beyond identifying sinners with the poor man: God became the poor man before human eyes. Christ became poor for others, made himself nothing, took a servant’s nature, was born in a manger, and was raised in Nazareth. Josh quotes Isaiah 53:5: Christ was pierced for transgressions, crushed for iniquities, bore the punishment that brings peace, and heals by his wounds. He then quotes Philippians 2:8: Christ humbled himself in obedience to death on a cross. Jesus was mocked, ridiculed, and cast out even among those who claimed to love God most.
    • Josh presses the contradiction: how can one claim to receive the poor and rejected Christ while rejecting the poor person present in the church? Jesus slept in dirt so his people could sit on a throne; he was judged so they could receive mercy. Knowing that mercy produces service from inward desire, not merely external compulsion. The Spirit-led impulse to show up for difficult people is evidence that Christ’s love exists within.
    • Treatment of others is therefore a test of genuine faith—not only treatment of friends and similar people, but of people who require more. Listeners must remember how much they required of God: they were the high-maintenance party, yet he still showed up. Josh ends the movement with the question: Who can you show up for like that?

  9. 48:34 Communion, self-examination, and an invitation to receive Christ

    Scripture: 1 Corinthians 11:26–28

    • Josh transitions from the poor Christ to the Lord’s Supper. Believers are to remember Jesus’s body given and blood poured out and to dwell in the mercy and grace made available in him. To those uncertain what they believe, Josh says he is glad they are present. Without Jesus he sees them as spiritually poor people needing a hand, and he wants to show them Christ rather than speak down to them.
    • He carefully denies saving power to the physical elements. The cracker and grape juice cannot help during the week or forgive sin. Their importance lies in the body and blood they represent; believers should rest in the joy of salvation. Anyone newly drawn to Christ is invited to speak with Josh, the pastors, or the elders. Skeptics are welcome to wrestle with truth while the church walks with them.
    • He reads 1 Corinthians 11:26–28. Eating the bread and drinking the cup proclaim the Lord’s death until he returns. Receiving them unworthily sins against the Lord’s body and blood, so each participant must examine himself or herself first. Those confident in salvation through Christ’s person and cross may participate; those who are not should let the elements pass and continue wrestling.
    • Josh gives his personal testimony and final appeal. Jesus changed his life permanently rather than temporarily. Everyone needs a Savior; the risen Christ alone conquered death. He paraphrases John 14:6: the God-man says the only way to heaven is through him, then asks, “Do you believe him?”

  10. 51:45 Closing prayer: dethrone favorites and make the church merciful

    • Josh begins with personal repentance for elevating human beings into a glorious place belonging only to God.
    • He tells the Lord, “You’re my favorite,” turning the sermon title into worship rather than human preference.
    • He asks God to dismantle circles in the church so that when the poor person enters, the congregation will see that person as God does and extend the mercy God has extended to them.
    • He prays for a church marked not by polished Christian behavior but by hearts divinely altered by the Holy Spirit.
    • He thanks Christ for undeserved death on the cross and asks the church not merely to change its internal culture but to change the city, including offering hope through the care center.
    • The sermon ends with gratitude and “Amen” at 53:53.
