Overview

  1. 00:14 The saving faith that receives Christ and then acts

    Scripture: Ephesians 2:8–9 Galatians 2:16 2 Corinthians 5:7 Hebrews 11:6 Romans 14:23b Hebrews 11 Hebrews 12:1

    • Josh opens by calling faith indispensable to Christian life. The sinner is saved by grace through faith, not works, so glory belongs to God rather than the believer (Ephesians 2:8–9). He reads the Westminster Confession of Faith on effectual calling and justification: God pardons and accepts sinners because Christ’s obedience and satisfaction are imputed to them; faith receives and rests in Christ’s righteousness but is itself God’s gift. He distills the confession: faith is the means by which believers receive and rest beneath Christ’s righteousness.
    • He establishes several biblical functions of faith in sequence. A person is justified through faith in Christ, not law observance (Galatians 2:16). The believer walks by faith rather than by visible circumstances (2 Corinthians 5:7); God’s Word, not merely sensory experience, governs decisions, priorities, and worldview. Faith is necessary to please God (Hebrews 11:6). The rescued believer longs to please the Father as a child longs for a father’s return. The world and Satan may press in, but faith gives an “undefeatable optimism”: even death opens into life on the other side because the Creator who holds the universe also holds his people. What does not proceed from faith is sin (Romans 14:23).
    • Josh defines faith against caricatures. It is not a shot in the dark or self-generated optimism. It is obedience despite consequences and persistence amid what is unknown and unseen. It is confidence that God’s Word is true, sustained attention to God’s promises, and a conviction that obedience ultimately brings blessing—even if that blessing comes beyond the grave.
    • He surveys Hebrews 11 in the chapter’s order: Noah, Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, Joseph, Moses and his parents, Israel at the Red Sea, Jericho, Rahab, and persecuted saints all acted on promises they had not yet received. Their stories exist because their faith worked; positive certainty in God’s promises became action. The “cloud of witnesses” supplies visible faith for later believers to follow.
    • Transition to the sermon’s governing issue: James connects the enormous subjects of faith and works. The danger is not only people who know they lack faith, but churchgoers who assume they possess saving faith when they do not. Josh asks: What kind of faith really saves a person, and how can someone tell whether actions grow from saving faith or something else? He previews James’s three kinds of faith: dead faith, demonic faith, and living faith.

  2. 08:04 James 2:14–26 read: genuine faith must survive the test

    Scripture: James 2:14–26

    • Josh reads the complete primary unit, James 2:14–26. A claim to faith without deeds cannot help a poorly clothed, hungry brother or sister. Faith unaccompanied by action is dead. Faith becomes visible through what a person does; bare monotheistic belief is no better than the demons’ belief and terror. Abraham’s offering of Isaac and Rahab’s protection of the spies show faith and action working together. As a body without the spirit is dead, faith without deeds is dead.
    • He introduces authenticity through two counterfeit illustrations. Real gold is heavy, valuable, soft, and malleable; fool’s gold merely looks similar and crumbles when worked. Counterfeit currency may look authentic beside real money, but testing exposes it.
    • Interpretive boundary: James is not presenting the gospel to unbelievers or explaining how sinners earn heaven. James addresses people who think they are Christians and assumes the saving work has already occurred. Josh’s contrast: James is not explaining how sinners get to heaven but how Christians bring “heaven to earth.”

  3. 10:31 James and Paul agree: works prove rather than purchase justification

    Scripture: James 2:24 Romans 3:28 Acts 15 Revelation 3:16

    • Josh stages the apparent conflict. James says a person is justified by what they do and not by faith alone (James 2:24). Paul says a person is justified by faith apart from works of the law (Romans 3:28). Skeptics treat those statements as a contradiction and force a false choice between “team Paul” and “team James.”
    • Perspective resolves the apparent conflict. James writes pastorally to lead professing believers into a living faith that blesses God and others; he is concerned with the effectiveness of the believer and the church. Paul writes as a missionary emphasizing entry into right standing with God. The early church treated James and Romans as complementary, and the Jerusalem council reached agreement (Acts 15). James addresses works of love; Paul rejects works of the law as a means of becoming right with God.
    • Josh explains two uses of “justify.” His word illustration contrasts an original sense of “nice” with its common modern use: words can carry more than one accepted sense. “Justify” can mean make right or prove right. The push-up illustration makes the distinction concrete: asking a man to “justify” his claim that he can do one hundred push-ups does not make the claim true; it asks him to demonstrate that it is true.
    • Applied to Paul and James: Paul’s “justified by faith” means sinners can only be made right with God through Jesus Christ’s person and finished work. James’s works of love prove that someone has been made right with God. A profession alone cannot provide that proof; consistent life change and an outpouring of love do.
    • Josh presses the diagnostic implication. If only the claimant can detect the supposed faith, and the life does not show it, the faith is dead or worse. Christians should not need to advertise the label; people should see the reality. He invokes Jesus’s warning to the lukewarm church about being spit out, then quotes the synthesis: “We’re saved by faith alone, but not by a faith that remains alone.” Works do not make a sinner right; in saving faith, works are not optional but inevitable—never perfect, yet consistent.

  4. 17:48 Dead faith substitutes religious words for costly love

    Scripture: Matthew 7:21 James 2:14–16 Galatians 6:10 Galatians 5:6b 1 Corinthians 13 Luke 10 James 2:17

    • Dead faith replaces works with words. A person may pray fluently, speak Christian language, and recite verses while never letting talk shape the walk. Jesus warns that saying “Lord, Lord” is not equivalent to doing the Father’s will (Matthew 7:21).
    • Josh returns to James’s needy-brother example (James 2:14–16). Empty wishes that a hungry or poorly clothed person be warm and fed accomplish nothing. A visitor should not leave a believer’s presence unaffected: when material needs are absent, offer the gospel and prayer; when needs are present, offer the gospel, prayer, and practical help. Paul likewise commands believers to do good to everyone, especially the family of believers (Galatians 6:10).
    • Crossroads Care Center becomes the immediate congregational application. Josh presents its opening as the church’s faith forcing it outward rather than allowing it to sit still. Members are the church’s eyes and ears: notice needs in family, neighborhood, workplace, or community; recommend the center and pass needs to people ready to serve. Because the local body is committed to this work, those who call Crossroads home have no excuse for ignoring another person’s physical need. Such help expresses love and changes how outsiders see the church—love extends to believers, strangers, and enemies.
    • Paul’s phrase “faith expressing itself through love” (Galatians 5:6) becomes Josh’s compact test. Religious accomplishments do not count if faith does not express itself in love. Josh includes himself: his sermons mean nothing without love; Elijah’s music is only noise and clanging if unaccompanied by love (1 Corinthians 13:1–3).
    • The Good Samaritan exposes respectable dead faith. A priest and Levite possessed formal religious standing and doctrine but passed the wounded man rather than ministering to him (Luke 10:30–32). Josh criticizes a modern church culture that trains as though defending the gospel were its sole purpose while neglecting active love. Defense matters, but Christianity has already survived for centuries and has moved believers to establish hospitals, schools, charities, welfare programs, and governments. Too many Christians measure effectiveness by pages turned rather than feet washed; unlimited sermon clips can make anyone appear theologically informed.
    • James’s question remains: Can a faith never seen in love save? Josh answers no. Any faith producing neither life change nor an outpouring of love is dead. He quotes John Calvin’s formulation that faith alone justifies, but justifying faith can never remain alone, then repeats James 2:17. Dead faith reduces salvation to a tidy intellectual exercise without personal trust in Jesus. Application: do not be deceived; examine whether faith is saving or counterfeit, because saving faith works.

  5. 26:13 Demonic faith possesses correct doctrine and emotion without trust

    Scripture: James 2:18–19 Colossians 2:9 Mark 3:11 Luke 8:31 Deuteronomy 6:4 James 2:19

    • James imagines an objector and answers from James 2:18–19: deeds display faith, while even demons believe there is one God and shudder.
    • Josh says this second counterfeit comes frighteningly close to saving faith. Demons are real, and he treats demonic activity as a reality even around church life, listing guilt, fear, depression, anxiety, discrimination, addiction, confusion, hatred, worry, and unexplained changes or reactions as ways people may describe its activity. Demons are neither atheists nor agnostics; they are theists.
    • Their doctrine is extensive. They recognize Christ’s deity: Josh cites the fullness of deity dwelling bodily in Christ (Colossians 2:9) and the evil spirits calling Jesus the Son of God (Mark 3:11). They know a place of punishment exists and beg Jesus not to send them into the abyss (Luke 8:31). They respect divine authority and power, fear judgment, and submit because they have no alternative.
    • The warning: humans can approach saving faith just as closely and remain lost. A person may affirm God’s greatness, respect his power, fear punishment, and become moral and religious, yet possess only shuddering terror. James invokes the Shema, Israel’s daily confession that the Lord is one (Deuteronomy 6:4), then observes that demons affirm the same monotheism (James 2:19).
    • Josh distinguishes valid components from saving evidence. Solid doctrine and intellectual understanding are good. Strong emotion in worship is also good; Josh admits worship moves him deeply. But neither intellect nor emotion proves authentic saving faith. A person may have correct beliefs and feelings and still be lost. Authentic faith is not merely believing in God; it is believing God—taking him at his Word regardless of cost because he is faithful.
    • Transition to living faith: saving faith can be seen, witnessed, shared, and celebrated because it produces a changed life and God’s love toward others.

  6. 31:43 God displays his workmanship in a changed life

    Scripture: Ephesians 2:10 Revelation 3:17 James 2:20 James 1:18 Romans 10:17

    • James and Paul again converge: faith cannot be displayed without deeds, and believers are God’s workmanship, created in Christ for prepared good works (Ephesians 2:10).
    • Josh’s staff-painting illustration explains visibility. His poor winter landscape belongs in the laundry room where only the dogs see it. God’s workmanship is a masterpiece, not something hidden in a laundry room or garage; he puts it in the foyer for everyone to see.
    • That visible grace shapes Christian testimony. Christians may describe their former condition with humiliating language—“wretched, pitiful, poor, blind and naked” (Revelation 3:17)—because they recognize what grace changed. Josh rejects sentimental claims that every person is already God’s child: all people bear God’s image, but adoption as God’s children comes through faith in Christ. Appeals that stop at intellect produce dead faith; appeals that stop at emotion can produce something no better than demonic faith. The decisive issue is not what someone feels or thinks but whom they trust.
    • James now demands evidence that faith without deeds is useless (James 2:20). Josh recalls an earlier point in the James : A Faith That Works series: God gives birth through the word of truth (James 1:18); the received word saves (James 1:21); faith comes by hearing the message about Christ (Romans 10:17). James’s positive evidence will be Abraham and Rahab—social and moral opposites united by saving faith.

  7. 35:42 Abraham: living faith proves itself through obedience

    Scripture: James 2:21 Genesis 15 Genesis 22 Matthew 26

    • Josh reads James’s first example: Abraham was shown righteous when he offered Isaac (James 2:21).
    • He distinguishes two moments in Abraham’s chronology. Genesis 15: Abraham trusts God; God credits righteousness to his empty spiritual account. This is Abraham’s justification by faith. Genesis 22: the already-justified Abraham offers the promised son. This does not purchase salvation; it demonstrates faith and receives blessing.
    • Josh defines justification carefully. God declares the believing sinner righteous on the basis of Christ’s finished work on the cross. It is a completed divine act, not a process and not something the sinner performs. The ensuing changed life and obedience reveal that God has done it.
    • He frames the change as movement from disobedience in Eden to surrender in Gethsemane; “your will be done” becomes the believer’s battle cry.
    • Abraham’s ascent of the mountain supplies the evidence. He was willing to surrender the child of promise. His obedience proved the salvation he had already received. God’s gift in Genesis 15 enabled Abraham’s surrender in Genesis 22.
    • Conclusion: Faith and works are partners, not rivals. By faith Abraham was declared righteous before God; by works that righteousness was displayed before people. Abraham was not saved by faith plus a work, but by a faith that works.

  8. 38:36 Rahab: living faith sacrifices, risks, and acts with courage

    Scripture: James 2:25 Joshua 2 2 Corinthians 5:17 James 2:26

    • Josh reads James’s second example, Rahab sheltering the spies and sending them out another way (James 2:25). Abraham demonstrates true obedience; Rahab demonstrates true sacrifice. The Jewish patriarch and the Gentile prostitute stand at opposite social locations, yet both possess saving faith.
    • He retells Joshua 2. Rahab hears that Israel’s God is the true God, hides the spies, risks her life, seeks protection for her family, and shares the news with them. Authentic faith reaches mind, heart, and hands together; it sacrifices for others, dies to self, and lives for God. Her transformed identity illustrates the new creation in Christ (2 Corinthians 5:17).
    • Josh contrasts Rahab’s limited revelation with the congregation’s abundance. She had only a garbled report about the Red Sea drying up, yet acted. Present believers possess the full revelation of God, evidence after Calvary, and the Spirit’s guidance and conviction. Her response to a small amount of testimony becomes Josh’s “mustard seed” picture of faith.
    • Her lie creates a moral-pressure application. Rahab defies her government, hides the spies, and lies to protect them. Josh acknowledges the command against false testimony, then asks what someone should do when the available choices are lying or helping a murderer. His answer: when trapped between sinful options, choose the act that brings God greater glory and seek God in the moment. He analogizes to Corrie ten Boom in Germany, who lied to German officers while hiding Jews: truth-telling in that setting would have assisted murder. Rahab does not convene a study group or wait for a long counseling session; she walks by faith, acts courageously, and does what she believes honors God.
    • James’s closing image (James 2:26) seals the argument: as a body without the spirit is dead, faith without works is dead.

  9. 43:52 “Do you have a faith that works?”: the self-examination

    Scripture: 2 Corinthians 13:5 Philippians 2

    • Josh summarizes living faith. Christians practice truth; they do not organize faith as an external system—faith reorganizes them. Abraham risks everything because God is able; Rahab takes courageous risks because God is with her.
    • He asks every believer to examine the heart, repeating Paul’s command to test whether one is in the faith (2 Corinthians 13:5). This is the most important test a person can take. The listener must evaluate self rather than spouse, neighbor, or anyone else.
    • The questions proceed in Josh’s order: Conviction of sin: Was there a time of humble, honest admission to God that you are a sinner? Has sin against a holy and just God ever disturbed and crushed you, even if others would call the offenses minor? Understanding the gospel: Do you understand that the Maker became man, bled and died for sins, and rose so sinners could have life—and that you cannot save yourself? Repentance: Have you sincerely turned from sin? Repentance is not apology followed by an unchanged sinful lifestyle; it includes frustration with and hatred of sin rather than secret preference for it. Trust and relationship: Have you trusted Christ for salvation? Do you enjoy him through the Word and fellowship, place anxieties and burdens at his feet, experience his help, and find joy in walking with him? Response to conviction: Do you welcome the Spirit’s correction, put down fleshly desires, and walk God’s way? Observable change: Can you identify changed attitudes, weakened addictions, altered mindsets, or renewed treatment of a spouse as places Jesus is working? Consistency and dependence: Does faith work consistently through sanctification, or repeatedly stall? Are you dependent on a church service, pastor, or other believers to feed you, or on God to fill, renew, and accompany you? Witness: Do you tell others about Jesus, or are you ashamed to speak the gospel plainly? Corporate worship: Do you delight to worship with God’s people, or can you barely bring yourself to attend? Christ’s return: When Jesus appears, will you rejoice that he has come, or look back anxiously at what you are leaving?

  10. 49:36 Prayer for conviction, rest, searching, and assurance

    Scripture: Psalm 139 Hebrews 13

    • Josh prays after acknowledging that the test gives the congregation much to consider.
    • He asks God to expose faith that is not working itself out through love. Some are still wrestling with the first question and need conviction. Others feel the burden of sin but have not fully trusted the One who lifts it. He asks Jesus to give them the promised rest offered to those who come to him (Matthew 11:28–29).
    • Recognizing that self-examination can itself be deceived, he turns the language of Psalm 139:23–24 into prayer: Search and know the heart more deeply than the worshiper can. Reveal any way that is not pleasing to God. Lead the congregation in the everlasting way.
    • As believers approach the communion table and sit under God’s promises, he asks for assurance and peace. Their security rests in God’s hands and in his promise never to leave or forsake them (Hebrews 13:5). He closes, “Thank you, Lord. We love you. It’s in your name we pray. Amen.”
