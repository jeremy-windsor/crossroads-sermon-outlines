  1. 00:33 Fulfilled history after four hundred years of silence

    Scripture: John 14:2–3 Luke 1:1 Luke 1:5 Genesis 3:15 Genesis 12:3 2 Samuel 7:12–16

    • Josh reconnects this message to the series launch. Luke investigated reports, interviewed eyewitnesses, organized his findings, and gave Theophilus an orderly account so readers could have certainty. Luke’s contribution is summarized as historical credibility: Jesus entered real history, lived under real rulers, was crucified on a real cross, left a real tomb, and ascended. The second half of Luke’s opening claim now controls the sermon: these are not merely things that happened but things “fulfilled among us”—history carrying prophetic significance.
    • Luke opens amid one of Israel’s darkest political and spiritual periods. Herod is not a Davidic son reigning under God but Caesar’s puppet. Though a brilliant builder who beautified the temple, Herod did not possess a heart surrendered to God; his reign rested on Roman power, fear, taxation, bloodshed, paranoia, and even the execution of his wife and three sons. Josh’s comic comparison—Herod makes Pharaoh look like a Sunday-school teacher—underscores the brutality hidden beneath an impressive exterior.
    • Josh asks the church to inhabit Israel’s predicament: Jerusalem has the wrong king. Rome controls the nation. Heaven has apparently been silent since Malachi for four hundred years.
    • Illustrations make the wait emotionally legible. People become anxious after being “left on read” for hours; Israel waited centuries. Going back four hundred years from 2026 predates the United States and modern electricity, cars, airplanes, internet, and phones—roughly fifteen generations.
    • Yet the unresolved promises remained: the woman’s offspring would crush the serpent; Abraham’s family would bless the nations; David would receive a son with an everlasting kingdom.
    • The tension is stated as Israel’s question: Where is the king? Has God forgotten, changed his mind, or abandoned his promises?

  2. 05:13 Zechariah: a heard prayer, an impossible promise, and a silenced priest

    Scripture: Luke 1:6–7 Luke 1:8–10 Luke 1:13–17 Luke 1:18 Luke 1:20 Luke 1:21–22 Malachi 3:1

    • Luke introduces Zechariah, whose name means “the Lord remembers.” The irony is deliberate: a man bearing that name lives while Israel wonders whether the Lord has forgotten. Luke calls Zechariah and Elizabeth righteous and blameless, yet they are childless and now old. Josh acknowledges the likely years of prayer, frustration, watching other families grow, and eventual human acceptance that they would never have a child.
    • Zechariah’s selection to burn incense is a once-in-a-lifetime priestly privilege. Josh estimates roughly 18,000 priests in twenty-four divisions and perhaps 750 in Abijah’s division. He calls it the “Super Bowl of priesthood” and the “priestly Powerball”: most priests would never be chosen. Alone in the holy place, Zechariah offers incense while the people pray outside; the rising smoke visibly represents their prayers before God. The scene holds a sharp personal/professional tension: Zechariah has reached a priestly high point while carrying unanswered prayer into the room.
    • Gabriel breaks the silence with a double answer. Zechariah’s prayer has been heard; Elizabeth will bear John. John will bring joy, be great before the Lord, abstain from fermented drink, be filled with the Holy Spirit before birth, turn Israelites back to God, minister in Elijah’s spirit and power, reconcile hearts, and prepare a people for the Lord. In one announcement, God addresses one family’s pain and ends four hundred years of prophetic silence.
    • Zechariah asks how he can be sure because he and Elizabeth are old. Gabriel answers that Zechariah will be unable to speak until the promise happens because he did not believe. Josh’s formulation: Zechariah asked for a sign, and God gave him one; every failed attempt to speak would remind him that God’s saying is God’s doing.
    • The irony deepens: the priest entered representing the people before God; received the announcement that Malachi’s forerunner was coming and the Lord was drawing near; then emerged unable to announce it.
    • Initial application: believers should be careful demanding a sign when God has already spoken.

  3. 10:32 Unbelief can silence a witness, but grace still gives the final word

    Scripture: Matthew 16:18 Luke 1:6, 18 Luke 1:67–79 Luke 1:45

    • Josh broadens Zechariah’s muteness into a warning about the church’s posture toward Scripture. A church may possess music, facilities, political opinions, and cultural criticism, but if it ceases to believe that Jesus saves, the gospel changes lives, the Spirit works through the Word, and Christ builds his church, it will lose its voice. Christians can become experts at identifying darkness while failing to point anyone to the light. “Christian veterans” must keep fighting; Christianity is not organized around their comfort.
    • Zechariah’s résumé could not substitute for trust. He knew Scripture, served in the holy place, and saw Gabriel, who stands in God’s presence. Proximity to holy things does not necessarily mean genuine faith.
    • Josh then refuses to caricature Zechariah. Luke also calls him righteous and blameless. He is an old man carrying disappointment accumulated over many years. Long disappointment makes hope feel risky because renewed hope creates another opportunity to be hurt; people therefore stop asking, believing, and hoping. Josh’s reading is that Zechariah believed God’s large redemptive promises but struggled to believe God would act through Elizabeth.
    • Pastoral assurance: even if faith falters, God remains faithful. God disciplines Zechariah but does not discard or replace him. Zechariah’s unbelief limits Zechariah; it cannot limit God.
    • When Zechariah’s mouth is opened, he uses it to praise God in the song beginning at Luke 1:67.
    • Luke 1 therefore presents two songs and two paths: Mary believes and sings now. Zechariah’s unbelief must be disciplined until he sings. Grace gets the final word on both paths, but the blessed path is to believe and sing now.
    • Transition and controlling claim: God’s silence is not God’s absence. While Israel heard nothing, God was arranging everything; at precisely the right time, his promises were about to be born.

  4. 16:12 God remembers what he promised, and every promise converges on Jesus

    Scripture: Luke 1:54–55 Genesis 8:1 Genesis 19:29 Genesis 30:22 Exodus 2:23–25; 3:7–8 Genesis 3:1–24 Romans 5:12 Genesis 3:15 Genesis 1–3 Genesis 12:3 2 Samuel 7:12–16 Isaiah 52:13–53:12 Isaiah 7:14 Micah 5:2 Isaiah 61:1 Malachi 3:1; 4:5–6 Luke 1:13 Luke 1:26–27 Luke 1:30–33 Genesis 3:15 Genesis 12:3; 22:18 2 Samuel 7:12–16 Galatians 3:16 Ephesians 2:8–9 Revelation 5:9 Luke 1:34–37 Luke 1:38

    • First major claim: God remembers what he promised. Josh reads the end of Mary’s song: God helped Israel, remembered mercy to Abraham and his descendants, and acted “just as he promised.” His verbal cue says “verse 39,” but the words actually read are Luke 1:54–55; this is a spoken verse-number mismatch, not a textual ambiguity.
    • “God remembered” never means God recovered forgotten information. Omniscience means God knows past, present, and future perfectly and immediately. Comic illustrations: God does not enter a room and forget why, misplace a phone already in his pocket, or discover mid-flight that he left Kevin at home. Biblical pattern: God remembered Noah and the flood receded; Abraham and Lot was rescued; Rachel and her womb opened; Israel’s cries and covenant, then came down to deliver. Definition: divine remembrance is God acting in faithfulness on his desired timetable.
    • Mary recognizes her pregnancy as the decisive, preplanned fulfillment of a promise older than she is. Humanity rebelled in Adam and Eve; sin fractured fellowship with God and one another, and death entered through Adam. Before exile from Eden, God promised that the woman’s offspring would crush the serpent—the protoevangelium, or first gospel. Genesis 1–3 establishes two truths: humanity rebelled; God promised redemption.
    • The promise develops through redemptive history. Abraham’s family would become a nation through whom all peoples would be blessed. David would have a son on an eternal throne. The prophets add the Messiah’s suffering, rejection, virgin birth, Bethlehem birthplace, good news for the poor, sin-bearing work, and an Elijah-like messenger preparing the Lord’s way.
    • Four hundred silent years do not mean inactivity. God preserves Abraham’s family and David’s line, raises and removes kings, governs nations, and directs history toward Gabriel’s announcement to Zechariah.
    • Gabriel then comes to Mary in Nazareth, pledged to Joseph, a descendant of David. Mary also asks a question, but unlike Zechariah she does not demand a sign. Gabriel promises Jesus, Son of the Most High, David’s heir, eternal ruler over Jacob, whose kingdom will never end.
    • Josh gathers the threads: the woman’s offspring, Abraham’s blessing, David’s son, Israel’s king, and the world’s Savior converge upon two pregnancies humanly impossible—an old woman and a virgin.
    • Paul’s explanation in Galatians identifies Abraham’s singular promised offspring as Christ. One promise, one Savior, one people redeemed by grace through faith, and one king gathering every tribe, language, people, and nation. Jesus is Israel’s long-awaited Messiah but not Israel’s possession: he is king for all.
    • Mary asks how this will happen; Gabriel says the Spirit will overshadow her, the holy child will be God’s Son, Elizabeth is already six months pregnant, and no word from God will fail.
    • Mary’s response supplies the model: “I am the Lord’s servant. May your word to me be fulfilled.”

  5. 23:59 Faith trusts God’s actual promises rather than turning preferences into prophecy

    Scripture: 2 Corinthians 1:20 Romans 8:17–18 Romans 10:13 John 6:37 Romans 8:1 Romans 8:38–39 2 Corinthians 12:9 Philippians 1:6 1 Thessalonians 4:16–17 1 Corinthians 15:25–26, 51–57 Revelation 21:3–4 Hebrews 13:5 Matthew 10:28 Luke 1:37 2 Corinthians 1:20

    • Between Mary’s question and song, Josh sees a woman whose governing desire is simply to serve the Lord: “So be it, God. Whatever you say.”
    • He pauses before celebrating promise-fulfillment to distinguish what God has and has not promised. Christian theology must be built from God’s unfailing Word, not imported systems or personal wishes. Christians are not removed from suffering and ushered directly into glory; they suffer to glory. Satan’s way promises glory and brings suffering; God’s way openly includes suffering that leads to glory.
    • The error under examination is converting desires into divine promises and then accusing God of failing to do what he never promised. God does not promise every believer a spouse. Marriage is a genuine gift, but singleness can enable gospel ministry in places or situations family obligations would complicate. God does not promise every married couple a child, every sick person immediate healing, every dream’s fulfillment through intense belief, comfort, uninterrupted success, or freedom from suffering.
    • Core correction: Turning preferences into prophecy places the person on a throne belonging only to God. Faith is not confidence that God will eventually do what the believer wants. Faith is confidence that God will accomplish everything he has actually promised in Christ.
    • Josh then inventories secure promises: everyone who calls on the Lord will be saved; no one who comes to Jesus will be cast away; there is no condemnation in Christ; nothing can separate believers from God’s love; grace is sufficient in weakness; God will complete his begun work; the dead in Christ will rise; sin and death will not have the final word; Jesus will return, every enemy will be defeated, and the final enemy—death—will be destroyed; every tear will be permanently removed and God’s people will dwell with him forever.
    • Pastoral contrasts apply those promises: human plans fail; God’s plans do not; human prophetic timelines are probably wrong; God’s is right; pain may remain, but God will not leave; disease may take the body but cannot touch the soul; tears may flow while grace overflows.
    • Conclusion: no word from God will fail; God remembers every promise, and every promise receives its “Yes” in Christ.

  6. 28:57 God confirms his promise in the meeting of Mary, Elizabeth, and their unborn sons

    Scripture: Luke 1:39 Luke 1:36 John 4:6–7 Luke 9:23 Luke 1:41–45

    • Second major claim: God confirms what he promised.
    • After learning Elizabeth is six months pregnant, Mary hurries to Judea. The list of what Mary does not know is much longer than what she knows. She does not know Joseph’s response, Nazareth’s judgment, the social cost of pregnancy outside marriage, or what raising the Son of God will require. Her uncertainty is illustrated with a line from Four Christmases and Josh’s comic questions about parenting Jesus.
    • Faith does not receive a full step-by-step map for denying self, taking up the cross, and following Jesus. Faith takes the next step. Mary’s next step takes her into Zechariah and Elizabeth’s home.
    • Josh humorously imagines the household: an older pregnant woman, a young virgin pregnant by God’s promise, and a mute priest who understands but cannot explain; many pregnancy hormones and Zechariah unusually silent during football; a “holy baby shower,” not an ordinary one.
    • When Mary greets Elizabeth: John leaps in the womb; Elizabeth is filled with the Holy Spirit; she blesses Mary and the child, calls Mary “the mother of my Lord,” and blesses her for believing the Lord would fulfill his promise.
    • The women are related by blood; the babies are joined by the Holy Spirit. Jesus has not yet preached, worked a miracle, or even become visible through Mary’s pregnancy, yet Elizabeth worships. John cannot preach from the womb, so he leaps. Elizabeth calls Jesus “Lord” before hearing his name. No one can see him, but everyone responds to his presence.

  7. 34:15 Faith sees the greater unseen reality and calls Mary blessed because God is with her

    Scripture: 2 Kings 6:8–17 Matthew 14:28–31 Romans 8:38–39 Revelation 19:11–16 Luke 1:42 John 19:25–27 Isaiah 7:14; Matthew 1:23

    • Josh compares the unseen Christ in Luke 1 to Elisha at Dothan. Aram’s army surrounds the city overnight. Elisha’s servant sees horses, chariots, and soldiers and panics. Elisha says those with them are more than those against them and prays for the servant’s eyes to be opened. God opens his eyes to the hills full of horses and chariots of fire. The heavenly army did not arrive when Elisha prayed; it had been there all along.
    • Fear came from seeing only half of reality. The servant saw enemies but not allies, what was against him but not who was for him. Peter likewise became consumed by the waves.
    • Luke 1’s visible scene is unimpressive—no throne, crown, army, or even visible pregnancy—yet John leaps, Elizabeth worships, Mary believes, and Zechariah longs to sing.
    • Josh applies the contrast to hearers who can see only what surrounds them: diagnosis, financial pressure, conflict, a drifting child, a cold marriage, persistent grief, temptation, criticism, and everything apparently impossible. Christianity does not require pretending those forces are unreal or never overwhelming; they are simply not the whole picture.
    • Prayer and assurance: may God open their eyes to the greater reality; their king reigns, heaven is not panicking, and nothing against them can separate them from God’s love.
    • The heavenly army is led by King Jesus. Josh quotes Revelation’s warrior king: Faithful and True, judging with justice, blazing eyes, heaven’s armies behind him, ruling with an iron scepter, treading God’s wrath, named King of Kings and Lord of Lords. His throne may be unseen, but believers know it is there.
    • Why is Mary blessed? Not because her circumstances are easy: she risks shame, losing Joseph, social rejection, and eventually watching her son die on the cross. She is blessed because the divine presence once approached only in the holy place is growing within her. Jesus is Immanuel, God with us.

  8. 39:02 God overturns the world through his promised king and dethrones human pride

    Scripture: Luke 1:48–49 Luke 1:46–55 Romans 5:8 1 Corinthians 1:29–31; Galatians 6:14 Luke 1:51–53 Luke 14:11 Matthew 10:34 2 Peter 3:9

    • Third major claim: God overturns the world through his promised king.
    • Mary understands the scale of God’s act: every generation will call her blessed because the Mighty One has done great things and his name is holy.
    • Her song is the Magnificat because it magnifies God rather than Mary. God is mighty: nothing lies beyond his power. God is holy: no darkness, corruption, injustice, or evil is in him. God is merciful: he regards helpless sinners with compassion.
    • Josh connects those attributes to the gospel. God demonstrates love while sinners are still sinners by Christ’s death. Holiness means sin cannot be ignored; mercy means sinners are not abandoned to hopelessness; might means God accomplishes what sinners cannot. At the cross, God does not dilute holiness to show mercy; Jesus bears the judgment holiness requires so undeserving sinners receive mercy. Therefore believers cannot boast in themselves but only in Jesus Christ.
    • Transition illustration: Josh’s wife and family will soon lead a song; her small physical size should not be mistaken for weakness. Mary is often imagined as petite, gentle, and quiet, yet her song is a declaration of war against human pride. She sings of the proud scattered, rulers removed, the humble lifted, and the rich sent away empty. She effectively sings one of her son’s recurring sermons before he preaches it: those who exalt themselves will be humbled, and the humble exalted.
    • The promised king puts everything in its proper place. Herod may occupy a throne but is not king. Caesar may imagine himself divine but is not sovereign. Rome may dominate the neighborhood but does not control history.
    • Application first turns inward rather than toward disliked politicians, celebrities, false teachers, or arrogant enemies. Before anticipating God’s humbling of others, believers should let him humble them. A person humbled by God does not desire another’s destruction but prays for enemies to come to faith.
    • Pride is hard to confront because proud hearers assume the sermon is for someone else. Pride is not limited to boasting; it often appears as control of spouse, children, church, outcomes, and narratives. Control is fear wearing a crown—Herod all over again. Fear climbs the throne and manipulates life to preserve personal kingship. Jesus does not advise people on improving their own kingdoms; he removes them from a throne they never belonged on and announces his kingdom. Entry is as a humbled servant, not a rival king.
    • Pride constructs a private world where the self is never truly wrong. Anger becomes “righteous anger,” criticism becomes passion or discernment, control becomes headship, gossip becomes a prayer request, unforgiveness becomes wisdom, and stubbornness becomes conviction. Pride admits wrong only after explaining who made the wrong necessary: “I did this because you did that.” Jesus destroys that false reality: humans are creatures, not Creator; sinners, not Savior; servants, not king.

  9. 45:36 The closing diagnostic: what does your life magnify?

    Scripture: Luke 1:46–47

    • The only fitting song echoes Mary: “My soul magnifies the Lord, and my spirit rejoices in God my Savior.”
    • Josh asks what each hearer magnifies and gives two diagnostics. Follow your joy: what makes the heart come alive reveals what it magnifies. Some barely respond to the gospel but erupt at a touchdown, promotion, grandchild, or delivered package. These are good gifts to enjoy with gratitude; the issue is whether the gift awakens more joy than the Giver. Follow your wallet: spending patterns also reveal what the heart enlarges and prizes.

  10. 47:21 Humble yourself, break the silence, and offer your own Magnificat

    Scripture: Luke 14:11

    • Josh asks everyone to bow, become quiet, and stop using the sermon to inspect other people. Each person should stand before God as an individual servant and identify unconfessed pride allowed to fester. This is not a once-every-twenty-years exercise; a believer may need it repeatedly in one week.
    • If God exalts the humble and humbles the self-exalted, wisdom is to humble oneself now. Josh asks whether each person is walking humbly before God. Some have not spoken to God in a long time; the God who broke four hundred years of silence and came down calls them to break their silence and run to him.
    • He prepares the room for prayer, worship, fellowship, and the Lord’s Table, which will recall salvation’s cost. Worship while retaining pride is not worth performing. Pride and long-held bitterness must be released; hearers should ask the Lord to reveal and convict them.
    • Each person may need to sing a different “Magnificat” in response: confess pride and ask forgiveness; bring bitterness to God and ask him to heal it; praise freely, with the freshness of first hearing the gospel, even lifting a hand without shame; ask for cleansing and renewal; begin investing financially in God’s kingdom rather than wasting what God supplied.
    • The whole church needs humbling. Josh closes by leading them before the throne in worship, praise, and fellowship with Mary’s posture: “Lord, whatever happens, we are your servants.”
