Overview

  1. 00:18 The schedule changes because tomorrow is not ours to command

    Scripture: James 4:13–17

    • Josh opens by revising the series plan in a way that illustrates the day’s text: the church intended ten weeks in James, but week nine has only reached chapter 4. Rather than rush important material merely to launch another series, he scraps the next planned series for now — it will come sometime in 2026 — and welcomes the church to week nine of an eleven-week study.
    • He restates James’s recurring contrast: Worldly wisdom is built on human ideas about who God is and how God behaves. Divine wisdom receives God’s self-revelation in his Word and in Jesus Christ. Trying to define God better than God defines himself is audacious arrogance; receiving even what people cannot yet understand is humility.
    • The transition into the sermon’s subject is logical: people who presume to define God will soon presume to direct God. Those who trust God’s sovereignty, by contrast, cease insisting on an independent will and surrender with a joy greater than their circumstances.
    • Last week concerned being at war with God; this week concerns God’s will. A person can have peace with God yet resist his will, receive mercy yet reject purpose, possess eternal life yet waste this life, or be redeemed yet refuse faithful risks.
    • Josh illustrates the damage believers can do outside God’s will: Jonah’s disobedience endangered the sailors (Jonah 1:1–16). Lot’s move toward Sodom exposed his family to wickedness and harm (Genesis 13:10–13). David’s sin with Uriah’s wife brought misery upon his household and kingdom (2 Samuel 11:1–12:14). Peter confessed Jesus as Messiah and later denied knowing him three times (Matthew 16:13–16; 26:57–75).
    • Because God went to great lengths to save his children, Josh argues, he also has wise purposes for them. God’s ways are above human ways (Isaiah 55:8–9); believers should want to know, obey, and surrender to his will.
    • Josh announces James 4:13–17 as the primary text and reads the whole unit (James 4:13–17): self-confident merchants plan when and where they will trade and profit, although they do not know tomorrow; human life is a vanishing mist; plans should be conditioned upon the Lord’s will; arrogant boasting is evil; and refusing known good is sin.
    • He previews the organizing frame: two negative responses to God’s will followed by one positive response.

  2. 05:40 First negative response: ignoring God’s will and living under the illusion of control

    Scripture: James 5:1–6 Isaiah 14:13–14 Luke 12:15–22

    • To ignore God’s will is to live as if God does not exist, does not care, or has no interest in human affairs. James’s “Now listen” is an urgent demand to pay attention.
    • Reading the passage alongside James’s opening rebuke in chapter 5, Josh understands the audience as prosperous businesspeople inside the church (James 5:1–6). They make strategic plans and measure success by goals and closed deals without praying about God’s purposes or how prosperity might bless God and neighbor.
    • Josh turns the ancient merchants toward the congregation: today’s planners, dreamers, administrative enthusiasts, and business strategists can commit the same sin. Planning itself is not wrong; the rebuke concerns planning that gives no thought to God.
    • Their repeated “I will” resembles the proud self-direction attributed to Satan in Isaiah 14:13–14. The merchants choose the time, place, duration, method, and desired profit. Their fault is not what they considered but the decisive reality they omitted.
    • Jesus’s warning against greed and parable of the rich fool exposes that omission (Luke 12:15–22): The rich man’s fields prosper, so he decides to enlarge his barns, preserve his surplus, and secure years of ease. God calls him a fool and demands his life that very night. Hoarding for oneself is the opposite of being rich toward God, and Jesus immediately warns his disciples not to worry about food or clothing.
    • Josh presses the parable’s urgency. Greed is more dangerous than a robber because it can take the soul rather than merely the wallet. The world disguises greed as success through screens, feeds, advertisements, and the heart’s demand for more, better, newer, mine, and now.
    • Possessions—car, savings, house, plans, business, retirement account—cannot satisfy. Josh recalls the miraculous catch and the fishermen’s call: they did not build a barn for the fish because they were called to fish for people (Luke 5:4–11, with Josh’s “other side” wording resembling John 21:6).
    • Christ, though equal with God, became a servant (Philippians 2:6–7). If the Master served, believers should not crown themselves.
    • Applications follow in rapid sequence: Answer greed with sufficiency in Christ. Answer fear of loss by remembering that life is not constituted by possessions. Do not spend life building larger barns; invest it in God’s kingdom through generosity, forgiveness, proclamation of Christ, meeting needs, and unconditional love. Josh hopes Christ will commend Crossroads for faithful work, echoing the faithful-servant commendation in Matthew 25:21, 23.

  3. 12:22 The first reason to include God in every plan: life is uncertain

    Scripture: Proverbs 27:1 Proverbs 16:9 Romans 8:28

    • Josh gives the first of two reasons from James not to leave God out: the uncertainty of life.
    • He explicitly roots James’s warning in Proverbs 27:1: no one should boast about tomorrow because no one knows what a day will bring.
    • Life is complex—past and future, work and bills, people and places, decisions and goals—yet very little of that complexity lies within personal control.
    • Proverbs 16:9 supplies the corrective: human hearts plan a course, but the Lord establishes the steps.
    • What could drive an unbeliever mad becomes comfort for a Christian. The sovereign, omniscient, omnipotent, and loving God controls events and weaves what believers cannot know into his purpose.
    • Josh quotes Romans 8:28 and emphasizes its stated recipients: the promise belongs to those who love God and are called according to his purpose.
    • Illustration: after Josh and Kristen had once broken up, he drove nonstop from Los Angeles to Indiana, then ran back to her. At about 3:00 a.m. he encountered Missouri fog so dense that he could not see turns, signs, or even lane markings and had to slow drastically.
    • The fog pictures human uncertainty, not divine uncertainty. God has no blind spots and does not struggle to see what lies ahead. Confidence about the future therefore comes only through confidence in him.

  4. 15:41 The second reason: life is a mist, so invest it rather than waste it

    Scripture: James 4:14 Isaiah 38:1 Job 7:6 Job 7:9 Job 8:9 Psalm 90:12 Psalm 39:4 Mark 8:34–37

    • James’s second reason is the brevity of life. Josh repeats James 4:14: human life is a mist that appears briefly and vanishes.
    • Human memory can look backward and make accumulated years feel long, but human beings cannot look forward. Against eternity, life is vapor—steam rising from hot coffee: “Get it while it’s hot.”
    • Sudden death teaches this brevity with unusual force. Old age can feel like the expected order; a younger death strips away the assumption that much time remains.
    • Josh invites the congregation to hear Isaiah’s warning to Hezekiah personally: put your house in order because you are going to die (Isaiah 38:1). Death respects neither character, age, optimism, nor experience.
    • Personal and congregational illustrations make the warning concrete: Josh’s strong, fit stepfather still died from cancer. His sister died at thirty-nine; death did not wait for her fortieth birthday. A woman in the church who had significant health struggles unexpectedly awoke to find that her husband and caregiver had died.
    • Josh turns to Job for ancient pictures of life’s brevity: Days move faster than a weaver’s shuttle (Job 7:6). A cloud vanishes, and those who descend to the grave do not return (Job 7:9). People were born only yesterday, know nothing, and pass like a shadow (Job 8:9).
    • Approaching his fortieth birthday, Josh treats birthdays as occasions for reflection rather than merely parties: Is he helping or hurting people? Shepherding well? Using his moments and gifts or wasting them?
    • God calls for that inventory daily, not yearly: Psalm 90:12 asks God to teach his people to number their days so they may gain wisdom. Psalm 39:4 asks to know life’s end, the number of one’s days, and how fleeting life is.
    • Josh has calculated that this is day 14,607 of his life—including leap days—but cannot know whether he will see day 14,608 or 28,000.
    • He confronts a common distortion: people know intellectually that they will die but still assume death is too distant to affect present behavior. Even late in life people tend to calculate in favor of more time and cling to life as it ends.
    • A wiser question is not why someone else died early but what makes anyone think the same cannot happen to them. Health, weight loss, or avoidance of one disease cannot guarantee freedom from another.
    • Therefore life and gifts must not merely be used; they must be invested in what has eternal significance. Jesus’s call in Mark 8:34–37 frames the choice as a profit-and-loss statement: deny self, take up the cross, follow Christ, lose life for Christ and the gospel in order to save it, and do not gain the world at the cost of the soul.
    • When time has been spent, it cannot be purchased back. Josh returns to the coffee image through a humorous household story: Kristen repeatedly reheats coffee, leaves the cup in the microwave, and Josh finds it when warming lunch. He once found it irritating but now sees it as an opportunity to serve her.
    • Unlike coffee, life cannot be reheated. This is the one opportunity: invite God into the planning and ensure that the planning pleases him.

  5. 25:59 Second negative response: knowing God’s will and deliberately disobeying it

    Scripture: James 4:17 2 Peter 2:21–22 Hebrews 12:6 1 Corinthians 9:27 Joshua 24:15 James 4:15

    • Disobedience is worse than mere neglect. Josh repeats James 4:17: knowing the good one ought to do and refusing to do it is sin.
    • This attitude says openly, “I know what you want me to do; I just don’t want to do it.” It boasts in control even though control is an illusion.
    • Josh quotes 2 Peter 2:21–22: it would have been better not to know the way of righteousness than to know it and turn away; the proverb about a dog returning to its vomit makes deliberate apostasy viscerally repulsive. The embedded proverb comes from Proverbs 26:11.
    • Some disobedience also grows from misunderstanding God’s will. Believers may treat it as an option, but Josh calls it an obligation: God is Lord and Savior; believers were purchased, adopted, and made servants, and therefore must obey.
    • Obedience is not the enemy of liberty. Ultimate freedom is found within God’s will.
    • Counseling illustration: cohabiting couples do not usually approach Josh to celebrate a superior form of dating; they arrive in misery and confusion. His diagnosis is that they rejected God’s design and then expected blessing within disobedience. Believers should never expect God to bless either disobedience or discontentment.
    • Discipline should reassure rather than merely frighten: the Lord disciplines those he loves (Hebrews 12:6). Josh compares it to disciplining his son Nolan—the process is not pleasant, but its motive communicates love.
    • He rejects the picture of Christian life as miserable rule-keeping. Holiness frees him from intoxication, theft, lies, sexual unfaithfulness, and covetousness; those sins did not make life good. Serving God, giving, and “slaying sin in the name of Jesus” are his joy.
    • He contrasts earthly pleasure with heavenly treasure (Matthew 6:19–20) and says the crown follows the cross: Satan advertises glory that ends in suffering, whereas God tells the truth about suffering that will be transformed into glory.
    • Josh attributes his next quotation to Paul’s “second letter to the Corinthians,” but the words are actually 1 Corinthians 9:27: Paul disciplines his body so that, after preaching, he will not be disqualified for the prize. Josh interprets the prize not as salvation but as rewards when the Lord examines believers’ works.
    • Disobedience has consequences now, at Christ’s return, or both. Whatever faithful believers miss on earth will be more than repaid in the Lord’s presence; therefore they should discipline themselves and await the crown.
    • He closes the negative responses with Joshua 24:15—choose whom to serve; “as for me and my household, we will serve the Lord”—then connects Joshua’s resolve to James 4:15: “If it is the Lord’s will, we will live and do this or that.”

  6. 33:05 Positive response: lifelong humble dependence upon the sovereign God

    Scripture: Revelation 4:11 Ephesians 1:11 Romans 8:28

    • Against practical atheism and self-sovereignty, James’s positive response is humble dependence.
    • This is not superstition or a one-time phrase attached to plans. It is surrender: persistent, lifelong, daily submission in every decision and season—“Lord, I want what you want.”
    • James’s brief sentence opens the immense doctrine of sovereignty. Josh moves beyond merely saying God created in Genesis 1: God is not only Creator and uncaused cause but present Sustainer. Creation exists not only because he once spoke but because he continues to uphold it.
    • Every sunrise, sunset, heartbeat, breath, ruler, household, and even sparrow lies within God’s knowledge and care; the sparrow language echoes Matthew 10:29–31.
    • Josh presents the mystery of providence: God guides human plans while people make real choices, and divine sovereignty does not destroy creaturely freedom but fulfills it. Rather than fuel factional argument, that mystery should put believers on their knees in worship.
    • Corruption, disorder, pain, struggle, and life’s storms never occur outside God’s knowledge or control. Even when his action and reasons are hidden, there are no exceptions to his rule.
    • Like heaven’s elders, the church should declare Revelation 4:11: God is worthy of glory, honor, and power because he created all things and by his will they exist.
    • “He is worthy of it all” becomes the congregation’s answer. Thoughts, possessions, and treasures all belong to God, even when the world looks chaotic.
    • Josh then quotes Ephesians 1:11: God works everything in conformity with the purpose of his will.
    • The Spirit within the redeemed longs to obey. The same hand that orders the universe holds each believer; the wisdom governing governments watches households and hears prayers. Josh returns to Romans 8:28: the sovereign God is working all things for his glory and his people’s good.
    • He wishes believers lived as though they could see the “cloud of witnesses” behind them (Hebrews 12:1) and the heavenly forces contending for them. Such sight, he suggests, would change their lives.

  7. 38:25 Failure is not final: restoration and a growing knowledge of God’s will

    Scripture: 1 Timothy 2:4 2 Peter 3:9

    • A believer’s stumble does not destroy the relationship. Josh grieves the pattern in which fallen believers cut ties with the church or are effectively cast out.
    • Paul’s restorative command in Galatians 6:1–2 governs the church’s response: when a Christian is drowning, failing to restore gently and carry the burden also places the observer outside God’s will.
    • Falling, missing the mark, discipline, and suffering are not the end. Abraham, Jonah, David, and Peter display God’s persistence with faltering saints—what many call the perseverance of the saints.
    • Growth in God’s will begins by knowing it: Paul tells Timothy that God wants all people to be saved and to come to a knowledge of the truth (1 Timothy 2:4). Peter says God does not want anyone to perish but everyone to come to repentance (2 Peter 3:9).
    • Within God’s desire to save sinners, believers perceive his saving plan, commands, and guidance into holiness. They must not merely be aware of his will but be filled and overflowing with it.
    • God’s will is not something to pick up and put down at convenience, because that would only preserve self-will. Maturing believers increasingly understand that every part of life matters to God.

  8. 40:47 Christ reveals the family business, and renewed minds learn it through practice

    Scripture: John 15:15 Colossians 2:9 Romans 12:2

    • Josh uses his son, who is eleven, almost twelve as an analogy: Nolan knows his father’s rules but does not yet grasp the full reasons for them. In the same way, God wants his children not only to know what he wills but increasingly to understand why it matters.
    • Jesus calls disciples friends rather than servants because he has made known everything learned from the Father (John 15:15).
    • Christ can reveal the Master’s business because all the fullness of deity lives bodily in him (Colossians 2:9). In Jesus, God shines light into darkness and brings believers into the divine family work of saving souls, being the church, and carrying hope into the world.
    • Entering that work does not eliminate bad days. Josh and worship pastor Eli both have them; a stumble while learning does not invalidate the calling.
    • Romans 12:2 gives the learning process: reject conformity to the world, be transformed by a renewed mind, and thereby test and approve God’s good, pleasing, and perfect will.
    • Discernment develops through practiced obedience in easy and hard conditions. Scripture trains the ear to hear God; repeated listening and obedience make recognizing his will more natural, as with any learned skill.

  9. 43:15 Take the next step, learn under Jesus’s yoke, and obey from the heart

    Scripture: Matthew 11:29 Psalm 40:8 John 4:34

    • Josh reduces the immediate response to one command: take a step. Mature life-group leaders were not always knowledgeable Christians; they leaned in and worked.
    • He recalls when he could not sit through a sermon without getting water or snacking on communion, much less write an eleven-part James series. Growth came through effort and attention.
    • New believers will encounter harsh or “crusty” Christians, but should not let others look down on them (1 Timothy 4:12). Keep eyes on Jesus, avoid comparison, and learn from failures as well as successes.
    • Jesus’s invitation in Matthew 11:29 provides the posture: take his yoke, learn from the gentle and humble Master, and find rest. Believers should walk closely with Jesus and practice obedience under the gentle Shepherd.
    • Josh insists twice that obedience must come from the heart. Jonah knew God’s command and eventually obeyed after the fish, but his heart remained resistant; he served to avoid another painful correction rather than because he loved God (Jonah 1:1–4:3).
    • Friends of Jesus instead delight to continue his work: David says, “I desire to do your will, my God; your law is within my heart” (Psalm 40:8). Jesus says his food is to do the will of the One who sent him and finish his work (John 4:34).
    • God’s will becomes praise, freedom, and nourishment when obeyed from the heart.
    • When believers fall short, they should rise, confess, receive forgiveness and purification (1 John 1:9), and resume faithful risk-taking within grace. The decisive matter is not a flawless success rate but a genuine heart that loves God, desires his will, and aims at his glory.

  10. 46:11 Communion response: face God’s will now, confess, remember the gospel, and surrender

    • Josh moves into communion and invites those who trust Christ to participate. Those still unsure of their standing with God may let the elements pass: bread and juice cannot save; only the true God can.
    • Anyone ready to entrust life to Christ can enter the prepared baptism waters and take communion there for the first time.
    • He asks each person to examine privately how they approach God’s will: Do they plan without considering it? Ignore God, his gifts, or his expectations? Know what he commands and still refuse?
    • This cannot be answered by a group, spouse, or preacher. Each individual must stand before God. The conversation is unavoidable; the choice is whether to have it willingly now or be forced to face it later.
    • Crossroads’s pastors and elders offer prayer, teaching, encouragement, and companionship, but each hearer must use this quiet moment to confess and repent.
    • Communion remembers the gospel: Jesus took sinners’ sin to the cross, satisfied divine wrath, rose again, and offers new life to believers—a material summary of 1 Corinthians 15:3–4.
    • In the closing prayer Josh asks God to: Make his voice heard and direct those seeking purpose and meaning. Help the congregation recognize and surrender to the work God has given. Free people trapped in a “tornado of shame” over yesterday and fill their minds with grace and mercy. Apply salvation not merely as a past rescue but as daily deliverance. Direct their steps and transform the church, hearts, homes, and marriages so their lives reflect God’s glory, goodness, and honor.
    • He closes the prayer at 50:47 with “Amen.”
