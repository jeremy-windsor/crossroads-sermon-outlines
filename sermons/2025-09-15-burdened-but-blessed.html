Overview

  1. 00:16 Grief, gratitude, and the blessing of a transformed church

    Scripture: Psalm 34:18

    • Josh opens emotionally and thanks the church for praying for his family, accepting his weariness, and preaching the hope of Jesus back to him. His prior church experience was marked by pain, disappointment, and institutions that knew the right words but did not love as God loves. God overturned that cynicism by giving him a vision of a church that knows truth and permits the gospel to transform people from the inside out. He describes Crossroads as a once-shipwrecked vessel being revived for God’s glory through a return to God and submission to his will.
    • Josh recounts writing the sermon beside his mother after the death of her husband. Together they counted both blessings and losses; both lists were long. In two years she had lost a daughter, a brother, and a husband, yet told Josh that nothing she lost could remove praise from her lips because God is good. Josh calls this a mother’s sermon to her son.
    • Psalm 34:18: the Lord remains close to the brokenhearted and saves those crushed in spirit. Grief strips away lesser concerns and prompts a reset around what truly matters. Josh confesses that he often lives in the past, evaluating what could have been done better, or in the future, planning what must be done next, while neglecting what God is doing now. He cannot control the church’s history or predict its future, but if Christ remains central, neither uncertainty is decisive.
    • Transition: he thanks the congregation for the way their gospel transformation blesses him, then announces the day’s topics—wealth, eternal rewards, temptation, and God’s unshakable goodness.

  2. 04:00 James 1:9–11: trials mature believers and reorder status

    Scripture: James 1:9–11 Hebrews 12:6

    • Josh identifies James 1:9–18 as the day’s continuing primary text and reads verses 9–11. Believers in humble circumstances should boast in their high position. Rich believers should boast in their humiliation because wealth and its beauty fade like a flower under scorching heat.
    • He places the paragraph within the series’ three-week opening treatment of James 1. Trials are inevitable, and immaturity in the church is a real problem. A trial gives an immature believer an opportunity to grow; God ordinarily forms character through lived circumstances rather than merely through books, sermons, songs, poems, or counseling. Character formation requires the believer’s cooperation through surrender and submission. When believers wander, the loving Father disciplines his children and brings them back.
    • Josh distinguishes maturity from sinlessness. Jesus supplied the perfection sinners could not. God’s goal in believers is a complete, mature work—“perfection in the faith”—rather than autonomous sinless performance.
    • Parenting illustration: Children must eventually grow up and leave shelter, even though greater independence exposes them to danger. Christians who refuse faith, risk, and bold obedience remain spiritually sheltered and immature.
    • Transition: one practical arena for maturity is learning to view both self and wealth as God does.

  3. 06:30 Poor and rich believers must boast in their position in Christ

    Scripture: Jeremiah 9:23–24 1 Thessalonians 4:11 Philippians 3:20–21 James 2:5

    • Josh explains the original socioeconomic setting. James writes to Jewish Christians displaced from Jerusalem and rebuilding in Syria and northern Palestine. Their economic outcomes differ, but all share the condition of being outcasts.
    • To the poor believer: look beyond present circumstances and boast in the high position received from God. Josh distinguishes healthy boasting from sinful pride through Jeremiah 9:23–24: wisdom, strength, and riches are not grounds for boasting; knowing the Lord—who delights in kindness, justice, and righteousness—is. Paul’s call to live quietly does not mean remaining silent about Christ. Josh warns that a faith that never offends anyone may have become too quiet. The real question is not whether one boasts, but what so defines a person that even strangers can see it. Christians must be publicly defined by their position in Christ rather than by what they possess or lack. Philippians 3:20–21: believers’ citizenship is in heaven, and Christ will transform their lowly bodies to be like his glorious body. James 2:5: those poor in the world’s eyes may be rich in faith and heirs of the promised kingdom.
    • To the rich believer: boast in the low position embraced in Christ rather than accepting the world’s elevation of wealth. The world regards wealthy Christians as irrational when they give sacrificially instead of maximizing comfort or early retirement. Josh answers that kingdom giving is itself an investment. Illustration: a very wealthy Christian drove a battered twenty-year-old Honda. His voluntary self-denial ministered to Josh because wealth did not dictate his identity. Clarification: the point is not that Christians may never replace a car or own something good; it is that possessions, ability, success, reputation, and worldly position must never become their boast.
    • Transition: because worldly wealth easily exposes immaturity, God gives believers a way to convert temporary resources into eternal investment.

  4. 11:09 Stop giving God scraps: tithing, sacrifice, and eternal investment

    Scripture: 1 Timothy 6:7 John 20:25 Luke 23:32–33; Galatians 3:13 Mark 15:34 2 Corinthians 5:18–21 James 1:11

    • Josh introduces why he and Kristen give at least ten percent of all they earn to the local church, then delivers his central confrontation: “Stop giving God your scraps and calling it generosity.” In a 90/10 frame, God is the generous giver of the ninety; returning ten does not make the recipient generous. Humans enter and leave the world with nothing; everything belongs to God.
    • He contrasts complaints that giving “hurts” with Christ’s sacrifice. Jesus endured nails, public condemnation, the curse, forsakenness, and the rupture required to reconcile sinners to God. Josh therefore refuses to treat a tithe as sacrifice on the scale of the cross.
    • Congregational application: Given the visible resources of the church body, the church should not face chronic financial inability, the care-center shelves should not remain empty, and genuine needs among members should not go unmet. Help must retain wise structure and avoid enabling destructive patterns, but Jesus’ people follow him into sacrifice.
    • Josh gives seven ordered reasons that he and Kristen tithe: Without the tithe, the Lord’s house cannot function as the hope of the world. Tithing expresses trust that God will meet their needs. It renews the mind rather than allowing conformity to the world. It unifies them with the church’s mission and purpose. It gives their work eternal significance; Kristen’s bank career contributes to ministry just as Josh’s church work does. It breaks greed and self-reliance and moves wealth from temporary use to eternal purpose. It shows that God is first in their home.
    • Returning to James 1:11, Josh states the unavoidable outcome: either a person loses wealth or wealth loses the person—one goes broke or dies. Kingdom investment outlives both outcomes. Buildings decay, but lives reached by the gospel are sealed forever in Christ.
    • Transition: how believers view self and wealth matters; Josh turns next to eternal rewards.

  5. 15:27 James 1:12: blessing is humble endurance crowned with life

    Scripture: James 1:12 James 1:2–3 1 Corinthians 9:25 Revelation 2:10

    • Josh reads James 1:12: the one who perseveres under trial is blessed and, after standing the test, receives the crown of life promised to those who love God. This returns to the previous week’s teaching from James 1:2–3 that many kinds of trials should be counted as joy because tested faith produces perseverance. Verse 12 adds the future reward attached to perseverance: the crown of life.
    • Josh identifies verse 12 as a beatitude and corrects two misunderstandings of blessing. Blessing does not flow from worldly alliances; it accompanies humble endurance. “Blessed” does not mean emotionally happy. A believer can be deeply sad and genuinely blessed at the same time. Emotions fluctuate; assurance rests in enduring by faith, keeping one’s eyes on Jesus, and receiving God’s favor.
    • “Trial” includes anything that threatens love for Christ or faithful obedience—disagreement, division, disease, divorce, and death. Last week emphasized present joy within trials; this movement emphasizes the future reward beyond them.
    • Crown imagery: James’s hearers would picture the victory wreath awarded to an athlete, not merely a jeweled royal crown. 1 Corinthians 9:25: athletes accept strict training for a perishable crown; Christians train for one that lasts forever. Josh’s athletic analogy stresses self-control, discipline, blood, sweat, tears, effort, and surrender to the process. Olympians sacrifice thousands of hours for roughly ninety seconds on a podium and then return to training. Revelation 2:10: Jesus tells suffering believers in Smyrna to remain faithful to death and promises life as the victor’s crown.
    • Josh answers the objection that Christians should never be motivated by reward. He rejects transactional obedience and “What have you done for me lately?” calculations. Nevertheless, God communicated promises so believers could remember them under pressure, reject material bait, prioritize spiritual reality, and rejoice during loss. Only a believer can properly value this reward; unbelievers do not boast in a victor’s crown of life. Application: stop starting pointless church fights over the legitimacy of reward language and serve someone instead—“go wash somebody’s feet.”
    • Transition: Josh moves from reward under trial to how believers should handle temptation.

  6. 20:28 James 1:13–15: God tests for maturity, but temptation exploits desire

    Scripture: James 1:13–15 James 1:2–4 Genesis 2:9 Genesis 2:16–17 Genesis 3:1–5

    • Josh reads James 1:13–15 and gives the conclusion first: the person responsible for one’s sin is oneself. He reinforces the point with a congregational exercise, asking everyone to point at the person responsible for their sin.
    • He distinguishes trials from temptations. God may send or allow trials that appeal to the Spirit’s work in believers and train them to listen, obey, and mature. Temptations appeal to sinful nature, seek rebellion against God, and may be deployed by Satan. Thanksgiving-plate illustration: Christians prefer trials and temptations not to “touch,” just as some people separate food, but James connects them closely. God is not temptation’s author, yet Satan can turn a sanctifying trial into a spiritually slippery occasion.
    • Josh shows how a trial can become a temptation to accuse God. Financial hardship can generate doubts about provision. Bereavement can generate doubts about love. Persecution combined with apparent wicked prosperity can generate doubts about justice. The proper response is to call out and run to God rather than complain against him.
    • He returns to Eden to explain freedom and choice. God placed the tree of life and the tree of the knowledge of good and evil in the garden, prohibited eating from the latter, and warned of death. God spoke first; the devil then twisted God’s word. Real freedom requires a meaningful choice. Without the possibility of disobedience, obedience would be automatic rather than freely rendered.
    • Application: Temptation itself is not the decisive problem; response is. Each right choice strengthens faith, while wishing temptation away does not produce maturity. God allows temptation not to destroy believers but as an occasion in which they may choose obedience and grow stronger with his help. These daily opportunities often begin before the first cup of coffee is finished.
    • Transition: having established where temptation does not originate, Josh asks where it does originate.

  7. 25:33 Desire becomes bait when it is pursued outside God’s will

    Scripture: James 1:14 Proverbs 19:3 Hebrews 13:4 Genesis 13:10–13 2 Samuel 11:2–12:23 Matthew 4:1–11 Galatians 6:1

    • Josh repeats James 1:14: each person is dragged away and enticed by his or her own evil desire. Proverbs 19:3 supplies the wisdom parallel: a person’s own folly produces ruin, yet the heart rages against the Lord. The pattern is self-caused harm followed by blame directed at God.
    • Josh broadens “desire” beyond sexual appetite. Hunger, thirst, sexual desire, sleep, and marriage have legitimate God-given places. The problem begins when people satisfy desires apart from God’s will. Ordered contrasts: eating versus gluttony; sleep versus laziness; sex versus sexual immorality; marriage versus same-sex union. Hebrews 13:4: marriage must be honored and the marriage bed kept pure because God judges adultery and sexual immorality. Diagnostic question: with respect to desire, are you servant or master?
    • Personal testimony and parental warning: Josh says Jesus enabled him to master a pornography addiction and saved his marriage. A choice at age fifteen developed into an addiction that nearly destroyed his marriage at twenty-nine. He urges parents to discuss pornography and temptation directly with children who are not hearing the sermon.
    • Marketing and fishing illustrations explain “enticed.” Satan does not advertise consequences with integrity or provide a surgeon general’s warning. Like tobacco marketing, temptation packages danger attractively. Josh says the Greek image behind “enticed” is “bait the hook”: the fish sees a shiny offer, not the hook, photograph, and meal awaiting it. Temptation is never presented as a naked hook; it promises that sin will be easier, better, faster, or pleasurable while covering what lies underneath.
    • Biblical examples: Lot saw Sodom’s well-watered land without foreseeing the outcome. David saw Bathsheba without seeing the suffering and tragedy that would follow.
    • Preventive application: Remain in Scripture so that, like Jesus in the wilderness, believers can discern God’s will from their own and surrender to his. Remain in fellowship so mature believers can restore anyone who falls and help that person return to the right path.
    • Transition: James changes metaphors from fishing to childbirth.

  8. 30:34 Desire, decision, consequence: sin gives birth to death, but God provides escape

    Scripture: James 1:15 Matthew 5:28 Genesis 2:17; 3:6–24; 4:1–8 1 Corinthians 10:13

    • Josh repeats James 1:15 and traces sin’s development: desire conceives, gives birth to sin, and mature sin gives birth to death.
    • He carefully locates moral culpability. The mere existence or intensification of a desire is not itself the “birth” of sin. Sin occurs when the idea becomes a decision—when someone acts on desire mentally or bodily. Jesus’ teaching about lust distinguishes noticing beauty from intentionally entertaining lustful thoughts or fantasies.
    • Maturity therefore governs reactions rather than obeying feelings. Immature Christians act from reluctance, hurt, revenge, or impulse without reflection. Mature Christians pursue what is right regardless of fluctuating emotions. Josh’s sequence is desire → decision → consequence; disobedience yields death rather than life.
    • He explains death as separation as well as physical cessation. Adam and Eve did not die physically the instant they ate, yet death entered immediately in spiritual, relational, and emotional forms. Fellowship with God and one another broke: they hid, covered nakedness, blamed one another, experienced fear, and eventually saw sin mature into Cain’s murder of Abel. A sin’s full death-bearing outcome may take years to appear.
    • Josh repeats the warning: “Don’t take the bait.”
    • 1 Corinthians 10:13 gives the alternative. Temptation is common rather than uniquely overpowering. God is faithful, limits what believers face, and provides a way out so they can endure. Practical response: pause, look for the exit, and ask a mature Christian for blunt counsel rather than rationalizing adultery, pornography, or another sin.
    • Transition: after the emphatic call to “find the way out,” Josh pauses, then turns to the final topic—God’s unshakable goodness.

  9. 35:06 James 1:16–18: meditate on the unchanging goodness of the Giver

    Scripture: James 1:16–17 Genesis 3:1–5 Matthew 4:2–4 Deuteronomy 6:10–12 Ephesians 1:3 James 1:18 1 Peter 4:17 2 Peter 1:4 James 1:15 James 1:18 Revelation 21:5

    • Josh reads James 1:16–17: believers must not be deceived; every good and perfect gift comes from the Father of heavenly lights, who does not change like shifting shadows.
    • The topics belong together rather than standing as isolated sermons. Meditating on God’s goodness is a means of overcoming temptation. Satan imitates God and seeks to make God appear withholding, as with Eve and with hungry Jesus in the wilderness. When believers doubt God’s goodness or look away from eternal reward, temporary bait begins to look good. Israel’s history illustrates the danger: people brought from slavery into land ownership forgot the God who delivered them.
    • Josh orders four truths in James’s wording: God gives only good gifts. Everything truly good comes from God; some gifts develop like seeds whose growth remains hidden before they sprout. God’s method of giving is good. Unlike a gift delivered manipulatively or in an “icky” manner, both God’s gift and his manner of giving are good. God gives by downpour. “Coming down” carries the continuing sense that gifts keep coming. Believers may not perceive the flow, but Scripture declares it; Ephesians 1:3 says God has blessed believers with every spiritual blessing in Christ. God is unchanging. His holiness means he cannot become worse; his perfection means he cannot become better.
    • If a shadow or distance exists between a believer and God, Josh assigns responsibility to the believer, not to an alteration in God. God cannot change, but through the gospel he makes human change possible.
    • Josh reads James 1:18: God chose to give believers birth through the word of truth so they would be firstfruits of creation.
    • Three ordered keys for fighting temptation: Cultivate a healthy fear of God; awareness that judgment begins with God’s household helps keep the church on course. Never forget God’s goodness; temptation loses its shimmer beside God’s promises. Walk according to the new divine nature rather than the old desire-driven one.
    • James redeems the childbirth image. Desire gives birth to sin and death, but God gives new birth through the word of truth. The redeemed are the opening act of God’s restoration of creation and reestablishment of Eden on earth. Revelation 21:5: the enthroned Lord declares that he is making all things new.

  10. 39:44 Two paths, one Deliverer, and the invitation to run to Jesus

    Scripture: James 1:14–15 James 1:2–4, 12 James 1:17 James 1:14–15 Matthew 4:1–11 Genesis 3:1–6 James 1:18 James 1:12 Hebrews 12:2 Matthew 27:29; James 1:12 John 1:12 Romans 6:6–14 1 Corinthians 15:54–57 1 Peter 1:3 Revelation 21:5 John 14:6 Acts 4:12 1 Corinthians 11:23–26 2 Corinthians 5:21 Ephesians 1:7

    • Josh gathers the sermon into two paths: Temptation → sin → death. Trials → perseverance → life. At the center stands the unchanging God, whose trials mature rather than destroy and whose greatest gift is new life through Jesus.
    • Everyone feels temptation’s inward lure: “just once,” “no one will know,” or “it’s fine.” Unresisted desire becomes decision and can carry a person away from God. Gospel hope is that believers do not have to bite or be dragged away; Jesus can deliver them every time as they run to him.
    • Jesus’ wilderness victory answers Adam’s garden failure. Satan whispered lies, twisted Scripture, and offered shortcuts, but Jesus stood on the word and won the battle sinners could not win. Where Adam failed, Jesus overcame; where human desire gives birth to sin, Jesus gives new life through the word of truth.
    • Jesus offers transformation, not forgiveness alone. He attacks sin’s root as well as its fruit. He gives power to resist, grace to stand, and mercy after a fall.
    • Josh returns to James 1:12. The crown of life awaits those who love God and endure, not because they never fail but because they keep returning to the life-giver. The victor’s crown is not for self-perfect people but for those who trust the perfect One.
    • Final appeal: The tired, weary, and wandering should join Josh in fixing their eyes on Jesus. Jesus wore the crown of thorns so believers could receive the crown of life; was treated as a criminal so they could become God’s children; broke sin’s power; defeated death; endured the cross; rose from the grave; gives new birth; makes all things new; and will return. Until then: do not take the bait. Turn and run to Jesus, the way, truth, and life; salvation is found in no other name.
    • 42:55–44:10 — Closing communion prayer: Josh thanks the Father for the elements that recall Christ’s sacrifice, for Jesus’ righteous life and substitutionary death, and for salvation through his blood. He prays that amid tragedy, loss, suffering, sickness, and sadness, the church would silence the noise, quiet heart and mind, reflect on Jesus, and trust him as the answer to what ails them.
