Overview

  1. 00:26 God’s promise to Abraham becomes an anchor for storm-tossed believers

    • Josh begins with the value of an anchor in rough water, places Hebrews’ discouraged first readers inside that storm, and then walks from creation to Abraham’s tested faith so God’s oath in Hebrews 6 can be understood in its biblical setting.

    1. 00:26 An anchor proves valuable when control is lost

      Scripture: Hebrews 2:1

      • An anchor is neither impressive nor necessary on glassy water; its worth appears when wind, waves, and current overwhelm a person’s ability to steer.
      • Josh compares that crisis to the Jewish Christians addressed by Hebrews. Following Jesus had become costly, persecution was increasing, and discouragement and confusion tempted some to quit, return to Judaism, or drift away.

    2. 02:11 Hebrews announces firm and secure hope behind the curtain

      Scripture: Hebrews 6:19–20

      • Josh opens with Hebrews 6:19–20: hope anchors the soul, enters the inner sanctuary, and rests where Jesus has gone as forerunner and eternal high priest in Melchizedek’s order.
      • He states the pastoral destination before beginning the journey through the text: believers striving toward Christlikeness must not fear that God will condemn them, because true believers can be certain of salvation.

    3. 03:03 The route to Hebrews 6 begins with humanity’s earliest history

      Scripture: Hebrews 6:13 Genesis 1:1–31 Genesis 2:18–24 Genesis 3:1–24 Genesis 6:5–8:19 Genesis 11:1–9

      • Although the day’s text begins at Hebrews 6:13, Josh first returns to the beginning: God creates the universe and humanity and provides the man a complementary helper.
      • Adam and Eve’s fall fills the earth with sinners; worsening evil leads to the flood, through which God preserves Noah’s family and the human race.
      • Afterward humanity gathers at Babel, seeks security and a great name through a tower, and is scattered when God confuses its language in response to arrogant self-reliance.

    4. 04:03 Abram leaves at seventy-five because he takes God at his word

      Scripture: Genesis 12:1–3

      • Josh quotes God’s call in Genesis 12: Abram must leave country, people, and household for an unknown land; God will make him a great nation and bless every people through him.
      • Abram departs at seventy-five on the strength of God’s word alone. Josh turns to retired hearers and says age does not put them on the bench: they can encourage younger believers, find a Timothy, meet needs, and remain on mission.

    5. 05:08 The promised heir does not arrive on Abraham’s timetable

      Scripture: Genesis 15:1–6 Philippians 1:21

      • In Genesis 15 Abram asks how the world can be blessed through him when his household heir is Eliezer. God promises a son from Abram’s own body and descendants like the stars; Abram believes, and God credits righteousness to him.
      • By Genesis 17 twenty-five years have passed without fulfillment. Josh challenges the assumption that Christ must return or a desired healing must occur on one generation’s timetable; God gave Abraham one promise to wait upon, while Christians possess many promises.
      • Rather than complain about God’s patience, believers should rejoice that living is Christ and dying is gain, and use the waiting time to share the gospel.

    6. 06:22 The impossible ages make clear that fulfillment requires God

      Scripture: Genesis 17:1–19 Genesis 18:10–15

      • God renames Abram and Sarai, promises that Sarah will become the mother of nations, and says she will bear Abraham a son. Abraham falls facedown laughing at the thought that he could father a son at one hundred and Sarah could bear one at ninety.
      • Sarah later laughs too. Josh treats their ages as Scripture’s deliberate emphasis: however long people lived, this birth was still humanly impossible and therefore had to be an act of God.
      • He briefly applies Sarah’s response to husbands, warning that a husband’s example gives his wife cues that matter.

    7. 07:45 Isaac arrives, and God tests love for the promise keeper

      Scripture: Genesis 21:1–3 Genesis 22:1–14

      • Genesis 21 records that the Lord did for Sarah what he had promised: she bore Isaac at the appointed time. After twenty-five years, the promised son is finally present.
      • God then commands Abraham to take the son he loves to Moriah and offer him. Josh clarifies that God already knows Abraham’s heart; the test reveals its result to Abraham and to later readers, asking whether he treasures the promise or the promise keeper more.

    8. 08:52 Immediate obedience trusts that God will provide

      Scripture: Genesis 22:1–14

      • God’s promises unfold on God’s timetable, but obedience is not assigned the same delay. Abraham rises early the next morning; Josh declares that delayed obedience is doubt rather than obedience.
      • Isaac carries the wood and asks about the lamb. Abraham answers that God himself will provide it, a line Josh calls Abraham’s Good Friday sermon and the first time a father told his son about Jesus without knowing the full significance.
      • Abraham binds Isaac and reaches for the knife, but the angel stops him. A ram is sacrificed instead, and Abraham names the place ‘The Lord Will Provide.’

    9. 10:28 God swears by himself after Abraham obeys

      Scripture: Genesis 22:15–18

      • The angel repeats God’s promise with an oath: Abraham will be blessed, his descendants multiplied like stars and sand, and every nation blessed through his offspring because he obeyed.
      • Josh uses a childhood ‘cross my heart’ oath to show how people strengthen testimony by invoking something greater than themselves, preparing the question of how the greatest possible speaker confirms his word.

    10. 11:21 Hebrews 6 joins promise, oath, hope, and priesthood

      Scripture: Hebrews 6:13–20

      • Josh reads Hebrews 6:13–20. With no one greater by whom to swear, God swore by himself; after patient waiting Abraham received what was promised.
      • God confirms his unchanging purpose so the heirs of the promise can be encouraged by two unchangeable things and the impossibility of God lying.
      • Those heirs flee to seize the hope set before them, an anchor entering behind the curtain where Jesus has entered as forerunner and permanent high priest. Josh previews three reasons this offer is unbreakable and says he is addressing people and families presently wrestling with assurance.

  2. 12:32 Assurance rests on God’s character, promise, and oath

    • In the first two of his three reasons that God’s offer is unbreakable, Josh argues that the promise survives Abraham’s failures because it is grounded in God, includes all who belong to Christ, and is guaranteed by both God’s sworn character and the Spirit’s seal rather than by the fluctuating strength of a believer’s faith.

    1. 12:32 Abraham’s failures do not become the covenant’s foundation

      Scripture: Genesis 16:1–16 Genesis 17:17

      • Abraham lied about Sarah, tried to fulfill the promise through Hagar, doubted, laughed, and led passively amid family conflict.
      • Josh’s point is not that Abraham was perfect but that God’s covenant faithfulness was built on God’s promise. Many promises depend not on human character but on God’s faithfulness.

    2. 13:08 The faithful witnesses magnify God, while heirs must flee and cling

      Scripture: Hebrews 12:1 Hebrews 6:18

      • The cloud of witnesses reveals a repeated theme: their stories concern God’s faithfulness more than the greatness of their own faith.
      • ‘Fled to take hold’ contains duty, responsibility, and urgency. The farmer does not harvest from the porch but plows, plants, and pulls weeds while leaving the timing of rain to the Lord.
      • Likewise, a believer disconnected from church life, Scripture, and consistent prayer should not expect much harvest. Hope is God’s gift, yet believers actively prepare and cling.

    3. 14:10 The greatest possible witness swears by himself

      Scripture: Hebrews 6:16–17

      • As a courtroom witness invokes God to confirm an oath, the lesser normally calls upon the greater. Since no one is greater than God, God swears by himself.
      • The oath is not only for Abraham but for the heirs of the promise, making the unchanging nature of God’s purpose clear across generations.

    4. 14:41 Abraham’s promised offspring carries blessing to the nations

      Scripture: Galatians 3:29 2 Samuel 7:12–16 Romans 9:4–5 John 4:22 Romans 11:17–24

      • Galatians says those who belong to Christ are Abraham’s seed and heirs according to the promise. Isaac leads to Jacob, a nation, kings, David’s promised throne, and finally Jesus through David’s line.
      • What appeared to concern one son was God’s plan to bless the world through his only Son. God entrusted Israel with promises, covenants, law, and Messiah; salvation came through the Jews because Jesus came from Abraham’s line and David’s house.
      • Israel was not the destination but the delivery system through which blessing reaches every tribe, language, people, and nation. Gentile believers have been grafted by faith into the ancient promise.

    5. 16:22 Generations pass, but God’s unchanging purpose remains

      Scripture: Hebrews 6:17

      • God confirmed the promise with an oath so later generations would know his plan cannot fail: his saying is his doing.
      • Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, and David are gone, but God has neither changed his mind nor moved to a second plan. The God who promised Abraham a son promised the world a Savior, and through that Savior people from every nation become heirs.

    6. 17:16 A check is valuable because of the one backing it

      • A ten-million-dollar check is not trusted because of strong paper or impressive ink; the decisive question is whose resources and character stand behind it. A check from Josh would inspire nervousness, while one backed by unlimited funds would inspire confidence.
      • In the same way, Christian assurance is not built on the strength of one’s faith but on the character of the God who made, signed, and confirmed the promise with an oath.

    7. 18:22 The promised Spirit is the deposit guaranteeing the inheritance

      Scripture: Ephesians 1:13–14

      • Josh reads Ephesians 1: those who heard and believed the gospel were included in Christ and sealed with the promised Holy Spirit, the deposit guaranteeing their inheritance until redemption.
      • God has given more than money: his Son, promise, oath, and Spirit. Josh urges the church to trust the one behind the promise and to show more joy than someone carrying a fully backed check to the bank.

  3. 19:14 The Christian anchor is secured upward in heaven

    • For his third reason that God’s offer is unbreakable, Josh develops Hebrews’ anchor image: Christian hope does not descend to hold a ship where it is but reaches behind heaven’s veil, where Jesus’ presence as forerunner guarantees that those united to him will arrive.

    1. 19:14 The early church displayed one sure and steadfast hope

      Scripture: Hebrews 6:19

      • Josh repeats that believers possess a sure and steadfast anchor of the soul, then compares the anchor’s early-Christian visibility to WWJD bracelets: it appeared on tombs, rings, and walls.
      • The symbol testified that Christians are people of one hope. Unlike an earthly anchor dropped into sea, their anchor goes upward behind the veil; rather than merely holding still, it pulls them safely home to paradise and clings to the risen Savior.

    2. 20:13 Jesus enters glory as forerunner, not merely representative

      Scripture: Hebrews 6:20 Matthew 16:18

      • The old high priest entered the Holy of Holies alone, performed his work, came back out, and repeated it. No one followed him inside.
      • Jesus entered heaven not only as representative but as forerunner—one who goes first because others are coming behind. His presence guarantees their arrival and announces that he has made a way.
      • The rope cannot break, current and storm cannot dislodge the anchor, and the gates of hell cannot overcome Christ’s work. Everyone united to the risen Savior will one day be where he is.

    3. 21:09 A heavenward anchor gives weary believers reason not to quit

      • Because Christ is already there, the church can endure suffering, fight sin, and keep going through weariness.
      • Josh gathers the image into the sermon’s central declaration: believers do not quit because they are ‘anchored heavenward.’

  4. 21:28 Melchizedek’s order reveals Jesus’ superior and permanent priesthood

    • Josh assigns Hebrews 7, Genesis 14, and Psalm 110 for further study, then traces Hebrews’ argument that Melchizedek is greater than Abraham and Levi, that Psalm 110 authorizes a priest from Judah, and that Jesus serves by the power of an indestructible life.

    1. 21:28 The order of Melchizedek demands patient study

      Scripture: Hebrews 6:20 Hebrews 7 Genesis 14 Psalm 110

      • Josh returns to Hebrews 6:20’s striking title: Jesus is high priest forever in Melchizedek’s order.
      • Rather than spend the remaining sermon explaining Melchizedek at the expense of Jesus, he assigns slow reading of Hebrews 7 followed by Genesis 14 and Psalm 110.
      • The Spirit’s reason for taking Hebrews back to Genesis is that Jesus’ priesthood is superior to Aaron’s because Melchizedek’s order is superior to Levi’s.

    2. 22:31 Melchizedek appears as king of righteousness, peace, and an enduring priesthood

      Scripture: Hebrews 7:1–3

      • Hebrews 7:1–3 presents Melchizedek as king of Salem and priest of God Most High who meets and blesses Abraham and receives a tenth from him.
      • His name means king of righteousness, Salem contributes king of peace, and Scripture presents no father, mother, genealogy, beginning, or end, so he resembles the Son of God and remains a priest forever.
      • He holds kingship and priesthood together, offices later separated under Moses between Judah’s kings and Levi’s priests.

    3. 23:16 Genesis 14 places the mysterious king-priest above Abraham

      Scripture: Genesis 14:11–20

      • After Abraham rescues Lot and defeats the kings, Melchizedek suddenly appears with bread and wine; Abraham gives him a tenth of the plunder, and the king-priest disappears from the narrative.
      • Josh uses Abraham’s gift to answer the claim that tithing originated only with the Mosaic law: Abraham tithed before the law commanded it.

    4. 23:49 The greater blesses the lesser, so Hebrews tells readers to consider his greatness

      Scripture: Hebrews 7:4–7

      • Hebrews 7:4–10 asks readers to consider Melchizedek’s greatness: even patriarch Abraham gives him a tenth, and a man outside Levi both receives the tithe and blesses the promise bearer.
      • The lesser is blessed by the greater. For Jewish Christians raised to revere Abraham and understand the Levitical system, this reversal would have been shocking.
      • If Melchizedek is greater than Abraham and Jesus is priest in Melchizedek’s order, then Jesus belongs to a priesthood greater than anything Israel had known.

    5. 25:32 The missing genealogy forms a scriptural portrait rather than a claim of no parents

      Scripture: Hebrews 7:3

      • Genesis is filled with genealogies, yet none is supplied for Melchizedek. Josh explicitly denies that this means the man literally had no parents.
      • Instead, Scripture intentionally presents him as resembling the Son of God, a picture of the greater priest whose ministry never ends.

    6. 25:56 A changed priesthood rests on an indestructible life and introduces a better hope

      Scripture: Hebrews 7:11–22 Psalm 110:4

      • Josh reads Hebrews 7:11–22: if Levi’s priesthood and the law had brought perfection, another priest in Melchizedek’s order would not be needed. A changed priesthood therefore entails a changed law.
      • Jesus descends from Judah, a tribe to which Moses assigned no priestly service. His priesthood rests not on ancestry regulations but on the power of an indestructible life and God’s declaration in Psalm 110: ‘You are a priest forever.’
      • The former regulation could not perfect anyone; a better hope now draws people near to God. God’s oath makes Jesus guarantor of a better covenant.

    7. 27:17 The change in office requires a corresponding change in law

      • The old priesthood could point toward and display forgiveness but could not finally provide it. Therefore the law governing priesthood had to change when the priesthood changed.
      • Josh compares this to the United States Constitution: a president cannot become king under laws that provide only a limited presidential term. Likewise, Mosaic law made no provision for a priest from Judah, yet Jesus is high priest because God has declared it.

    8. 28:20 God’s oath establishes one priest who never dies

      Scripture: Psalm 110:4 Hebrews 7:23–24 Galatians 6:16 Hebrews 13:8

      • Josh quotes Psalm 110:4 again: the Lord has sworn, will not change his mind, and appoints a priest forever in Melchizedek’s order. The shadow passes because the substance has come.
      • Levitical priests died and required replacement, but Jesus lives forever and therefore holds a permanent priesthood without turnover, corruption, or a successor needing to be trained.
      • The people of God—whom Josh connects with Paul’s phrase ‘the Israel of God’—have the one high priest who is the same yesterday, today, and forever.

  5. 29:16 The indestructible King-Priest saves completely by always interceding

    • Hebrews 7:25–28 brings the argument to its pastoral center: Jesus’ once-for-all sacrifice and endless life secure complete salvation for those who come through him, and his ongoing advocacy means assurance depends on his indestructible commitment rather than theirs.

    1. 29:16 Hebrews 7:25–28 displays the indestructible priest at work

      Scripture: Hebrews 7:25–28 Psalm 110:4

      • Josh asks the church to watch the indestructible King-Priest work and reads Hebrews 7:25–28: Jesus saves completely those who come through him because he always lives to intercede.
      • This holy, blameless, pure, exalted priest does not repeatedly sacrifice for his own sins and then the people’s; he offered himself once for all. The oath appoints the Son, made perfect forever.

    2. 30:20 Completely means neither mostly nor only until the next failure

      Scripture: Hebrews 7:25

      • Hebrews does not say Jesus saves mostly, until the next failure, until life becomes difficult, or until a season of doubt. It says he saves completely.
      • Josh speaks to those carrying guilt, shame, and fear that they have failed once too often. Christ saves those who come through him—not those who perform perfectly or never struggle.
      • The door is open: people may bring weakness, doubt, failure, and fear and simply come to God through Christ.

    3. 31:45 Salvation rests on the risen Savior’s present intercession

      Scripture: Hebrews 7:25 1 Timothy 2:5

      • Salvation rests not only on something Jesus did in the past but on what he is doing now. Josh calls it an active and passive salvation: the cross behind believers and the risen Savior advocating today belong together.
      • Jesus always lives to intercede, so he never stops saving. He is the one mediator and therefore the only way to approach God.

    4. 32:23 Christ is not reconsidering whether his people are worth keeping

      Scripture: Romans 8:30

      • Jesus is not reviewing the week’s performance, reconsidering his commitment, or deciding whether a struggling believer remains worth the trouble. The risen Son willingly intercedes for his people.
      • The Savior who shed his blood still speaks for them; the one who called still keeps, and the one who justified will sanctify and glorify.
      • Salvation is therefore as secure as the indestructible priest before the Father. Confidence rests not in the strength of believers’ commitment but in the nature of King-Priest Jesus.

    5. 33:38 Christian testimony must include Christ’s present ministry

      • Josh appreciates decisions, prayers, altar calls, and the completed cross, but rejects speaking as though salvation were only a closed event in the past.
      • Hebrews shows something larger: at this moment Christ is actively ministering for his people in the Father’s presence.

    6. 34:16 The scars of Calvary testify while the Advocate defends

      Scripture: Romans 8:34 1 John 2:1

      • Josh pictures Jesus beside the Father, the enduring scars of Calvary testifying that sin has been paid for by his blood. The sacrifice is never repeated because its power never diminishes.
      • Christ does not represent his people distantly. He knows every weakness, temptation, fear, and failure and brings the redeemed before the Father with love, compassion, and perfect concern.
      • Romans 8 says Christ intercedes; 1 John calls him Advocate and defender. Josh applies both personally: Jesus is praying for his church and for the believer now.

    7. 35:51 The interceding Son invites sinners to draw near to his Father

      Scripture: Hebrews 7:25 Hebrews 4:16 1 John 1:9

      • Josh imagines the substance of Jesus’ prayer: draw near to the good Father who keeps promises; Christ has made a way, and the throne of grace remains available to humble confession.
      • He quotes 1 John 1:9: the faithful and just God forgives confessed sins and purifies from all unrighteousness.
      • This humble walk with the forever high priest is what keeps a Christian from being swept away.

  6. 36:46 Communion and a rope confess that Jesus is the church’s hope

    • Josh closes by inviting the church to remember Christ’s sacrifice at the table, tie a rope to the anchor as a public confession of hope, and pray with humility, confession, gratitude, and confidence in the high priest who stays with his people.

    1. 36:46 The doctrine moves into worship and a public response

      • Josh transitions from exposition to worship and introduces a physical response for the congregation.
      • Each person received a short rope or can take one from a basket in preparation for a physical confession at the anchor.

    2. 37:10 The rope symbolizes faith but carries no saving power

      • Tying the rope is neither magical nor a way to earn more love from God. It publicly joins brothers and sisters in saying, ‘Jesus is my hope, and I’m anchored to him.’
      • Weary, tired, frustrated, or drifting people are invited to stop trusting themselves and trust Jesus again.
      • At communion they remember his body broken and blood shed, give thanks for his prayers, then tie the rope as a shared confession that their lives are anchored to Jesus Christ, Savior and God.

    3. 38:14 The closing prayer draws near in confession and gratitude

      Scripture: 1 John 1:9 Matthew 28:20 Hebrews 7:25

      • Josh thanks the Father for sending the Son to die, for cleansing through his blood, and for the promise to forgive and purify those who confess.
      • He asks for help to resemble Jesus and thanks the great high priest who knows burdens, remains with his people to the end of the age, and is at work in the room.
      • As the church comes to the table, Josh prays that humility will displace pride and concern for others’ opinions, that people will draw near through Christ, tie onto the anchor, and hold on.
