Overview

  1. 00:22 Enter controversy committed to unity, then meet the practical letter of James

    Scripture: John 17:20–23 James 1:22

    • Josh opens by asking who enjoys controversy, debate, and friction. He welcomes “sparks” only when they sharpen believers for battle against evil—not when church members prepare to fight him or one another. Crossroads is to build a culture of unity on Jesus Christ. Josh recalls Jesus’ prayer that his followers would be one (John 17:20–23) and asks the congregation to recommit publicly to that work: “I will.” He does not want “mini me” disciples who merely look or sound like him. He wants people to know the revelation of Jesus, trusting that people centered on Jesus can work through lesser disagreements together.
    • He introduces James as perhaps the New Testament’s most controversial book. Martin Luther called it an “epistle of straw,” and some modern theologians treat it as a Jewish carryover that fails to express Christianity’s essence. Yet ordinary believers gravitate toward James and frequently quote it from memory.
    • Josh gives three reasons for James’s popularity. Efficiency and practicality: James gives concise guidance for living the Christian life, epitomized by “Do not merely listen … Do what it says” (James 1:22). James is not constructing a complete theological framework; he is spurring Christians to live their faith. Rapid movement: Like Jewish wisdom literature such as Proverbs, James often states a point, lets it stand, and moves on rather than exhausting it. Memorable metaphors: James uses pictures that lodge his teaching in the believer’s mind.
    • Transition: Today will differ from later messages because Josh must establish the framework for the entire series. He compares that preparation to packing for a trip: he hates the tedious planning and procrastinates with any available chore, but the journey goes better when he first considers its length, weather, clothing, and needs.

  2. 04:58 Pack for the study: four interpretive questions and the identity of James

    Scripture: James 1:1a Matthew 4:21–22 Acts 12:1–2 Mark 2:14; 3:18 Luke 6:16 Matthew 13:55; Galatians 1:19 Acts 15 Galatians 2 Matthew 1:18–25

    • Josh urges listeners to prepare for any biblical book by answering four questions in order: Who is the author—not merely his name, but who he is? Who are the recipients as actual people? Why is the author writing to them? Only after that groundwork, what can readers far removed from the original audience learn?
    • He begins with James 1:1a: “James, a servant of God and of the Lord Jesus Christ.” Because James was a common name, Josh evaluates the New Testament candidates. James son of Zebedee, John’s brother, was called from fishing by Jesus (Matthew 4:21–22) but was killed by Herod around AD 44 (Acts 12:1–2), before Josh’s proposed AD 49 date for the letter. James son of Alphaeus is too obscure to connect confidently with the letter. Josh notes that Levi/Matthew is also called a son of Alphaeus (Mark 2:14; 3:18) and playfully raises, without asserting, the possibility that they were brothers. James associated with Judas is even less likely because Scripture tells so little about him (Luke 6:16).
    • Josh accepts the traditional identification: the author is James, Jesus’ half-brother, Jude’s brother, and the leader of the Jerusalem church (Matthew 13:55; Galatians 1:19). He recommends comparing the Jerusalem Council in Acts 15 with James and Galatians 2, because their language and concerns show striking similarities. “Half-brother” matters because Joseph was James’s father, while Jesus was conceived by the Holy Spirit; James and Jesus shared Mary but not a human father (Matthew 1:18–25).

  3. 08:00 James’s testimony: unbelieving brother, resurrection witness, church pillar, and mature shepherd

    Scripture: John 7:5 Acts 1:14 1 Corinthians 15:6–7 Galatians 2:9 Acts 1:8 Luke 23:34 Acts 15:13–21 Matthew 5–7

    • Josh says James’s testimony is the greatest reason redeemed people love his letter. He imagines the difficulty of growing up with a brother who never lied, sinned, retaliated, or joined sibling schemes. The humor gives way to John 7:5: even Jesus’ brothers did not believe in him.
    • Application to Christian households: Being raised near faith does not regenerate anyone. “People don’t raise Christians. God calls Christians.” Josh asks his children what they truly believe and whether they want self-rule or to follow Jesus. If James could live with Jesus and reject him for decades, children can live with Christians and still deny the faith. Parents should create a safe place for children to wrestle honestly, cover them in prayer, plead for God to save them, and trust God’s timing. They cannot manufacture an “upper room experience.” Josh connects this conviction to Crossroads’ practice of believer’s baptism: a confession of faith is required, which makes him oppose infant baptism and cautious about baptizing children prematurely.
    • The resurrection changes James. In the upper room, Mary and Jesus’ brothers join the disciples in constant prayer (Acts 1:14). The family that once rejected Jesus is present at the birth of the Jerusalem church. Paul reports that the risen Jesus appeared to more than five hundred believers and then specifically to James (1 Corinthians 15:6–7). Josh answers his own question—what changed James?—with the resurrection. “He is risen” remains Christianity’s greatest historical claim. The risen Christ turned James around in AD 33, turned Josh around in 1995, and still transforms lives. Josh asks listeners when that encounter changed them.
    • Salvation leads James into local-church service. Paul calls James, Cephas, and John “pillars” and recounts their fellowship and mission agreement with Paul and Barnabas (Galatians 2:9). Josh pictures an apostolic staff meeting in which leaders discern who can best reach Jews and Gentiles. Their shared Great Commission and movement toward “the ends of the earth” model gospel partnership (Acts 1:8). Against social media’s appetite for failure and armchair criticism, Josh wants Crossroads to display leaders and members who cooperate, love, sacrifice, and preserve unity. He illustrates this with a recent firm disagreement with a church member. They recognized Satan’s attempt to divide them, prayed for each other, and restored unity. The church needs to tell more stories of believers overcoming evil rather than highlighting only abuse, adultery, theft, division, and defeat.
    • James finishes faithfully. Tradition says the Pharisees threw him from the temple and clubbed him to death in AD 62; some accounts say he prayed for his killers, following Jesus’ “Father, forgive them” (Luke 23:34). Scripture presents him as a strong leader who brings peace and direction in the Jerusalem Council (Acts 15:13–21), a defender of the gospel, a man of prayer, and a leader Paul could esteem as a pillar. Tradition describes his knees as hardened like a camel’s from prayer. His Jewish formation under Moses’ law remains visible in his letter.
    • Hope for unbelieving loved ones: Although James did not believe during Jesus’ teaching ministry, he had listened closely; Josh notes at least fifteen echoes of the Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 5–7) in James. Someone who presently disbelieves may still be watching and listening.
    • Historical transition: James led while Jewish believers were learning grace amid an operating temple system. Modern readers should not look down on them after inheriting Romans, Galatians, and Hebrews; these pioneers had to work out the transition from law to grace before most of the New Testament existed.

  4. 19:02 The scattered recipients and the single disease beneath their many church problems

    Scripture: James 1:1b Acts 11:19–21 James 2:1–7 James 3:1–12 James 4:1–3 James 4:4 James 4:6–17 James 5:14–16 James 5:19–20

    • Josh answers the second question from James 1:1b: James writes “to the twelve tribes scattered among the nations.” The letter applies to all Christians, but its immediate recipients were Jewish Christians, written before Gentiles entered the church in large numbers: Scripture is not necessarily written to modern readers, but it is written for them.
    • Acts 11:19–21 supplies the historical picture. Persecution after Stephen’s death scattered believers to Phoenicia, Cyprus, and Antioch. Some preached only to Jews; others preached Jesus to Greeks, and many believed. Josh explains “scattered” as diaspora language that also evokes seed broadcast into soil. Persecution appeared to disperse and weaken the church but actually planted gospel seed across the Roman world. The displaced believers became the original church planters, and Jewish and Greek believers became one people of God by faith.
    • Those Christian Jews faced rejection from both sides: Gentiles rejected them as Jews, while unbelieving Jews rejected them as Christians. Many were poor, oppressed, and exploited by the rich.
    • Josh answers why James wrote: pressure exposed and amplified immaturity. Some kept Christianity private to preserve work, reputation, and security. That secrecy produced partiality: some courted the wealthy while poorer believers in the same church were mistreated (James 2:1–7). A leadership vacuum made many want to teach (James 3:1). Instead of speaking directly and building one another up, members used the tongue to campaign, demean rivals, and elevate themselves (James 3:2–12). Their competing desires erupted into fights in church (James 4:1–3). Genuine believers still loved the world and forgot Jesus under pressure (James 4:4). Pride, arrogant plans, disobedience, sickness, and wandering also marked the community (James 4:6–17; 5:14–20).
    • Josh turns the mirror toward the modern American church: suffering, dishonesty, professed faith without obedience, worship of money, power, and sex, pornography, and destructive speech remain recognizable.
    • Beneath the entire list is one issue: immaturity. James wants Christians to grow up because Christians who refuse to mature eventually tear others down.

  5. 24:33 Maturity grows under resistance; prepare to receive and obey James

    Scripture: Romans 5:3–4 Hebrews 5:12–14 John 3:3–8 Genesis 1:2–3 Ezekiel 36:26–27 Ephesians 2:8–9 Romans 10:17 James 1:22

    • Josh illustrates perseverance while describing a workout with his son Nolan. Nolan’s bench press has doubled, and after a difficult set Josh asks him to define perseverance: enduring through hardship. Because Romans 5:3–4 is a family memory passage, Nolan knows that perseverance produces character. Muscle grows by being torn, nourished, and rebuilt stronger; character develops the same way. Physical discipline is good, but Nolan must also “lift the Word.” Life will tear him, and spiritual milk cannot sustain maturity; he needs solid food (Hebrews 5:12–14).
    • The first- and twenty-first-century church share the same immaturity problem. With the “packing” complete for a 10-week journey, Josh gives three ways to get the most from the series. Be born again. Spiritual maturity cannot exist without spiritual birth (John 3:3–8). The Spirit who hovered over the waters and brought light from God’s spoken Word (Genesis 1:2–3) uses the Word to create new life in a sinner who trusts Jesus. Josh calls this recreation or regeneration: whatever the delivery method—private reading, sermon, song, play, or film—the message remains the need for a new heart (Ezekiel 36:26–27). Salvation is God’s gift by grace through faith, not human works (Ephesians 2:8–9). Faith comes by hearing the message about Christ (Romans 10:17). Examine and obey. Hear each sermon for yourself, not for an absent cousin, spouse, in-law, child, or preacher. Pray for others, but examine your own life and obey what the Word teaches. Merely listening is easy; James says to do it (James 1:22, reapplied). Expect resistance. If believers begin walking more intentionally with Christ, the devil will try to trip them. Josh’s memorable counsel is to “kick the stick and keep stepping”: growing seriousness invites more serious schemes, but the church need not surrender.
    • Transition: After the lengthy series framework, Josh returns to James’s one-word greeting and begins the primary lesson on trials.

  6. 30:18 Count trials as joy by looking beyond present pain

    Scripture: James 1:2–3 Revelation 20:6, 14 John 16:33 James 1:2 Hebrews 12:2 Hebrews 12:3

    • Josh reads James 1:2–3: believers should consider trials of many kinds pure joy because tested faith produces perseverance. A playful lemon/lemonade riff gives way to a sober correction of shallow teaching about suffering.
    • Foundational claim: Anything that can happen to another human can happen to a Christian except the second death, eternal separation from God (Revelation 20:6, 14). Jesus promises both trouble and victory (John 16:33); trials are not hypothetical.
    • Josh argues that joy is unnatural because contemporary culture expects circumstances to go well and treats happiness as the measure of winning. He contrasts that expectation with earlier generations that assumed life could be unfair and difficult. In a pointed cultural application, he condemns prescribing puberty blockers or powerful medication to distressed adolescents rather than helping them endure, and he links that posture to high 2023 suicide statistics. His stated concern is a social narrative that losing what makes a person happy means life is irretrievably lost. He says the church has contributed by implying that God’s children are protected from major suffering. He rejects the distinction between “little” and “big” bad things: the issue is when, not if, trials come.
    • Joy is chosen by the Spirit, not performed for an audience. Believers are not merely to wear a cheerful face; because God commands joy, the Spirit enables them to set their minds on it (James 1:2, reapplied). Christians may need to pull one another back on course and ask how Jesus would view the situation.
    • Jesus supplies the pattern. For the joy ahead, Jesus endured the cross and its shame and sat at God’s right hand (Hebrews 12:2). He looked beyond the immediate trial to the completed work on the other side. Believers are likewise to consider the one who endured sinners’ opposition so they do not grow weary or lose heart (Hebrews 12:3). Jesus directed his mind toward joy rather than allowing suffering to define the horizon.
    • Josh then makes the teaching personal. His stepfather Dave, his mother’s husband, died the previous afternoon. Josh is hurting and will miss him, while believing Dave is with the Lord and better off. Joy does not mean pretending everything is fine. Joy can coexist with tears, misery, and loss; standing confidently before God while circumstances are not fine is where others see joy and God’s glory.

  7. 37:35 Let perseverance finish: suffering’s fire produces four marks of maturity

    Scripture: James 1:4 Isaiah 53:3 Matthew 8:20 John 11:33–35 Hebrews 12:2 Philippians 3:10–11 Romans 12:2 2 Corinthians 12:7–9 2 Corinthians 1:3–4 Job 13:15 James 1:2

    • Josh reads James 1:4: perseverance must finish its work so believers become mature, complete, and lacking nothing. Society trains people to inventory what suffering took away, but Scripture says God can enrich believers through loss. If perseverance makes a person complete, then without perseverance that person remains incomplete. Josh names four “features that only come through fire.”
    • 1. Christlikeness: Jesus was a “man of suffering” (Isaiah 53:3) without even a settled place to lay his head (Matthew 8:20). Sharing suffering can deepen fellowship with him. As Josh grieves Dave, he follows Jesus, who grieved Lazarus and wept (John 11:33–35). Tears do not erase the joy still set ahead (Hebrews 12:2, reapplied). Paul wanted to know both the power of Christ’s resurrection and participation in his sufferings, becoming like him in death and reaching resurrection (Philippians 3:10–11). Josh says Christians rarely speak this way because their minds are being conformed to the world rather than renewed by the Word (Romans 12:2).
    • 2. Humility: Trials break pride. Paul’s brilliance, leadership, revelation, and capability could have fed self-exaltation, but the thorn in his flesh kept him dependent on Christ’s sufficient grace (2 Corinthians 12:7–9).
    • 3. Compassion: Deep suffering equips believers to empathize with others. God, the Father of compassion and God of all comfort, comforts his people so they can pass that comfort to others in trouble (2 Corinthians 1:3–4). Josh warns against wasting suffering in self-pity when it can become a pathway to help someone else.
    • 4. Faith: When other supports are stripped away, Christians learn to lean on God alone: “Though he slay me, yet will I hope in him” (Job 13:15).
    • Christlikeness, humility, compassion, and faith stand on the far side of the trial. That produced maturity is the promised joy; there is no alternate path to being complete. Josh repeats the command: “Consider it pure joy” (James 1:2, reapplied).

  8. 41:27 Ask the generous God for wisdom before exhausting every other source

    Scripture: James 1:5–8 James 4:2 Matthew 7:7

    • Josh reads James 1:5–8: anyone lacking wisdom should ask God, who gives generously without fault-finding, but the asker must believe rather than doubt; the doubter is wave-tossed, double-minded, and unstable.
    • James observed that immature early believers struggled in prayer, just as Christians do now. Later James will say that some simply fail to ask (James 4:2). People claim to trust God but do not talk or walk with him.
    • Josh rejects prayer as a religious assignment. For the redeemed, it is unlimited access to the limitless God; refusing that access means refusing to walk in God’s offered power.
    • Why ask for wisdom rather than only strength, grace, or deliverance? Strength may carry someone through, grace may take someone over, and deliverance may pull someone out. Wisdom keeps the trial from being wasted by teaching the believer how to receive and use the moment.
    • Christians often consult a bestseller, preacher, podcast, spouse, parent, and friends before sitting quietly before God. If God gives generously, sparse prayer makes no sense. Josh does not forbid human counsel; his ordering point is that God must not be the backup plan or supporting role. The Creator is the lead. He invokes “Ask and you will receive” (Matthew 7:7) and challenges listeners to ask God for wisdom believing that he will answer.

  9. 44:11 Ask in faith: do not live like a wave thrown between hope and despair

    Scripture: James 1:6–8 Revelation 21:4 Romans 8:18

    • Josh returns to James 1:6–8: doubt can kill the request before it is made, and James’s first major metaphor pictures the doubter as a wind-tossed wave.
    • He illustrates the metaphor with a 2021 family trip to Big Beach, Hawaii. Josh and Madison eagerly swam through the large incoming waves because getting out seemed easy. Returning to shore was different: a rip current and repeated eight-foot waves battered them. Each time hope rose as they neared safety, another wave crashed, pulled them back, and forced them to begin again. Many Christians live the same way—up in one moment, crushed in the next, tossed between hope and despair.
    • Josh carefully says this instability is evidence of immaturity, not proof of lost salvation. The person has faith, or there would be no prayer at all; the same person also entertains unbelief, or there would be no doubt. Bouncing between the two makes the person double-minded and unstable.
    • Life may not match the desired plan or checklist, but Christ suffered for believers, and believers may now suffer for him. Pain, sickness, suffering, poverty, and death will be swallowed up in the coming reality with God (Revelation 21:4). Present sufferings cannot compare with the glory to be revealed (Romans 8:18).
    • Josh’s repeated pastoral command is: Hold on. Do not doubt God. Ask for wisdom. Persevere as Jesus did.

  10. 47:57 Stop waiting: kneel, ask for wisdom, commune with God, and trust him through grief

    Scripture: James 1:5–6

    • Josh closes the teaching with a direct question built from James 1:5–6, reapplying the passage: Do listeners believe God can give wisdom and will give it when they ask in faith? If so, why wait?
    • He invites physically able listeners to kneel immediately. Pads are available for those who do not want “camel knees,” recalling James’s traditional life of prayer.
    • Josh supplies the substance of a candid prayer: Admit confusion over recurring hurt, loss, and suffering. Ask for understanding, but submit if explanation is not given. Ask for the wisdom needed to see a path through the tragedy toward God’s glory. Ask to become light to someone else in the middle of the tragedy. Confess trust in God regardless of the outcome.
    • He diagnoses frantic, prayerless living as a cause of much that is wrong in churches. Communion with God requires stopping, ceasing to fill every silence with words, sitting with him, and listening.
    • Josh directs the room into an integrated response of fellowship, prayer, the Lord’s Supper, singing, music, and access to elders at the tables. People need not fake emotion: the hurt may hurt and the sad may be sad, but they must talk to God.
    • In the closing prayer, Josh models his own appeal. He tells the Father that he is struggling, hurting, sad, and weary. He leans into God, asks comfort, presence, and peace for everyone mourning, and thanks Jesus for suffering so sinners can have peace with God. He offers to suffer faithfully to the end if that is his calling, thanks the Lord for loving him, and closes with “Amen.”
