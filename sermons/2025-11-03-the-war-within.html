  1. 00:08 The visible battles are symptoms of a spiritual war within

    Scripture: 2 Corinthians 12:7–9

    • Josh opens personally: the war inside him can drive him to war with other people and can put him opposite God as an enemy rather than beside God as a friend.
    • He extends the war image through several conditions: Sometimes the “bullets” are overhead and survival feels uncertain. Sometimes the immediate danger has eased, but the person is still stranded in hostile territory and cannot go home. Sometimes a believer feels less like a soldier than a prisoner of spiritual war, chained by Satan and unable to escape.
    • He names concrete forms of captivity in the congregation: Drug or alcohol addiction, where chemical craving dictates thought, feeling, speech, and behavior. Abuse or trauma, whose scars seem to return a person to the same place despite attempts to break free. Anxiety and depression, alternating between fear of tomorrow and suffocation beneath yesterday’s mistakes.
    • Josh locates himself inside the problem rather than above it: Jesus helped him escape nearly two decades of pornography addiction and a nicotine addiction. After those chains loosened, he says Satan changed the angle of attack toward doubt, worry, and episodes of depression that he can fight only with the Holy Spirit’s help. He describes the mental battle as sudden heaviness—sometimes a flash flood, sometimes water that remains.
    • He criticizes shallow church teaching that turns Paul’s unresolved “thorn in the flesh” into a blanket assumption that every mental, emotional, or physical stronghold is God’s will. Paul did not receive removal of his thorn, but Josh insists that this does not justify embracing every form of bondage as one’s assigned story. The devastation is knowing addiction and suffocating depression are not God’s desire while seeing no path out and simply sitting in chains.
    • Transition to the text: Whatever form the fight takes—physical, emotional, relational, or mental—the believer must recognize its spiritual dimension. Victory requires knowing both the enemy and the environment; his illustration is a soldier arriving in the Arctic wearing green “frog-skin” camouflage.

  2. 05:33 James exposes selfish desire beneath conflict with others

    Scripture: James 4:1–2 Psalm 133:1 1 Corinthians 6:1–8 Galatians 5:15 Ephesians 4:1 Philippians 4:2 Philippians 2:5–7

    • Josh announces the primary structure of the message from James 4: the church is at war with others, self, and God, and James explains how those wars stop.
    • Reading James 4:1–2, he stresses that quarrels among Christians arise from pleasures or desires battling within them. He explains the underlying Greek term hēdonē as sensual, natural, fleshly gratification and connects it to hedonism. His contemporary paraphrase of hedonism is “you do you”; beneath it lies selfishness.
    • He contrasts selfish gratification with Psalm 133’s picture of God’s people living in unity: the church’s witness shines when God’s house “sings in harmony.”
    • The New Testament churches demonstrate that this conflict is not theoretical: Corinth pursued pleasure and even took fellow believers to court. Galatia bit and devoured one another. Ephesus needed to be called back to a life worthy of its calling. Euodia and Syntyche in Philippi had to agree in the Lord, with Christ’s self-emptying servant posture presented as the corrective.
    • The Spirit of the selfless Christ cannot be blamed for selfishness or envy; those impulses come from somewhere else.
    • James is pulling back the curtain: Church “death fights” are not finally caused by someone taking a preferred seat or serving stale communion. The decisive problem is not what is happening around or even to a person, but what has gone wrong inside that person.
    • Josh quotes Baruch Spinoza’s criticism that professing Christians can display rancorous hatred more readily than love, joy, peace, temperance, and charity. His diagnostic question: why is Christian hatred sometimes more obvious than Christian love? Christians possess a new nature yet can revert to the old nature when fighting among themselves, becoming indistinguishable from the world once they are inside the church walls.

  3. 09:34 Gratitude sees God’s goodness; entitlement counts his supposed misses

    Scripture: 1 Timothy 6:7 James 4:2 James 4:11–12 Ephesians 4:15 Genesis 3:1–6 Genesis 12:10–20; 20:1–18 Joshua 7:1–26 2 Samuel 11:1–27 Isaiah 53:6

    • Josh presents two trajectories for believers: Grateful believers become skilled at noticing God’s goodness in family, home, health, children, career, church, leaders, friends, and spouse. Like putting on special glasses, they suddenly see God breaking chains and saving souls everywhere. Repeated recognition produces gratitude, then durable joy. Entitled believers become skilled at noticing what they regard as God’s misses—the blessing, opportunity, relationship, or promotion he “should” have given them.
    • The grateful believer knows he entered the world with nothing and will leave the same way, and therefore does not treat health, wealth, an effortless marriage, friends, or career as a debt God owes.
    • Entitlement reverses that posture: Instead of counting blessings, the person counts their absence. Another person’s promotion becomes an accusation against God rather than an occasion for joy.
    • Josh returns to James’s severe wording, “You desire and do not have, so you kill,” and repeats the movement’s thesis: coveting leads to conflict. People think they deserve an admission, family history, relationship, or opportunity; when they do not receive it, they throw a fit and expose slavery to sin. They do not truly want peace with God; they want God’s possessions. War with self, God, and others follows, producing joylessness, irritability, conflict, and blame.
    • He treats “kill” as deliberate rhetorical escalation rather than evidence that murder was the congregation’s normal practice. James is destroying the listener’s pity party by naming how believers fight for position, speak evil, judge, classify people, and weaponize the tongue instead of speaking truth in love. The war with others persists because the person refuses to face the war within.
    • Worldly pleasure cannot end the cycle because coveting is continuous and envy never declares itself satisfied. Josh urges listeners to accept now that selfish desire will not satisfy, sparing themselves and others the pain caused by trying to make it do so. Peace with others is impossible while one is constantly fighting for oneself.
    • He closes the movement with biblical examples of selfish desire: Eve ate in order to gain wisdom like God. Abraham lied to preserve himself. Achan kept Jericho’s devoted goods and brought defeat on Israel. David killed to conceal adultery. Isaiah’s diagnosis gathers them all: like sheep, each has turned to his or her own way.
    • Transition: people fight for themselves because they do not trust God.

  4. 14:58 Disordered desire produces a distorted or absent prayer life

    Scripture: James 4:2–3 Psalm 2:4–8 John 13:16 1 Timothy 2:1–2 James 1:27 Philippians 2:3 Ephesians 4:27 Matthew 6:10

    • Reading James 4:2–3, Josh identifies two failures: Some do not have because they do not ask God: a nonexistent prayer life. Others ask but do not receive because they ask from wrong motives, intending to spend the answer on their pleasures: an ineffective prayer life.
    • His maxim is, “If we don’t pray right, we can’t live right.” Selfishness is serious enough in daily life, but worse when carried unchallenged into private prayer with God.
    • Psalm 2 supplies the positive model: God tells his installed King and Son to ask for the nations as his inheritance. Josh reasons that if Jesus asks to receive, servants should not imagine themselves above asking their Master.
    • He turns conviction into a catalogue of intercession: Pray for world peace rather than mocking the cliché. Pray for every leader, not only those on one’s political side, asking for integrity, justice, and compassion. Pray for the lonely, orphans, widows, children without a home for the holidays, and young people. Pray for return to God and revival in the church instead of spending that energy complaining about another generation.
    • He contrasts another promotion request with the harder prayer for a compassionate heart that genuinely values others above self. People avoid that prayer because compassion may demand money, time, energy, and personal effort. They do not receive such transformation because they do not truly want its cost.
    • Josh supplies further corrective prayers: humility; exposure of places given to the devil; courage to do right at personal cost; opportunities to share testimony and gospel; God’s view of other people; awareness of blessing; contentment; and the capacity to bless others.
    • Transition: instead of praying for God’s will on earth as in heaven, people pray for their own will, then become angry with God when it is denied and carry that anger into relationships.

  5. 18:47 Coveting matures into spiritual adultery and friendship with the world

    Scripture: Exodus 20:17 (parallel Deuteronomy 5:21) Exodus 20:12–16 2 Samuel 11:1–27 James 4:4 Matthew 5:21–30 Jeremiah 3:8–9 Ezekiel 16:32 Matthew 12:39–41 Matthew 6:5 John 5:39–40 Matthew 6:24 1 John 2:15–17

    • Josh identifies “You shall not covet” as the final commandment and argues that violating it can drive violations of the others—murder, lying, theft, dishonoring parents, and adultery. David’s story is his example.
    • James 4:4 intensifies the diagnosis: friendship with the world is hostility toward God, and choosing the world makes a person God’s enemy.
    • Josh connects James to Jesus’ Sermon on the Mount: The issue is the heart, not only the externally completed act. In that heart-level sense, all are implicated as murderers and adulterers.
    • “Adulterous people” addresses spiritual rather than sexual infidelity: Jeremiah depicts faithless Israel receiving a certificate of divorce while Judah repeats her adultery. Ezekiel calls Judah an adulterous wife who prefers strangers to her husband. The nations’ gods became illicit partners for God’s covenant people.
    • Jesus’ rebuke of a wicked and adulterous generation continues the theme: That generation demanded a sign but would receive Jonah’s sign. Nineveh would condemn it because Nineveh repented at Jonah’s preaching while Israel rejected the One greater than Jonah. Religious display—including prayer on street corners—could coexist with hearts that preferred strange gods to the true God.
    • John 5 sharpens the warning: people can study Scripture diligently while refusing to come to the Christ to whom Scripture testifies.
    • Josh applies James’s rebuke to nominal Christians: They join a church without a personal saving relationship with God through Christ. They say they love God while “laying with the world” and keeping other partners. No one can serve two masters.
    • From 1 John, he warns against loving the world’s sinful cravings, lust, and boasting because the world and its desires pass away; the one who does God’s will lives forever.
    • Transition: the church is Christ’s bride and must remain faithful to the end.

  6. 23:15 The Spirit opposes the flesh, and abounding grace overturns sin’s rule

    Scripture: James 4:5–6, including the quotation of Proverbs 3:34 in verse 6 Galatians 5:17 Romans 8:6 Romans 5:20–21 Exodus 32:1–24 Exodus 20:3 Exodus 20:16 John 8:10–11

    • Reading James 4:5–6, Josh says human beings display an envious spirit from birth, but God jealously desires the spirit he placed within his people and “gives more grace.” God opposes pride and gives favor to humility.
    • He sets the conflict alongside Galatians 5:17: sinful nature and Spirit desire contrary things, preventing believers from simply doing whatever they want. This is the war within every follower of Christ. Like covenant jealousy between spouses, the Spirit guards the believer’s relationship with God and grieves over sin against God’s love.
    • Romans 8:6 supplies the battle plan for the mind: flesh-governed thought leads to death, while Spirit-governed thought brings life and peace.
    • Josh names the opposing strategies: God calls believers to depend on grace. The devil urges them to depend on themselves.
    • Romans 5:20–21 explains grace’s superiority: The law makes trespass visible. Josh illustrates this through Moses descending from Sinai into the golden-calf rebellion. The commands against other gods and false witness expose Aaron and every hearer as lawbreakers. Yet where sin increases, grace increases all the more and reigns through righteousness to eternal life in Jesus Christ.
    • He carefully denies that grace licenses sin; grace is power to overcome it. No quantity of sin exhausts grace. Grace does not barely cover the ledger; it overflows and wins decisively.
    • The gospel climax is the woman caught in adultery: God sees the act but, in Christ, does not join the stone throwers; condemnation gives way to the command to leave sin.
    • Josh summarizes grace as God turning “rebels into relatives,” then repeats the contrast between dependence on grace and dependence on self.

  7. 27:43 Surrender is the Christian’s only path to victory

    Scripture: James 4:7–10 2 Samuel 11:1–12:13 Psalm 51:1–2 1 John 1:9

    • Josh gathers every battle, anxiety, stronghold, entitlement, and sinful desire under James 4:7–10: Submit to God. Resist the devil, who will flee. Draw near to God, who will draw near. Wash hands and purify divided hearts. Grieve sin and humble oneself before the Lord, who lifts up the humble.
    • He tells listeners to leave the throne of their own lives immediately. The demand for control, success, and relevance must be dropped at God’s feet because those demands are producing misery.
    • “Submit” is explained as a military term: take one’s proper rank. A sergeant acting like a four-star general would destroy military order. The believer must “fall in,” because in this war surrender to God is the only route to victory.
    • He addresses fear of making Jesus Lord: New and mature believers alike fear what Jesus might take away. Josh asks whether the feared loss is actually the very thing causing war in the mind. People cling to vices because they feel human or alive, but Jesus removes them to give something better and reveal that they were made for more.
    • David illustrates the transition from concealment to peace: After adultery with Bathsheba and Uriah’s death, secrecy made David increasingly miserable and put him at war with God, himself, and others. Surrender leads to Psalm 51’s cry for mercy, cleansing, and forgiveness.
    • Application: resisting the devil begins with submitting to God and drawing near through an honest plea for mercy. Confession receives faithful forgiveness and purification.

  8. 30:40 Wash, grieve, confess, and take responsibility for sin repeatedly

    Scripture: Jeremiah 17:9 Matthew 23:12 (parallels Luke 14:11; 18:14) Luke 15:11–24 Isaiah 32:17

    • Josh develops James’s “wash your hands” image: Believers must watch what they touch, what they hold tightly, their recurring tendencies, nearby temptations, and the damage their choices can cause. They must recognize the heart’s deceitfulness, the mind’s darkness, and the seriousness of sin.
    • His household illustration exposes misplaced urgency: Parents repeatedly tell small children to wash germ-covered hands. James calls adults to handle sin with still greater urgency; parents asking children to wash must ask whether their own hands are clean.
    • Grieving, mourning, and wailing mean taking responsibility rather than outsourcing blame. Josh confesses that it took him a long time to stop pointing at other people. He acknowledges painful childhoods and histories without allowing past harm to exonerate present sin.
    • Refusal to expose and mourn sin is pride, and Josh asks whether a current struggle might continue because God opposes the proud. A believer unwilling to own sin should expect divine opposition. God’s children cannot sin comfortably.
    • Pride is compared to facial hair and confession to shaving or washing: The work is recurring, not completed once. Believers repeatedly bring sin into God’s light, and God repeatedly lifts them because grace remains sufficient.
    • Jesus’ teaching that self-exaltation ends in humiliation and humility in exaltation sets up the prodigal son: The son returns willing to be a servant, owns and confesses his sin, and falls before his father. Josh’s personal maxim is that he would rather be a servant in God’s house than a king in his own. The father answers repentance with robe, ring, sandals, a feast, and restored sonship: the dead is alive and the lost is found.
    • Transition: when people submit, draw near, and humble themselves, God draws near, cleanses, forgives, and ends the wars with God, self, and others. Isaiah’s “quietness and confidence forever” names the resulting peace.

  9. 36:26 Grace replaces slander with humble, strengthening relationships

    Scripture: James 4:11–12 Matthew 7:3–5

    • James 4:11–12 shows what inward peace does to community: Believers stop slandering and judging one another. Speaking against a brother or sister sets the speaker over the law rather than under it. God alone is Lawgiver and Judge, able to save and destroy.
    • Those who have received mercy, mourned sin, humbled themselves, returned home, and learned that God gives more grace become experts in other people’s strengths rather than weaknesses. They celebrate the Holy Spirit’s work in others. They build up rather than tear down.
    • Josh asks listeners what they instinctively notice: Another person’s fall or faithful stand? Failure or even the smallest growth? Do they voice encouragement when they see strength?
    • The answers reveal not merely whether someone can define grace, but whether they recognize their own need for it.
    • Becoming attentive to grace in others does not require naivety or refusal to confront wrongdoing. Jesus’ speck-and-plank teaching requires self-examination first. The problem is not wise recognition of poor behavior but the harsh, unkind, continually fault-finding spirit.
    • Proper humility before God prevents arrogance toward others. Love does right; slander does wrong.

  10. 39:02 Personal response: name the war, confess, and live as someone Christ freed

    Scripture: 2 Corinthians 10:5

    • Josh hands the message to the congregation for reflection: Where is confession needed? What sin must be owned? Is there a slandered believer who needs an apology? Where has the listener sinned against self by indulging evil thought and desire rather than taking thoughts captive? Where is self-dependence replacing trust in God?
    • He returns to the adultery and throne images: In what areas has the listener been spiritually unfaithful? Who occupies the throne, and how far down the succession plan is Jesus? Does the listener intend to come home, or continue among the pigs? Is there a real desire to become God’s servant?
    • These answers are to disclose the heart’s condition.
    • As the congregation considers Jesus’ cross, Josh emphasizes that the sacrifice purchased freedom. The church should therefore behave as free people: break strongholds, slip chains, leave prisons, and reclaim hearts, minds, homes, church, and city. He is not searching for freedom; he says he has found it in Christ and intends to rest in its quiet confidence.
    • Conclusion of the sermon's central argument: resting in Christ’s freedom is the only way to win the war within.

  11. 41:14 Communion declares the battle won; prayer lays every rival desire at Jesus’ feet

    Scripture: 1 Corinthians 11:23–25 Hebrews 10:10–14 John 19:30 Matthew 6:10

    • Josh recounts Jesus’ words over the bread and cup on the night of his arrest: The bread represents Christ’s body given for his people and is taken in remembrance. The cup announces the new covenant in his blood.
    • He explains the once-for-all force of the cross: Repeated sacrifices for sin are no longer needed because Christ is the final sacrifice. Believers need not live under anxiety, burden, or an unfulfilled debt; Christ fulfilled the saving work in his death, vindicated by resurrection. “The debt is paid in full” and “It is done” become reminders that the battle for souls has been won.
    • He invites everyone who has put faith in Jesus to share the Lord’s Supper and simply urges, “Trust Jesus.”
    • In the closing prayer, Josh asks for courage to pray courageous prayers. He admits that during many years of following Jesus he retained things God never wanted for him. Recognizing the spiritual battle around mind, heart, soul, family, home, town, and church revealed that these things needed to be dropped at God’s feet.
    • The final act of surrender names envy, covetousness, desires, and pleasures, then releases them all: “Take this world. And just give me Jesus.”
    • He closes with allegiance and the Lord’s Prayer’s posture: God’s will in his life and in the church, as in heaven; “We’re yours”; Amen.
